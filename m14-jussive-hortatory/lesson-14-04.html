Lesson 14.04
Main Clause or Subordinate?
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Ten constructions now use the subjunctive. One question sorts them into two piles, and it is free.
Purpose, indirect command, result, cum, indirect question, fear — and now jussive, hortatory, optative and deliberative. That is a lot of things one mood can be doing. But the first cut is easy and mechanical: is this subjunctive the main verb of its sentence, or is it inside a clause? Answer that and you have halved the problem before thinking about meaning at all. Then you get the table this whole week has been pointing at — nē and its four different jobs.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to run the four-step sort on any subjunctive I meet, and say whether it is a main verb or inside a clause.
2
I will be able to name all four jobs of nē and the one question that tells them apart.
3
I will be able to sort an unlabelled pile of sentences drawn from Modules 8 to 14 without being told which is which.
📚 PART 1 · THE FOUR-STEP SORT

Steps 1 and 2 are pure counting and reading — you can run them on a sentence you do not understand at all. Only steps 3 and 4 ask about meaning, and by then the field is down to a handful of options.

1
Find every verb in the sentence and mark each one indicative or subjunctive.
This is reading the endings, nothing more. Timeō indicative · condemnētur subjunctive · ōrāvit indicative · faciat subjunctive.
2
Is there an indicative verb that could be the main verb of the sentence?
No — the subjunctive is the only verb there is → it is a MAIN-CLAUSE subjunctive. Go to step 3.
Yes → the subjunctive is almost certainly SUBORDINATE, hanging off that indicative verb. Go to step 4.
3
MAIN CLAUSE. Which of the four? Ask in this order:
Is there a utinam? → optative wish, and the tense says which kind (present = still open · imperfect = not so now · pluperfect = did not happen).
Is it a question — a question word and a question mark? → deliberative: “am I to…?” / “was I to…?”
Is the verb 1st person plural? → hortatory: “let us…”
Is the verb 3rd person? → jussive: “let him / let them…”
4
SUBORDINATE. Read the word that opens the clause, and look at the main verb.
ut + signpost (tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō) → result · ut after a verb of ordering → indirect command · ut otherwise → purpose · nē after a verb of fearing → fear clause · nē otherwise → negative purpose or negative indirect command · cum → temporal, causal or concessive · a question word after a verb of asking or knowing → indirect question.
✍️ Why step 2 is the valuable one. Every construction from Modules 8 to 13 needs two verbs: an indicative one running the sentence and a subjunctive one inside a clause. Every construction in Module 14 needs one. So counting verbs — a thing you can do without knowing what a single word means — puts you in the right half of the map immediately. Do it first, every time.
💡 One more free clue you already own. A subordinate subjunctive got its tense from the sequence rule — it had to look up at its main verb. A main-clause subjunctive has no main verb to look at, so sequence of tenses does not apply to it at all. That is why a wish can choose freely between present, imperfect and pluperfect: nothing is governing it. If you catch yourself asking “is this primary or secondary sequence?” about a sentence with only one verb in it, you have already gone wrong at step 2.
📚 PART 2 · FOUR WORKED WALK-THROUGHS — ALL WITH NĒ

Every sentence below contains nē. Every one means something different by it. Watch the steps run.

Nē veniat.
1. One verb: veniat, subjunctive.  2. Any indicative verb? No → main clause.  3. No utinam, not a question, 3rd singular → jussive, negative.
“Let him not come.” The nē is not introducing anything — there is nothing for it to introduce. It is simply the negative of the command.
Cōnsul mīlitēs collocāvit nē coniūrātī ex urbe fugerent.
1. Two verbs: collocāvit indicative, fugerent subjunctive.  2. Indicative main verb present → subordinate.  4. Opens with nē; collocāvit is not a verb of fearing and not a verb of ordering; the clause answers “why did he post them?” → negative purpose (m08).
“The consul stationed soldiers so that the conspirators would not escape from the city.” Gloss: collocō, collocāre — to station, post.
Cicerō ōrāvit nē iūdicēs Murēnam condemnārent.
1. Ōrāvit indicative, condemnārent subjunctive.  2. Subordinate.  4. Nē, and the main verb is a verb of asking → negative indirect command (m08).
“Cicero begged the jurors not to condemn Murena.” Notice the English: an indirect command comes out as an infinitive, not as a “so that” clause. That is how you can tell your own translation which one you chose.
Cicerō timēbat nē iūdicēs Murēnam condemnārent.
1. Timēbat indicative, condemnārent subjunctive.  2. Subordinate.  4. Nē, and the main verb is a verb of fearing → fear clause (m12), where nē flips its meaning.
“Cicero was afraid that the jurors would condemn Murena.” Compare the sentence above it word for word. The subordinate clause is identical — nē iūdicēs Murēnam condemnārent. Only the main verb changed, from ōrāvit to timēbat, and the meaning of nē reversed from “not to” to “that… will.” This is the pair the whole of Module 12 was about, and it is why step 4 says to look at the main verb.

So the nē procedure is two questions, and only two.
First: is there another verb in this sentence at all? No → main clause, and nē just means “not.”
Second: if yes, what kind of verb is it? Fearing → the reversal. Ordering or asking → “not to.” Anything else → “so that… not.”

And while the table is out, here is ut for comparison. It has three jobs, and none of them is a main-clause use — ut never introduces a main-clause subjunctive, so its presence is itself a reliable sign that you are in a subordinate clause.

⚠️ Two honest limits on the procedure. (1) Step 3 cannot always separate a jussive from an optative wish in the third person: Salva sit rēs pūblica is “let the republic be safe” or “may the republic be safe,” and grammars file it both ways. Where there is no utinam, either answer is defensible and nothing in this module is graded on it. (2) Step 2 says the subjunctive is “almost certainly” subordinate when there is an indicative verb about — because a sentence can contain both. Videāmus quid Catilīna faciat has a main-clause hortatory and a subordinate indirect question, and no indicative anywhere. Read the whole sentence, not just the first verb you find.

Main clause or subordinate — say it out loud, then flip. Aim for a second a card. Tomorrow's reading has nine of these in twelve lines.

🃏 FLIP CARDS · MAIN CLAUSE OR SUBORDINATE?
Nē veniat.
which job?
MAIN — jussive, negative. One verb only. Nē is just “not.” “Let him not come.”
Timeō nē veniat.
which job?
SUBORDINATE — fear clause (m12). A verb of fearing above it flips nē to “that… will.” “I am afraid that he will come.”
Ōrāvit nē venīret.
which job?
SUBORDINATE — negative indirect command (m08). A verb of asking above it. “He begged him not to come.”
Fūgit nē caperētur.
which job?
SUBORDINATE — negative purpose (m08). An ordinary main verb, and the clause answers “why?” “He fled so that he would not be caught.”
Utinam nē vēnisset!
which job?
MAIN — optative wish, pluperfect. “If only he had not come!” — and he did.
Quid faciam?
which job?
MAIN — deliberative question. “What am I to do?” The only one of the four whose negative is nōn.
Rogāvit quid facerem.
which job?
SUBORDINATE — indirect question (m11). Rogāvit above it, so the question has been reported, and facerem got its tense from the sequence rule.
Tam foedus erat ut nēmō aspiceret.
which job?
SUBORDINATE — result (m09). The signpost tam in the main clause and ut opening the clause. Ut never marks a main-clause subjunctive.
