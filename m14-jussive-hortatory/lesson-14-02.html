Lesson 14.02
Utinam: Three Kinds of Wish
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In a wish, the tense is not telling you when. It is telling you whether.
Add the little word utinam to a main-clause subjunctive and you have a wish. Then Latin does something remarkable: it uses the tense of that subjunctive to tell you how the speaker rates his own chances. Present tense — the wish is still open. Imperfect — the speaker is telling you it is not so now. Pluperfect — the speaker is telling you it did not happen, and cannot now. One word of grammar carries a whole judgment about the world, and last week's perfect and pluperfect subjunctive were built for this.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognise an optative wish and name utinam as its signpost.
2
I will be able to choose between present, imperfect and pluperfect in a wish, and say what each one claims about the facts.
3
I will be able to make a wish negative with utinam nē, and translate it without losing the negative.
📚 PART 1 · ONE WORD TURNS A COMMAND INTO A WISH

The name for this use is the optative subjunctive, from optō, optāre, optāvī — “to wish for, to choose.” The same root gives English option, optional and adopt. A Roman who wanted to mark a subjunctive out as a wish rather than an order put utinam in front of it.

Utinam + subjunctive = a wish. Negative: utinam nē + subjunctive.
English has no single word for it, so you have a choice of scaffolding: “If only…!”, “Would that…!”, “I wish…”, or sometimes just “May…!”. Pick whichever makes the English sound like something a person would say.

Notice that the negative is nē again — the same negative as the jussive and the hortatory yesterday. That is not a coincidence. All three are main-clause subjunctives, all three are about something willed rather than something reported, and Latin gives the whole family one negative.

💡 Where a wish and a command overlap. Yesterday you translated Salva sit rēs pūblica as “May the republic be safe” and called it a jussive. Some grammars would file that same sentence as an optative wish, and they are not wrong: with a third-person verb and no utinam, “let it be” and “may it be” run into each other. This is a genuinely soft border, and nothing on your quiz depends on it. What utinam gives you is certainty in the other direction: where utinam appears, you are looking at a wish, full stop.
📚 PART 2 · THE TENSE IS THE WHOLE MESSAGE

Everywhere else in this course, the tense of a subjunctive has been decided for you — by sequence of tenses, looking up at the main verb. A wish has no main verb above it. So the tense is free, and Latin spends that freedom on something else entirely: saying whether the wish can still come true.

Tense of the subjunctiveWhat kind of wish it isEnglish scaffoldingWhat the speaker is telling you about the facts
PRESENT
veniat, sit, absolvātur		a wish that may still come true“If only he may come!”
“May he come!”
“I hope he comes.”		Nothing yet. The door is open; the speaker is hoping, not reporting.
IMPERFECT
venīret, esset, imminēret		a wish about how things are now — and they are not“If only he were coming!”
“Would that he were here!”		That it is false right now. The wish is being made against a fact the speaker already knows.
PLUPERFECT
vēnisset, fuisset, facta esset		a wish about the past — and it did not happen“If only he had come!”
“Would that he had spoken!”		That it did not happen, and nothing can change that now.
✍️ Read the middle row again, slowly. The imperfect subjunctive in a wish does not mean the wish is about the past. It means the wish is about now, and that the speaker is admitting it is not the case. This is the single commonest mistake in the module: students see an imperfect and translate “if only he was coming.” The right English is “if only he were coming” — the same odd English form you use in “if only I were taller,” which is not about the past either.

And here is the payoff from last week. In Module 13 you built the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive and were told they existed to handle “already finished” action in subordinate clauses. This is the other reason they exist. Without the pluperfect subjunctive you simply could not say the most human sentence in Latin: I wish it had not happened.

STILL OPEN — present
Utinam Murēna absolvātur!
“If only Murena may be acquitted!” The trial is still going. Nobody knows yet.
NOT SO NOW — imperfect
Utinam Rōma tūta esset!
“If only Rome were safe!” She is not. The sentence says so without saying so.
DID NOT HAPPEN — pluperfect
Utinam nē Lentulus patriam prōdidisset!
“If only Lentulus had not betrayed his country!” He did. Nothing is left but the wish.
📖 GLOSS FOR THE THREE EXAMPLES
  • absolvātur — present subjunctive passive of absolvō, absolvere, absolvī, absolūtum: to acquit, set free from a charge
  • tūtus, -a, -um — safe, protected
  • prōdidisset — pluperfect subjunctive of prōdō, prōdere, prōdidī, prōditum: to betray, hand over
  • patria, patriae f. — native land, fatherland
📚 PART 3 · MORE WISHES, AND TWO HONEST FOOTNOTES
Utinam vōbīs haec ōrātiō persuādeat!
“If only this speech may persuade you!” Present — the speech is not finished yet, so the wish is live. Gloss: persuādeō, persuādēre (+ dative) — to persuade; ōrātiō, ōrātiōnis f. — speech.
Utinam nē tantum perīculum reī pūblicae imminēret!
“If only so great a danger were not hanging over the republic!” Imperfect — and the wish quietly reports that the danger is hanging over it. Gloss: immineō, imminēre (+ dative) — to hang over, threaten.
Utinam nē ista coniūrātiō umquam facta esset!
“If only that conspiracy had never been made!” Pluperfect, and passive — the two-word kind from Lesson 13.03: the participle facta plus esset. Gloss: iste, ista, istud — that (of yours), that one there; umquam — ever.
Utinam cōnsul illā nocte nōn dubitāvisset!
“If only the consul had not hesitated that night!” Pluperfect active. Gloss: dubitō, dubitāre, dubitāvī — to hesitate, doubt. Note the negative here is nōn rather than nē — see the second footnote below, and expect nē to be the form you are asked to produce.
⚠️ Footnote one: the fourth tense exists, and this module leaves it alone. A perfect subjunctive after utinam is possible in Latin — a wish about something that may already have happened, along the lines of “I hope he has said it.” It is uncommon, the grammars disagree about how to describe it, and it is not on your quiz or your translation. Three tenses do the work: present, imperfect, pluperfect. If you meet a perfect subjunctive after utinam in outside reading, note it and move on.
⚠️ Footnote two: utinam nōn does happen. The regular negative for a wish is utinam nē, and that is what you should write. But real authors sometimes use nōn after utinam, especially when what is being denied is one particular word rather than the whole wish. Do not let it shake you: utinam is still the signpost, the construction is still a wish, and the tense still tells you which kind. Produce nē; recognise both.

📜 PART 5 · SPEED SORT · CLICK EACH CARD

Name the tense and the kind of wish out loud, then flip. Aim for a second a card.

🃏 FLIP CARDS · TENSE FIRST, THEN THE KIND OF WISH
Utinam veniat!
which wish?
Present. Still open: “If only he may come!” No claim about whether he will.
Utinam venīret!
which wish?
Imperfect. About now, and it is not so: “If only he were coming!” — he is not.
Utinam vēnisset!
which wish?
Pluperfect. About the past, and it did not happen: “If only he had come!”
Utinam sit! · Utinam esset! · Utinam fuisset!
the three of sum
Present / imperfect / pluperfect. “May it be” · “if only it were” (it is not) · “if only it had been” (it was not).
Utinam nē…
what is nē doing?
The negative of a wish. Same negative as the jussive and hortatory, because all three are main-clause subjunctives.
Utinam nē facta esset!
which wish, and which voice?
Pluperfect passive — participle facta + esset, the two-word form from 13.03. “If only it had not been done!”
Timeō nē veniat.
wish or not?
Not a wish — a fear clause (m12). Timeō is the main verb; veniat is subordinate. “I am afraid that he will come.”
Veniat.
wish or command?
Jussive command (m14, yesterday): “Let him come.” With no utinam and a third-person verb, read it as a command first.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

In a wish, ask the tense a question it has never been asked before: not “when?” but “is it true?” Present → nobody knows yet. Imperfect → it is not so now. Pluperfect → it did not happen. The wish and the admission arrive in the same word.
