Lesson 14.01
The Subjunctive Steps Out

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Every subjunctive you have met since Module 6 has been somebody's dependent. Today one walks out on its own.
Purpose, indirect command, result, cum, indirect question, fear — six constructions, and in all six the subjunctive verb sat inside a subordinate clause, introduced by a signal word and governed by a main verb somewhere else in the sentence. Latin also uses the subjunctive as the main verb of a sentence, with nothing in front of it and nothing governing it. When it does, it stops meaning “that” or “when” or “whether” and starts meaning something much more direct: let him do it. Let us do it. That is today.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell a main-clause subjunctive from a subordinate one by looking for the signal word that is not there.
2
I will be able to form and translate the jussive (3rd person, “let him…”) and the hortatory (1st person plural, “let us…”).
3
I will be able to make both of them negative with nē, and say why nōn is wrong there.
📚 PART 1 · SIX MODULES, ALL INSIDE SOMETHING

Look at what you have built. Every row is a subjunctive, and every row has a word in front of it doing the introducing.

ConstructionSignal wordAn example you have read
purpose · m08ut / nēCicerō vēnit ut Catilīnam accūsāret.
indirect command · m08ut / nēCōnsul imperāvit ut coniūrātī dūcerentur.
result · m09ut / ut nōnTam foedus erat locus ut nēmō eum aspiceret.
cum clause · m10cumCum Lentulus praetor esset, magistrātū sē abdicāvit.
indirect question · m11a question wordCicerō rogāvit ubi Catilīna esset.
fear clause · m12nē / nē nōnTimeō nē coniūrātī urbem incendant.

Notice the shape they all share. Each sentence has two verbs: an indicative main verb (vēnit, imperāvit, rogāvit, timeō) and a subjunctive one tucked inside a clause. The subjunctive was never the whole sentence. It was always an ingredient.

Now delete the main verb and the signal word, and leave the subjunctive standing by itself:
Veniat.
One word. Present subjunctive, third person singular. No ut, no cum, no question word, no main verb anywhere. It is the whole sentence — and it means “Let him come.”
💡 This is not a new tense or a new set of endings. Veniat is the same form you have written a hundred times in purpose clauses. Nothing about the form changes in this module. What changes is where the form is standing — and standing alone gives it a different job. Module 14 is the only module this year that teaches you no new endings at all.
📚 PART 2 · THE JUSSIVE — GIVING AN ORDER TO SOMEBODY ELSE

The word comes from iubeō, iubēre, iussī — “to order.” A jussive subjunctive is a command in the third person, and Latin needs it for a simple reason: you cannot give an order directly to somebody who is not there.

Jussive = present subjunctive, 3rd person singular or plural, standing as the main verb.
Translate it with “let him / let her / let them…” — or, when the tone is more official, “he is to… / they are to…”, or “may he…” when it is closer to a blessing.

laudō, laudāre (1st)laudetlaudentlet him praise / let them praise
maneō, manēre (2nd)maneatmaneantlet him stay / let them stay
dīcō, dīcere (3rd)dīcatdīcantlet him speak / let them speak
faciō, facere (3rd io)faciatfaciantlet him do / let them do
veniō, venīre (4th)veniatveniantlet him come / let them come
sum, essesitsintlet him be / let them be
loquor, loquī (deponent, m02)loquāturloquanturlet him speak / let them speak

In sentences, from the world of the module:

Reus ipse respondeat.
“Let the defendant answer himself.” Gloss: reus, reī m. — defendant, the accused.
Iūdicēs lēgem spectent, nōn hominem.
“Let the jurors look at the law, not at the man.” Gloss: iūdex, iūdicis m. — juror, judge. Note that nōn hominem here is negating a noun, not the verb — that is ordinary Latin and has nothing to do with the rule in Part 4.
Salva sit rēs pūblica.
“May the republic be safe.” A jussive of sum, and the same form you would use for a prayer. Gloss: salvus, -a, -um — safe, unharmed.
✍️ English does the same thing and hides it better. “Let him come” is not really about permission — nobody is being let. English has to borrow the verb “let” because it has no third-person command form of its own. Latin has one, so it just uses it: veniat. When you translate, the word “let” is English scaffolding, not a Latin word you have to find in the sentence.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Before the drills: does it surprise you that the same form can mean “so that he may come” in one sentence and “let him come” in another? What is your instinct about why that works?
📚 PART 3 · THE HORTATORY — PULLING YOURSELF IN

From hortor, hortārī, hortātus sum, the deponent you met in Module 2: “to urge, encourage.” A hortatory subjunctive is the first person plural, and it means “let us…” — the speaker urging a group that includes himself.

Hortatory = present subjunctive, 1st person plural, standing as the main verb.
Translate with “let us…” or, in more natural English, “let's…”. Some books call it the exhortative or file it with the jussive as one construction; the form and the meaning are the same whatever it is called.
Verb1st plural subjunctive“Let us…”
laudō, laudāre (1st)laudēmuslet us praise
maneō, manēre (2nd)maneāmuslet us stay
dīcō, dīcere (3rd)dīcāmuslet us speak
faciō, facere (3rd io)faciāmuslet us do, let us make
audiō, audīre (4th)audiāmuslet us listen
veniō, venīre (4th)veniāmuslet us come
sum, essesīmuslet us be
sequor, sequī (deponent, m02)sequāmurlet us follow
Nunc videāmus quid rēs pūblica postulet.
“Now let us see what the republic demands.” Gloss: postulō, postulāre — to demand, require. Two subjunctives in one sentence, doing two entirely different jobs: videāmus is a hortatory main verb, and postulet is an indirect question hanging off it. You will sort sentences like this on Thursday.
Rem pūblicam dēfendāmus dum possumus.
“Let us defend the republic while we can.” Gloss: dum (+ indicative) — while.
⚠️ The most famous hortatory in Latin is one you may not use yet. Eāmus — “let us go” — is the example every textbook reaches for, and it comes from the irregular verb eō, īre, which is Module 15. So does ferāmus, from ferō. This module builds everything on regular verbs and on sum: veniāmus, dīcāmus, faciāmus, audiāmus, sequāmur, sīmus. When you reach Module 15 and the irregulars arrive, they will slot into this construction with no new rule at all — which is the point of doing the construction first.
📚 PART 4 · THE NEGATIVE IS NĒ, AND THAT MATTERS

Here is the rule, and it is one line long:

A jussive or hortatory subjunctive is made negative with nē, never with nōn.
Veniat. → Nē veniat. — “Let him not come.”
Dīcāmus. → Nē dīcāmus. — “Let us not speak.”
Iūdicēs hominem spectent. → Nē iūdicēs hominem spectent. — “Let the jurors not look at the man.”

Say the reason out loud, because it is the reason for the whole family: nōn denies a fact; nē blocks a wish. Nōn venit means “he is not coming” — a report about the world. Nē veniat does not report anything; it pushes against an action nobody has taken yet. Latin keeps two different words for those two different jobs, and the indicative gets one of them while the willed subjunctive gets the other.

⚠️ And this is now nē's fourth job. Negative purpose (m08). Negative indirect command (m08). Fear clause — where it means the opposite, “that will happen” (m12). And now the negative of a main-clause subjunctive (m14). Four jobs, one word, and no ending anywhere tells you which. That is Thursday's whole lesson, and it is the most useful thing in this module. Do not try to solve it today — just notice that the pile has got one item bigger.

One more thing about the form, and then we stop

Every jussive and hortatory in this lesson is in the present subjunctive. That is not an accident of the examples: a command or an exhortation is about what should happen now or next, so the present is the tense that fits. And notice what does not apply here — sequence of tenses. The two-row table from Module 7 tells a subordinate subjunctive which tense to take by looking at its main verb. A main-clause subjunctive has no main verb above it. There is nothing to sequence against, so the tense is chosen by meaning alone. Tomorrow that fact does real work, because with wishes the tense is the entire point.

💡 For recognition only, not for production. You will sometimes see nē plus a perfect subjunctive used to forbid something to a person being addressed directly: nē dīxerīs, “don't say it.” That is a real classical prohibition, it uses the m13 form you built last week, and it is worth being able to recognise. This module will not ask you to write one, and it is not on the quiz.

★ OFFICIUM · SERVICE

Officium, officiī n. is built from ob (“toward”) and facere (“to do”) — a thing done toward somebody else. You met it in Module 5, where the gerundive of obligation named what must be done and whose name was on it. It gives English office, officer, and official: all words for a duty that belongs to a person because of where they stand.

Module 5's officium was something owed by you. This module's is something you ask of other people, and that is a harder thing to do honestly. Every sentence in this lesson is aimed outward: veniat, let him come; dēfendāmus, let us defend. A jussive is a man with no army and no power of arrest asking a room full of strangers to act. That was most of what a Roman advocate had. Cicero could not compel a jury, and in the speech you will read on Friday he does not pretend to — he asks. Notice, too, what the hortatory does that the jussive cannot: dēfendāmus puts the speaker inside the duty he is naming. “Let us” costs the speaker something; “let them” does not. It is worth watching, in Cicero and in yourself, which of the two a person reaches for.

Lesson 14.02 — Utinam: Three Kinds of Wish
One little word, utinam, turns a subjunctive into a wish — and then the tense decides what kind of wish it is: one that might still come true, one about how things are now and are not, or one about a past that did not happen. This is where last week's perfect and pluperfect subjunctive suddenly pay for themselves.
💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A subjunctive with nothing in front of it is not a broken clause with a missing ut. It is a command or a wish, and it is the whole sentence. Third person means “let him”; first person plural means “let us”; and either one goes negative with nē.
