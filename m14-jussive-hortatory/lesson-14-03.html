Lesson 14.03
What Am I to Do?
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Some questions are not looking for information. They are looking for a way out.
The fourth and last main-clause use is the deliberative question: Quid faciam? — “What am I to do?” It is a real question, with a question mark, and the person asking it usually has no answer and does not expect one from anybody else. The subjunctive is what makes it that kind of question rather than a request for facts. You will also pick up this module's vocabulary today, all of it from the Roman courtroom — and you will meet the one place in this whole family where the negative is not nē.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognise a deliberative question and translate it with “am I to…?” or “was I to…?”
2
I will be able to choose present or imperfect according to whether the speaker is deciding now or looking back at a decision already made.
3
I will be able to use this module's fifteen words about trials, votes and pleading, and name three English derivatives from them.
📚 PART 1 · A QUESTION WITH NO ANSWER IN IT

The name comes from dēlīberō, dēlīberāre — “to weigh up, consider.” A deliberative question puts the subjunctive in a main clause and adds a question mark. The speaker is not asking for a fact. He is asking what course of action is even available.

Quid faciam?
“What am I to do?” — or “What should I do?”, or “What is there for me to do?”
Compare the indicative: Quid faciō? would be “What am I doing?” — a question about the actual present, the kind you could answer by looking.

Three more, all of them the sort of thing a man says when the situation has closed in on him:

Quid ā vōbīs petam, iūdicēs?
“What am I to ask of you, gentlemen of the jury?” Gloss: petō, petere, petīvī — to seek, ask for; iūdex, iūdicis m. — juror.
Ubi nunc auxilium petāmus?
“Where are we now to look for help?” First person plural — a group deliberating together. Gloss: auxilium, auxiliī n. — help, aid.
Utrum taceam an vēra dīcam?
“Am I to keep silent, or to say what is true?” The utrum…an frame from Module 11, doing service in a deliberative question. Gloss: vēra — true things, the truth (neuter plural of vērus, -a, -um).
💡 Where you have heard this before. Not in Latin — in English, constantly. “What am I supposed to do?” “Where do we go from here?” “How was I to know?” None of those wants an answer either. Latin has a grammatical form for that gesture, which is one of the small ways the language is more precise than ours: it can mark the difference between asking for information and asking out of difficulty.
📚 PART 2 · TWO TENSES, AND THEY DO MEAN TIME THIS TIME

Yesterday the tense of a wish told you about truth, not time. In a deliberative question the tense goes back to meaning what you would expect:

TenseWhen the deliberating happensEnglish
PRESENT
faciam, petāmus, dīcam		now — the speaker is stuck at this moment“What am I to do?”
“What should we do?”
IMPERFECT
facerem, dīcerem, resisterem		then — the speaker is looking back at a moment when he was stuck, usually to defend what he chose“What was I to do?”
“What should I have done?”
Quid facerem? Quid dīcerem?
“What was I to do? What was I to say?” Imperfect — and notice what the question is really doing. It is not curiosity. It is a defence: there was nothing else I could have done, and you know it. This is the tense a politician reaches for when the decision has already been made and is being criticised.
✍️ And here comes a collision, exactly like the one in Module 13. For third- and fourth-conjugation verbs, the 1st person singular present subjunctive and the 1st person singular future indicative are the same form. Faciam is both “let me do / am I to do” and “I will do.” So is dīcam, petam, veniam, audiam. Latin lived with it; so must you. The good news is that the first and second conjugations keep them apart: laudem (subjunctive) against laudābō (future); videam against vidēbō. And in the plural everything separates: faciāmus (subjunctive) against faciēmus (future).
💡 So how do you decide? By the question, not the form. “Quid faciam?” asked by a man in trouble, with no answer following, is deliberative: “what am I to do?” The same words in a plain statement of intention — crās hoc faciam, “tomorrow I will do this” — are future indicative. And English has exactly the same ambiguity in the words “What shall I do?”, which is why the collision feels less strange than it looks.
📚 PART 3 · THE EXCEPTION: THIS ONE TAKES NŌN

You have been told twice this week that main-clause subjunctives take nē. That is true of the jussive, the hortatory and the optative — three of the four. The deliberative question is the exception, and it negates with nōn.

Main-clause useNegativeExample
jussive — “let him…”nēNē veniat. — “Let him not come.”
hortatory — “let us…”nēNē dīcāmus. — “Let us not speak.”
optative — a wishnēUtinam nē veniat! — “If only he may not come!”
deliberative questionnōnAn ego cōnsul nōn resisterem? — “Was I, the consul, not to resist?”

The reason is worth having, because it is the same reason as before, read from the other end. Nē belongs to a willed action — something the speaker is trying to bring about or prevent. A deliberative question is not willing anything: it is asking, genuinely, and a question is closer to a statement of fact than to a command. So it takes the ordinary negative of statements, nōn. Three of the four want nē because three of the four are trying to make something happen. The fourth is only asking.

⚠️ Learn the exception now rather than discovering it later. This is the sort of detail that gets skipped in a hurry and then costs a mark on a translation. Write it on the same card as the nē rule: jussive, hortatory, optative → nē. Deliberative → nōn. Gloss for the example: an — here introducing an indignant question, “what, was I…?”; resistō, resistere, restitī — to resist, stand against.

✍️ Etymology minute: ambitus. The word means, literally, a going-around — because that is how a Roman ran for office. A candidate walked the city with a crowd of supporters, greeted voters by name, and asked for their suffrāgia. Going round was legal; paying while you went round was ambitus, the crime. And the English word that came out of it is ambition — which still carries a faint whiff of the original suspicion. (The verb behind ambitus is a compound of eō, which is Module 15.)

Six derivatives. Say the English word before you flip the card.

🃏 FLIP CARDS · WHICH ENGLISH WORD CAME FROM THIS?
absolvō
to acquit
absolve, absolution. Literally “to loosen away from” — ab + solvō. A Roman jury did not forgive a defendant; it untied him from the charge.
suffrāgium
a vote
suffrage. The right to vote. The nineteenth-century campaigners called themselves suffragists from this Latin noun.
iūdex, iūdicis
juror
judge, judicial, judicious, prejudice. A prae-iūdicium is a judgment made in advance — before the trial.
precor
to beg
prayer — and, less obviously, precarious. Something precārius was held only by begging, at somebody else's pleasure. That is why a precarious position is one that can be taken away.
cōnservō
to keep safe
conserve, conservation, conservative. Con- (thoroughly) + servō (to save, keep). Cicero's word for what he wanted the jury to do with Murena.
ōrō, ōrāre
to plead
orator, oration, oral. And ōrātiō, a speech — the word Cicero used for the thing you will read on Friday.
