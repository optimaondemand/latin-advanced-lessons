Prō Murēnā: The Consul Pleads
Reading · Module Review
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Cicero wrote the law. Then he stood up and defended the man charged under it.
Late in 63 BC, with Catiline still in the field in Etruria and his own consulship nearly over, Cicero went into court to defend Lucius Licinius Murena — elected to be consul the following year, and now accused of buying the election. This is not the Cicero of the Catilinarian speeches, thundering at an enemy. This is Cicero asking: for a favour, for a verdict, for the survival of the state as he sees it. Which is exactly why the module's grammar lives here. When a speaker stops reporting and starts pleading, Latin reaches for the main-clause subjunctive.

📚 PART 1 · BEFORE YOU READ

Four things the passage assumes and does not explain.

1. Consuls took office on 1 January. Two of them, always two, elected the previous summer. Between the election and 1 January a man was cōnsul dēsignātus — consul-elect: chosen, but not yet holding the office. Murena was consul-elect for 62 BC when he was put on trial.
2. Ambitus was the crime of buying an election. Not campaigning — campaigning was expected, and the word literally means “a going-around,” because that is what a candidate did. What the law forbade was paying for votes, hiring crowds, and the rest of it. A conviction ended a political career.
3. Cicero had carried a bribery law himself, that year, as consul. Then a man was charged under the new law, and Cicero appeared for the defence. He does not hide this in the speech — he raises it himself, which is what a good advocate does with a fact the other side is going to use.
4. Catiline was still alive. He had left Rome in November and was in Etruria with an army. The battle of Pistoria — Module 12's reading — had not happened yet. So when Cicero says the republic cannot afford to lose a consul, he is not being dramatic; he is describing the calendar.
💡 Watch the grammar do the arguing. There are nine main-clause subjunctives in thirteen lines — one hortatory, three jussives, three wishes and two deliberative questions — and three subordinate subjunctives mixed in among them, one of which is there deliberately to catch you out. Every one of the main-clause ones is a move in an argument: let us look at this, if only it had not happened, what am I to do? This is what the construction is for.
📜 PART 2 · THE READING
Adapted from Cicero, Prō Murēnā. The Latin below is not Cicero's own Latin. It is an adaptation written for this course, using only grammar you have been taught, so that the argument of the speech can be read in December of your first year of Advanced Latin. The substance is drawn from the speech and from what is securely known about the trial: Murena was a consul-elect prosecuted for ambitus; Servius Sulpicius, a defeated candidate, and Marcus Cato were among the prosecutors; Cicero had carried a bribery law as consul that same year; and part of his argument was the danger of leaving the state a consul short while Catiline was in the field. Do not quote any line of this passage as Cicero's words.

1Iūdicēs, Lūcius Licinius Murēna, cōnsul dēsignātus, hodiē reus ambitūs est.

2Accūsat eum Servius Sulpicius, quī ipse cōnsulātum petīverat; accūsat etiam Mārcus Catō, cuius sevēritātem omnēs timēmus.

3Ipse hōc annō lēgem dē ambitū cōnstituī, nec eam lēgem dēfendere dēsinō.

4Sed videāmus tandem quid rēs pūblica hōc tempore postulet.

5Catilīna cum exercitū in Etrūriā adhūc est; bellum reī pūblicae imminet.

6Utinam nē ista coniūrātiō umquam facta esset!

7Sed facta est; itaque nunc dē salūte omnium cōgitandum est.

8Kalendīs Iānuāriīs duo cōnsulēs rem pūblicam accipiant.

9Nam ūnus cōnsul tantum perīculum sōlus sustinēre nōn potest.

10Utinam nē tantum perīculum reī pūblicae iam imminēret! Sed imminet.

11Quid igitur faciam, iūdicēs? Quid ā vōbīs petam?

12Vōs igitur ōrō atque obsecrō ut hunc virum reī pūblicae cōnservētis, nē initiō annī alterum cōnsulem āmittāmus.

13Utinam vōbīs haec ōrātiō persuādeat! Salvus sit Murēna; salva sit rēs pūblica.

⚠️ Two honest notes on the passage. (1) Cicero's actual speech is far longer, wittier and more devious than this, and much of its most famous material — his teasing of Cato's philosophy, his comparison of a lawyer's career with a soldier's — has been left out because it needs grammar you do not own yet. What is here is the backbone of the argument, not the speech. (2) Line 12 contains a trap, and it is deliberate: āmittāmus is a first person plural subjunctive, which is what a hortatory looks like — but it is not one. Look at what is in front of it.

🏛️ THE ROMAN WORLD · A TRIAL IN THE OPEN AIR

A Roman criminal trial happened outdoors, in or beside the Forum, in front of a large panel of jurors and whoever else felt like stopping to listen. A magistrate presided, but nobody in the space had the job of explaining the law to the jury or ruling out an unfair argument. The advocates spoke in turn, at length, and the speech was the case: there was no cross-examination as we would recognise it, and the crowd standing round the edges was part of the event. Cicero was not alone on Murena's side — Quintus Hortensius and Marcus Crassus, two of the most formidable speakers in Rome, spoke before he did. Then the jurors voted with ballots, and there was no appeal.

What makes this particular trial uncomfortable is the law. Rome passed anti-bribery laws over and over across the late Republic, which is the surest possible sign that they were not working; Cicero had carried one himself, as consul, that same year. Weeks later he stood up to defend a man charged under it, arguing that the republic could not be left a consul short on 1 January with Catiline's army in the field. Murena was acquitted. Whether that verdict was justice or convenience is a fair question, and we cannot settle it, because we have only Cicero's side: the prosecution speeches are lost. Cato's, in particular, we would very much like to have.

✍️ And the two rules that apply to every row. (1) Sequence of tenses does not apply — there is no main verb above a main-clause subjunctive, so nothing governs its tense. The tense is chosen by meaning: which is why the three utinam rows exist at all. (2) The negative is nē for the first five rows and nōn for the last two. Jussive, hortatory and optative are willing something; a deliberative question is only asking, so it takes the ordinary negative of statements.

Module 15 — Conditions and Irregular Verbs
“If the Senate had not acted, Rome would have burned.” Latin has a whole system for saying what would be and what would have been — and the imperfect and pluperfect subjunctive do it, in exactly the way they did in this week's wishes. 💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Line 10 of the reading is a wish and an admission in the same breath: Utinam nē tantum perīculum imminēret! Sed imminet. Cicero chose the imperfect, and then said out loud what the imperfect had already told the jury. That is what this module buys you — a grammar in which hoping for something and knowing better can be said at once.
