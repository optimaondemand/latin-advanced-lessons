📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Change one word at the front, and the last word has to move too.
The other half of the rule: main verb imperfect, perfect, or pluperfect → the subordinate subjunctive is imperfect. This is the sequence of storytelling, which means it is the sequence you will meet most often in Sallust — and today you will feel it work, by taking yesterday's sentences and pushing them into the past.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name all three main-verb tenses that trigger secondary sequence.
2
I will be able to build the imperfect subjunctive of any verb from its present infinitive, in any person.
3
I will be able to take a primary-sequence sentence and rewrite it in secondary sequence, changing both verbs correctly.
📚 PART 1 · THREE TENSES, ONE CAMP

Primary had two tenses. Secondary has three — and they all behave exactly the same way. You do not need to tell them apart for this rule. You only need to notice that the main verb is past.

Main verb tenseExample main verbA whole sentence
Imperfectvigilābat — he was keeping watchCicerō vigilābat ut cīvēs tūtī essent.
Perfectvigilāvit — he kept watchCicerō vigilāvit ut cīvēs tūtī essent.
Pluperfectvigilāverat — he had kept watchCicerō vigilāverat ut cīvēs tūtī essent.
💡 Three main verbs, one answer. Whether the watching was ongoing, finished, or already over before something else, the clause after it gets essent. This is why the rule is worth learning as two camps rather than six pairings: the work is in deciding past or not-past, and after that there is exactly one form to reach for.
📚 PART 2 · THE ANSWER FORM — IMPERFECT SUBJUNCTIVE

the friendliest formation rule in the whole verb system

regerem, regerēs, regeret — regerēmus, regerētis, regerent

Passive and deponent: the same rule, the passive endings

Type3rd singular3rd plural
Passive (1st) — amoramārētur — he might be lovedamārentur
Passive (3rd) — regorregerēturregerentur
Deponent — loquorloquerētur — he might speakloquerentur
✍️ Deponents need a moment's care. The rule says "present infinitive plus endings," and a deponent's present infinitive is loquī. But the imperfect subjunctive is built on the active-shaped infinitive that the verb would have if it had one — loquere- — and then takes passive endings: loquerer, loquerēris, loquerētur, loquerēmur, loquerēminī, loquerentur. Learn loquerētur as a whole word; you will see it in Friday's reading.
📚 PART 3 · THE FLIP TEST

Yesterday's sentences, pushed into the past. In each pair the meaning of the subordinate clause is unchanged — only the main verb moved, and the subjunctive moved with it. Read the left column, predict the right, then check.

PRIMARY — not-past main verbSECONDARY — past main verb
Cicerō vigilat ut cīvēs tūtī sint.
He keeps watch so that they may be safe.		Cicerō vigilābat ut cīvēs tūtī essent.
He was keeping watch so that they might be safe.
Cōnsul mīlitēs mittit ut urbem dēfendant.
He sends soldiers so that they may defend the city.		Cōnsul mīlitēs mīsit ut urbem dēfenderent.
He sent soldiers so that they might defend the city.
Cicerō rogat cūr Catilīna discēdat.
He asks why Catiline is leaving.		Cicerō rogāvit cūr Catilīna discēderet.
He asked why Catiline was leaving.
Cicerō ad populum prōcēdit ut apertē loquātur.
He comes forward to the people in order to speak openly.		Cicerō ad populum prōcessit ut apertē loquerētur.
He came forward to the people in order to speak openly.
Coniūrātī arma parant ut urbem capere possint.
They are preparing weapons so that they may be able to take the city.		Coniūrātī arma parāverant ut urbem capere possent.
They had prepared weapons so that they might be able to take the city.
💡 Look at the last row. The main verb is parāverant — pluperfect, "they had prepared." Pluperfect is a past tense, so it is secondary, so possent. Students often stall on the pluperfect because it feels like it needs something extra. It does not. Past is past.
✍️ How to render it in English. Primary clauses usually come out with "may" or a plain present; secondary clauses with "might" or "would," or a plain past. These are conveniences, not rules — sometimes the smoothest English drops the helper verb entirely ("he came forward to speak openly"). Get the Latin tense right first; then write the English a person would actually say.
📚 PART 4 · TWO PAIRS TO GUARD

Two places where a single character carries the whole meaning. Both cost students marks every year.

ONE MACRON — TWO MOODS
amās — you love
present indicative
amēs — you may love
present subjunctive
ONE LETTER — TWO SUBJECTS
regeret — he might rule
imperfect subj., 3rd singular
regerent — they might rule
imperfect subj., 3rd plural

The first pair is about mood: get it wrong and you have turned a wish or a purpose into a flat statement of fact. The second is about who: get it wrong and you have handed the action to the wrong people. Neither error is caught by the sequence rule — you catch them by looking.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Imperfect, perfect, and pluperfect all count as past, and all three call for the imperfect subjunctive — which you build by taking the present infinitive and adding -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt. Then check the last letter: -t is one person, -nt is several.
