📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Half the rule, learned properly, is worth more than all of it learned vaguely.
Today is the primary half. Main verb present or future → the subordinate subjunctive is present. That is one line of grammar, so we are going to spend the lesson making the forms automatic and learning what kind of clause they show up in.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognize a present or future main verb on sight and call the sequence primary.
2
I will be able to supply the present subjunctive a primary clause needs — any conjugation, active or passive, deponent, or esse.
3
I will be able to use this module's new vocabulary, including vigilō and custōdia, in a subordinate clause.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT COUNTS AS PRIMARY

Two tenses, and only two, put a sentence into primary sequence. Learn them as a pair: the non-past tenses.

Main verb tenseExample main verbA whole sentence
Presentvigilat — he keeps watchCicerō vigilat ut cīvēs tūtī sint.
"Cicero keeps watch so that the citizens may be safe."
Futurevigilābit — he will keep watchCicerō vigilābit ut cīvēs tūtī sint.
"Cicero will keep watch so that the citizens may be safe."
💡 Same subjunctive, twice. That is the whole point of the table above. Whether Cicero keeps watch now or will keep watch tomorrow, the clause after it gets sint. Latin sorts main verbs into past and not-past, and the present subjunctive answers everything in the not-past pile.
✍️ The English trap. You will want to translate the second sentence "so that the citizens would be safe," because that is how English handles a future frame. Then, translating back into Latin, "would" will pull you toward the imperfect subjunctive. Do not let it. In English, may and would are stylistic choices; in Latin the tense is decided by a rule, and the rule looks only at the main verb.

amem, amēs, amet — amēmus, amētis, ament
regam, regās, regat — regāmus, regātis, regant
⚠️ First conjugation is the odd one out, and it is the one that bites. Everywhere else the subjunctive marker is an a. In the 1st conjugation it is an e — which means the 1st-conjugation present subjunctive and the 2nd-conjugation present indicative can look alarmingly similar, and it means amēs (subjunctive) sits one macron away from amās (indicative). Slow down on 1st-conjugation verbs.

📚 PART 3 · THE CONTAINERS — AND AN HONEST NOTE ABOUT THEM

A subjunctive does not float loose. It sits inside a subordinate clause of some particular kind, and Latin has a good number of those kinds. Here is the honest situation: this module teaches the tense rule, not the clause types. The clause types each get their own module later. So all week, every clause you meet is labelled for you.

ContainerWhat it meansStatus in this course
ut + subjunctive"so that…, in order that…" — a purpose clauseNot yet studied — gloss provided. Taught fully in Module 8.
nē + subjunctive"so that… not…, to prevent…" — a negative purpose clauseNot yet studied — gloss provided. Module 8.
cum + subjunctive"when…, since…, although…"Not yet studied — gloss provided. Module 10.
question word + subjunctive
cūr, quid, ubi		"…why / what / where…" reported inside another sentence — an indirect questionNot yet studied — gloss provided. Module 11.
💡 Why teach the rule before the clauses? Because the rule is the same in all of them. Learn it once now, with the clause types handed to you, and every module from 8 to 12 gets easier: you will already know which tense goes in the box before you learn what the box is called. Nothing you are graded on in this module asks you to name a clause type.
✍️ One exception worth knowing now: plenty of subordinate clauses take the indicative, and those obey no sequence rule at all. Catilīna discessit, quia in urbe manēre nōn poterat — "Catiline left, because he could not stay in the city." Poterat is indicative; it reports a plain fact and picks its own tense freely. Sequence of tenses is a rule about subjunctives. If the verb is indicative, the rule is simply not in play.

📜 Etymology moment. Vigilō gives English vigil, vigilant, vigilance, and (through French) surveillance — all of them about staying awake so that someone else can sleep, which is exactly the claim Cicero makes about himself. Custōdia gives custody and custodian. Aperiō gives aperture, an opening. Porta gives portal and portico. Salvus gives salvation, salutary, and, by a long road through French, safe. And admīrātiō gives admire, marvel, and miracle.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Present main verb and future main verb behave identically: both are primary, and both take the present subjunctive. When your English says "so that he would…" but the Latin main verb is future, trust the Latin rule and write the present subjunctive.
