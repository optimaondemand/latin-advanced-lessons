📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Eight subjunctives, and not one of them chose its own tense.
The day after Catiline left Rome, Cicero spoke again — but not to the Senate this time. To the people. Same crisis, different room, different voice. Watch what the sequence rule does as he moves between telling them what happened and telling them what he will do next.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to read an adapted passage from Cicero's In Catilīnam II and translate every sequenced subjunctive in it.
2
I will be able to point at each subjunctive's own main verb and justify its tense out loud.
3
I will be able to explain why a speech to the Roman people sounds different from a speech to the Senate.
📜 PART 1 · THE READING

Adapted from Cicero, In Catilīnam II. A simplified Latin retelling — not Cicero's own sentences. One line, marked below, is his own words. Every subjunctive is marked in red. Read it once straight through for sense before you analyse anything.

Postrīdiē Cicerō ad Rōstra prōcessit ut cum populō Rōmānō apertē loquerētur. Prīdiē in senātū ōrātiōnem habuerat; nunc cīvēs ipsōs docēre cōnābātur.

"Quirītēs," inquit, "Catilīna ex urbe discessit. Abiit, excessit, ēvāsit, ērūpit."  ⚖️ underlined = Cicero's own words, unadapted — see the note below

Multī in populō rogābant cūr hostis ex urbe discēderet et quid nunc ageret. Cicerō rem tōtam eīs explicāvit.

"Ego semper vigilāvī ut vōs tūtī essētis. Cōnsulēs custōdiās posuērunt nē coniūrātī portās aperīrent. Catilīna igitur discessit, quia in urbe manēre nōn poterat.

"Nunc quoque vigilō ut rēs pūblica salva sit. Ego mīlitēs mittam ut urbem dēfendant. Cōnsulēs semper vigilābunt ut nēmō rem pūblicam dēleat. Nihil timēte, Quirītēs: nam cōnsul vester vōbīscum est."

Populus verba cōnsulis magnā cum admīrātiōne audiēbat.

⚖️ The one unadapted line. Abiit, excessit, ēvāsit, ērūpit is Cicero's own Latin, from the opening of In Catilīnam II — four perfect indicative verbs, all meaning roughly "he left," stacked so that each one hits harder than the last: abiit, "he went away" · excessit, "he withdrew" · ēvāsit, "he slipped out, escaped" · ērūpit, "he burst out." Everything else on this page is a simplified retelling written for you.
📖 VOCABULARY GLOSS · every word not on your running list
  • postrīdiē (adv.) — on the next day
  • Rōstra, Rōstrōrum n. pl. — the Rostra, the speaker's platform in the Forum
  • prōcessit — perfect of prōcēdō, "went forward, came out"
  • ut + subjunctive — "so that, in order that" (purpose clause; not yet studied — gloss provided. Module 8)
  • apertē (adv.) — openly, plainly
  • loquerētur — imperfect subjunctive of the deponent loquor, "he might speak"
  • prīdiē (adv.) — on the day before
  • in senātū — "in the Senate" (ablative of senātus, 4th declension)
  • ōrātiōnem habuerat — "he had delivered a speech" (idiom: habeō ōrātiōnem)
  • ipsōs — acc. pl. m. of ipse, here "the citizens themselves"
  • cōnābātur — imperfect of the deponent cōnor, "he was trying"
  • Quirītēs — "Citizens!" the formal address to the Roman people in assembly
  • inquit — "he says / he said," inserted inside a quotation
  • abiit, excessit, ēvāsit, ērūpit — "he went away, he withdrew, he escaped, he burst out" (four perfect indicatives; Cicero's own words)
  • hostis, hostis m. — enemy (of the state)
  • cūr … discēderet, quid … ageret — reported questions, "why he was leaving," "what he was doing" (indirect question; not yet studied — gloss provided. Module 11)
  • rem tōtam — "the whole matter" (acc. of rēs tōta)
  • eīs — "to them" (dat. pl. of is, ea, id)
  • explicāvit — perfect of explicō, "explained, laid out"
  • vigilāvī — perfect of vigilō, "I kept watch, I have kept watch"
  • tūtī — nom. pl. m. of tūtus, "safe"
  • essētis — imperfect subjunctive, 2nd pl. of esse, "you might be"
  • custōdiās posuērunt — "posted guards" (acc. pl. of custōdia + perfect of pōnō)
  • nē + subjunctive — "so that… not" (negative purpose clause; not yet studied — gloss provided. Module 8)
  • portās — acc. pl. of porta, "the gates"
  • aperīrent — imperfect subjunctive, 3rd pl. of aperiō, "they might open"
  • igitur — therefore, and so
  • quia — because (takes the indicative)
  • poterat — imperfect indicative of possum, "he was able" — a plain fact, not a subjunctive
  • quoque — also, too
  • salva — nom. sg. f. of salvus, "safe, unharmed"
  • mittam — future indicative 1st sg. of mittō, "I will send" — it is the main verb of its sentence, not a subjunctive
  • dēleat — present subjunctive, 3rd sg. of dēleō, "may destroy"
  • nēmō — no one, nobody
  • Nihil timēte — "Fear nothing" (imperative plural of timeō)
  • nam — for, because
  • vester, vestra, vestrum — your (of you all)
  • vōbīscum — "with you (all)" (cum attached to the end of vōbīs)
  • magnā cum admīrātiōne — "with great wonder" (ablative of admīrātiō)
✍️ Two notes about the adaptation. First, the clause types here — purpose with ut and nē, reported questions with cūr and quid — are all glossed for you, because they are taught in Modules 8 and 11. Your job this week is the tense inside them, not the name on the box. Second, the whole passage stays inside two subjunctive tenses, present and imperfect. Real Cicero uses all four; when you read him unglossed in Module 13 and beyond, the other two will be yours.

🔍 Watch the hinge in the middle of the speech. While Cicero is explaining what happened, his main verbs are perfect — vigilāvī, posuērunt — and every clause under them is imperfect subjunctive. The moment he turns to what he is doing now and will do next, the main verbs switch to present and future — vigilō, mittam, vigilābunt — and the subjunctives switch with them. Nothing about the citizens' safety changed. Cicero's stance toward time changed, and the grammar reported it.
💡 And one verb the rule never touched. In quia in urbe manēre nōn poterat, poterat is imperfect indicative. Quia governs the indicative, so there is no sequence decision to make: the clause states a fact and takes whatever tense the fact needs. Check the mood before you reach for the rule.
✍️ PART 3 · TRANSLATE, THEN CHECK

Write your English out before flipping. All four of these are good rehearsal for the translation assignment.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Postrīdiē Cicerō ad Rōstra prōcessit ut cum populō Rōmānō apertē loquerētur.
click to compare
"On the next day Cicero came forward to the Rostra in order to speak openly with the Roman people." Main verb prōcessit is perfect → secondary → loquerētur, imperfect subjunctive of a deponent, translated actively. Notice that the smoothest English drops the helper verb entirely: "in order to speak."
Multī in populō rogābant cūr hostis ex urbe discēderet et quid nunc ageret.
click to compare
"Many in the crowd were asking why the enemy was leaving the city and what he was doing now." One main verb, rogābant (imperfect → secondary), governing two clauses — so two imperfect subjunctives. The word nunc, "now," does not drag the second clause into primary sequence.
Ego semper vigilāvī ut vōs tūtī essētis.
click to compare
"I have always kept watch so that you might be safe." Vigilāvī is perfect → secondary → essētis, imperfect subjunctive, 2nd plural to agree with vōs. Tūtī is nominative plural masculine, agreeing with vōs.
Cōnsulēs semper vigilābunt ut nēmō rem pūblicam dēleat.
click to compare
"The consuls will always keep watch so that no one may destroy the republic." Vigilābunt is future — and future is primary, so dēleat is present subjunctive. If your instinct wrote dēlēret, that instinct was English, not Latin.
🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · TWO ROOMS, TWO SPEECHES

There are four Catilinarian speeches, and they are not four drafts of one thing. Each was delivered to a particular audience at a particular hour of a fast-moving crisis, and Cicero built each one for the people in front of him.

In Catilīnam I — the one you met in Module 6, with Quō ūsque tandem… — was delivered in the Senate, in November of 63 BC, with Catiline himself sitting in the room. Senators already knew the details. What they needed was constitutional argument: what a consul was entitled to do, what precedent allowed, why acting now was lawful rather than tyrannical. Cicero addressed them the way a Roman always addressed the Senate: patrēs cōnscrīptī, "conscript fathers."

In Catilīnam II came the next day, and to a completely different audience: the Roman people, in a public assembly — a cōntiō. Ordinary Romans knew only that something alarming was happening and that a well-connected man had left town in a hurry. They did not need constitutional theory. They needed three things: an explanation, reassurance, and a consul who looked like he had it under control. So Cicero calls them Quirītēs, "Citizens" — the address you use for the people, not the Senate — and he opens not with a legal argument but with a burst of four verbs.

Abiit, excessit, ēvāsit, ērūpit. He could have said abiit and stopped. Instead each verb escalates: went away, withdrew, slipped out, burst out — and by the fourth the enemy sounds less like a politician leaving town and more like something that broke loose. That is a crowd being handed a feeling, not an argument. And every one of the four is perfect indicative: Cicero is not hedging or supposing. He is telling them what is true.

The Roman word for it: admīrātiō. Cicero wants the crowd to feel astonishment — and then to feel that the astonishing thing was managed by their consul while they slept. Watch how the grammar carries that. When he accounts for the past, his main verbs are perfect and the clauses under them are imperfect subjunctive. When he pivots to now and next, the main verbs go present and future and the subjunctives follow. Sequence of tenses is not a decoration on this speech; it is the instrument by which Cicero moves an anxious crowd from what happened to what will be done.

🧠 MODULE 7 IN FIVE LINES
LessonThe one thing to keep
7.01A subordinate subjunctive's tense is governed by the main verb, not chosen. That is the sequence of tenses.
7.02Present or future main verb → primary → present subjunctive. Future counts as not-past.
7.03Imperfect, perfect, or pluperfect main verb → secondary → imperfect subjunctive (present infinitive + -m, -s, -t…).
7.04Two questions, in order: past or not-past? → which subjunctive? The clause's own subject fixes person and number.
7.05In real prose the sequence tracks the speaker's stance — and an indicative subordinate clause is outside the rule entirely.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

In a Latin subordinate clause, tense is not a clock reading — it is a statement about relationship. Find the main verb, ask past or not-past, and the subjunctive is decided. Cicero used that machinery to walk a nervous crowd from what had happened to what he would do next, and you can now watch him do it.
