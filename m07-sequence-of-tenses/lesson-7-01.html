Who Decides the Tense?

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You can build the forms. Now: which one does Latin actually want?
Last module you learned to make the present and imperfect subjunctive. This module you learn something stranger: when a subjunctive sits in a subordinate clause, you do not get to pick its tense. The main verb picks it for you. Latin calls this the sequence of tenses, and it means time inside a Latin sentence is measured by relationship, not by the clock.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what "sequence of tenses" means and why a subordinate subjunctive's tense is not a free choice.
2
I will be able to sort a main verb into primary or secondary sequence by looking only at its tense.
3
I will be able to name which subjunctive tense each sequence calls for — and say honestly which part of the table is not mine yet.

📚 PART 2 · THE PROBLEM, IN TWO SENTENCES

Here are two Latin sentences. Everything is the same except the first verb — and yet the last verb changes too. Look at what moves.

MAIN VERB IS PRESENT
Cicerō vigilat ut cīvēs tūtī sint.
"Cicero keeps watch so that the citizens may be safe."
sint = present subjunctive
MAIN VERB IS PAST
Cicerō vigilābat ut cīvēs tūtī essent.
"Cicero was keeping watch so that the citizens might be safe."
essent = imperfect subjunctive

Notice what did not happen. The meaning of the ut clause did not change — both times the citizens are simply "safe." No new information arrived. The only thing that changed was the main verb, and the subjunctive followed it like a shadow.

That following is the rule. The tense of a subjunctive in a subordinate clause is governed by the tense of the main verb. Latin calls this the sequence of tenses — cōnsecūtiō temporum.

📚 PART 3 · THE RULE: TWO SEQUENCES, TWO TENSES

Every main verb in Latin falls into one of two camps. The camp has a name, and the name tells you which subjunctive to use.

If the MAIN verb is…the sequence is…then the subordinate subjunctive is…
present or future
vigilat, vigilābit, rogat, rogābit		PRIMARY
(also called "present" sequence)		PRESENT subjunctive
sit, sint, amet, regat, audiat, loquātur
imperfect, perfect, or pluperfect
vigilābat, vigilāvit, vigilāverat		SECONDARY
(also called "historic" sequence)		IMPERFECT subjunctive
esset, essent, amāret, regeret, audīret, loquerētur
💡 The one that catches everybody. A future main verb is primary, not secondary. Vigilābit ut cīvēs tūtī sint — "he will keep watch so that the citizens may be safe." It feels like it should reach for something other than the present, because in English we say "so that they would be safe." Latin does not care. Future is a non-past tense, so it lives in the primary camp with the present.
✍️ "Historic" is the better nickname. Secondary sequence is the sequence of narrative — of things told as history. Sallust's narration runs almost entirely in secondary sequence, because he is telling you what happened. Cicero, arguing live in front of a crowd, keeps sliding into primary, because he is talking about now. Once you know the two camps, you can hear an author's stance from his subjunctives.

★ ADMĪRĀTIŌ · WONDER

Admīrātiō is built from ad- ("toward") and mīror ("to wonder at") — the deponent you met in Module 2. It is the act of turning toward something and finding it larger than you expected. It gives us admire, marvel, miracle, and — through the same root — mirror.

Here is what is worth wondering at today. You have spent years assuming that a verb's tense reports when something happened, measured against now. Sequence of tenses says: not in a subordinate clause. There, tense reports a relationship — this action stands beside that one. Latin built a grammar in which time is relative before anyone had a word for relativity. When a rule like that stops looking arbitrary and starts looking designed, that shift is admīrātiō, and it is the whole reason to study an old language carefully.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

In a subordinate clause you do not choose the subjunctive's tense — the main verb chooses it. Present or future main verb → primary → present subjunctive. Imperfect, perfect, or pluperfect main verb → secondary → imperfect subjunctive. Two rows, and they are enough until Module 13.
