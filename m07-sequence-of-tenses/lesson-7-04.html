📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two questions, asked in the same order, every single time.
You now know both halves of the rule. What you need next is a routine — a fixed pair of questions you run without thinking about it, so that sequence stops costing you attention and you can spend that attention on the sentence's meaning instead. Today you drill the routine, meet three cases that trip people up, and unlock Lūdus VII.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to run the two-step sequence check on any main verb without looking at a table.
2
I will be able to find the real main verb even when the subordinate clause comes first, or when one main verb governs two clauses.
3
I will be able to say why an indicative subordinate clause is not governed by this rule at all.
📚 PART 1 · THE ROUTINE
1
Find the main verb. Ask: is its tense past, or not?
Present or future → primary. Imperfect, perfect, or pluperfect → secondary. This is one yes-or-no question, and it decides everything else.
2
Take the matching subjunctive.
Primary → present subjunctive. Secondary → imperfect subjunctive. Then make it agree in person and number with its own subject — which may not be the subject of the main verb.
Worked example. Cōnsul custōdiās posuit nē coniūrātī portās aperīrent.
Step 1: main verb posuit — perfect → past → secondary. Step 2: secondary → imperfect subjunctive; subject of the clause is coniūrātī, plural → aperīrent, not aperīret. "The consul posted guards so that the conspirators might not open the gates."
💡 Notice the division of labour. The sequence rule decides the tense. The subject of the clause decides the person and number. Two separate decisions, made from two separate pieces of evidence. Students who mix them up end up matching the subjunctive to the wrong subject.

📚 PART 3 · THREE CASES THAT TRIP PEOPLE UP

1. The main verb is not always first

Latin puts the subordinate clause first whenever it likes. Find the verb of the main clause, not the first verb your eye lands on.

Cum Catilīna in senātū sedēret, Cicerō surrēxit.
"When Catiline was sitting in the Senate, Cicero stood up." (cum + subjunctive — container glossed, Module 10)
The main verb is surrēxit, at the very end. Perfect → secondary → sedēret, imperfect subjunctive.

2. One main verb can govern two clauses

When it does, both clauses follow the same sequence. The main verb does not get tired.

Populus rogābat cūr Catilīna discēderet et quid nunc ageret.
"The people were asking why Catiline was leaving and what he was now doing."
One main verb, rogābat (imperfect → secondary), so two imperfect subjunctives.

3. An indicative subordinate clause is simply not in the game

Sequence of tenses governs subjunctives. A subordinate clause with an indicative verb states a fact on its own authority and picks its tense freely.

Catilīna discessit, quia in urbe manēre nōn poterat.
"Catiline left, because he was not able to stay in the city."
Poterat is imperfect indicative. Quia takes the indicative, so no sequence question arises. Check the mood before you check the sequence.
✍️ An honest footnote for careful readers. Roman authors sometimes break sequence on purpose: in the middle of a past narrative they will slip into primary sequence to make a scene feel present and immediate. Latin grammarians call the effect repraesentātiō. If you meet a present subjunctive after a past main verb in real Cicero, that is probably what you are looking at — and it is a deliberate choice, not sloppiness. You will not be tested on it, and you should not do it in your own Latin this module. Follow the rule; recognize the exception when an author earns it.

🎮 PART 5 · LŪDUS VII — ŌRDŌ TEMPORUM

"The Order of Tenses." Seventy-five seconds, two choices per card, and one job: decide fast. Cards come in four kinds — name the sequence from a main verb, name the subjunctive tense the sequence calls for, pick the correctly sequenced form in a sentence, and tell subjunctive from indicative where a single macron decides it.

🎯 How it is graded: best of unlimited attempts. Play until the two-step routine happens without you narrating it to yourself — that automatic feeling is the whole point, and it is what makes Friday's reading readable at speed.
✍️ One rule for the game. No card in Ōrdō Temporum uses a perfect or pluperfect subjunctive, because you have not built those. If a card asks for "the subjunctive tense," the honest answer is always present or imperfect.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Two questions, always in this order: is the main verb past or not-past? → take the imperfect or the present subjunctive. Then let the clause's own subject fix the person and number. Everything else — word order, how many clauses, where the main verb sits — is noise around those two questions.
