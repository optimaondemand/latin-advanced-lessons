Lesson 13.03
Passive, Deponent, and Esse
Lesson · PPP + sim, PPP + essem, and this module's vocabulary
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday's warning was that a form can hide its mood. Today's relief is that the passive never does.
Lessons 13.01 and 13.02 built the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive active — one word each, and the perfect one dangerously like a future perfect indicative. Today you build the same two tenses in the passive, and the passive is made of two words: a participle plus a form of sum. That extra word turns out to be a gift. It carries the mood openly, so nothing in the passive collides with anything. Then the deponents, which use exactly these forms and mean the opposite of what they look like — as they have since Module 2 — and this module's vocabulary.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to build the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive passive of any verb as PPP + sim or PPP + essem, with the participle agreeing correctly.
2
I will be able to tell a two-word subjunctive from a two-word indicative by looking at the helper word alone.
3
I will be able to form and translate the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive of a deponent or semi-deponent verb, keeping the meaning active.
📚 PART 1 · YOU HAVE DONE THIS BEFORE, IN THE INDICATIVE

Since 0706010 you have known that Latin cannot make a completed passive out of one word. It uses the perfect passive participle — the fourth principal part, turned into an adjective — plus a form of sum:

Indicative you already ownBuilt fromMeaning
captus estPPP + present of sumperfect passive — "he has been captured, he was captured"
captus eratPPP + imperfect of sumpluperfect passive — "he had been captured"

The subjunctive does the identical thing. Keep the participle exactly as it is and swap the helper word for the subjunctive of sum — which you have owned since Module 6:

Perfect subjunctive passive = PPP + sim, sīs, sit, sīmus, sītis, sint
Pluperfect subjunctive passive = PPP + essem, essēs, esset, essēmus, essētis, essent
Helper wordIts tense & moodSo the whole form isMeaning
estpresent indicativeperfect passive indicative"was captured"
sitpresent subjunctiveperfect passive subjunctive"had been captured" — in a subjunctive clause
eratimperfect indicativepluperfect passive indicative"had been captured"
essetimperfect subjunctivepluperfect passive subjunctive"had been captured" — in a subjunctive clause
🎉 The best news since the pluperfect. Look at the helper words: est and sit are different words. Erat and esset are different words. There is no collision anywhere in the passive — not identical forms, not even a macron-only difference. Yesterday's whole problem was that amāverit could be two moods. Captus sit can only ever be one. The two-word passive wears its mood on the outside.
📚 PART 2 · THE FULL PARADIGMS — AND THE PARTICIPLE AGREES

Here is capiō, capere, cēpī, captum right through. The participle is an adjective, so it agrees with the subject in gender, number and case — nominative, because the subject of the clause is nominative. Only the helper word changes for person.

Perfect subjunctive passivePluperfect subjunctive passive
1 sg.captus, capta, captum simcaptus, capta, captum essem
2 sg.captus, capta, captum sīscaptus, capta, captum essēs
3 sg.captus, capta, captum sitcaptus, capta, captum esset
1 pl.captī, captae, capta sīmuscaptī, captae, capta essēmus
2 pl.captī, captae, capta sītiscaptī, captae, capta essētis
3 pl.captī, captae, capta sintcaptī, captae, capta essent
✍️ The macrons are not decoration here either. Sim but sīs; sit but sītis; essem but essēs; esset but essētis. That is the same long-before-s/-mus/-tis, short-before--m/-t/-nt rule you applied to amem/amēs, amārem/amārēs, amāverim/amāverīs and amāvissem/amāvissēs. Five tenses, one rule, and this module is where the rule finally pays for itself — because in the perfect subjunctive active the macron is the only thing separating amāverīs (subjunctive) from amāveris (future perfect indicative). Write it and you have said which mood you meant.

All four conjugations, 3rd singular, so you can see that the only thing that changes is the participle:

Now watch the agreement do real work. Same clause, three different subjects:

Cicerō rogat cūr coniūrātus captus sit. — "Cicero asks why the conspirator has been arrested." (masculine singular)
Cicerō rogat cūr coniūrātī captī sint. — "…why the conspirators have been arrested." (masculine plural — the helper moves to sint too)
Cicerō rogat cūr epistula lēcta sit. — "…why the letter has been read." (feminine singular: lēcta, not lēctus)
Cicerō rogat cūr haec facta sint. — "…why these things have been done." (neuter plural)
⚠️ The error to guard against. Students get the helper word right and freeze the participle at -us. Epistula lēctus sit is not Latin: epistula is feminine, so the participle must be lēcta. Two words means two things to get right, and the participle is the one people forget. Say the subject's gender out loud before you write the participle.
✍️ One honest footnote, and then forget it. Roman authors sometimes write captus fuerit and captus fuisset instead of captus sit and captus esset — the same idea, built with the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive of sum rather than the present and imperfect. It is real, it is uncommon, and this course never asks you to produce it. If you meet one in outside reading, translate it the same way and move on.
📚 PART 3 · DEPONENTS: THE SAME FORMS, THE OPPOSITE MEANING

A deponent verb has passive forms and active meaning. That was Module 2, and nothing about it changes here. Its third principal part is already a two-word form — locūtus sum, "I spoke" — so its perfect and pluperfect subjunctive are built by swapping the helper word, exactly as in Part 1:

DeponentPerfect subj.Pluperfect subj.Means
loquor, loquī, locūtus sum — to speaklocūtus sitlocūtus esset"he had spoken" — active
sequor, sequī, secūtus sum — to followsecūtus sitsecūtus esset"he had followed"
proficīscor, proficīscī, profectus sum — to set outprofectus sitprofectus esset"he had set out"
cōnor, cōnārī, cōnātus sum — to trycōnātus sitcōnātus esset"he had tried"
morior, morī, mortuus sum — to diemortuus sitmortuus esset"he had died"
patior, patī, passus sum — to suffer, allowpassus sitpassus esset"he had suffered"
vereor, verērī, veritus sum — to fearveritus sitveritus esset"he had feared"
cōnfiteor, cōnfitērī, cōnfessus sum — to confesscōnfessus sitcōnfessus esset"he had confessed"
💡 Why this is easier than it looks. You do not have to decide whether a two-word form is passive or deponent. The verb's dictionary entry already told you: if the first principal part ends in -or or -eor, the verb is deponent and everything it does is active in meaning. Captus sit is a real passive because capiō is a real active verb. Cōnātus sit is active in meaning because cōnor has no active forms to be passive against.

And now the small surprise of the lesson — the semi-deponents from Module 3. Audeō, audēre, ausus sum is active in the present system and deponent in the perfect system. Which means its four subjunctive tenses are two one-word forms and two two-word forms:

Semi-deponentPresent subj.Imperfect subj.Perfect subj.Pluperfect subj.

gaudeō, gaudēre, gāvīsus sum — to rejoicegaudeatgaudēretgāvīsus sitgāvīsus esset

So the same verb changes shape halfway down the column: Rogāvit num Catilīna hoc audēret — "he asked whether Catiline was daring this" — but Rogāvit num Catilīna hoc ausus esset — "he asked whether Catiline had dared this." Module 3 told you semi-deponents are "exactly what they are, with no false form." This is where that honesty costs you an extra word.

📚 PART 4 · AND ESSE ITSELF

The word esse is in this lesson's title doing two separate jobs, and it is worth separating them.

1
Sum as the helper word. That is Parts 1–3: the sit in captus sit, the esset in locūtus esset. Present and imperfect subjunctive of sum, doing service for another verb.
2
Sum as itself. Sum has no passive — nothing can "be been" — so its own perfect and pluperfect subjunctive are single words on the perfect stem fu-, which you built in 13.01 and 13.02: fuerim and fuissem.
Tensesum — all six personsSample
present subj.sim, sīs, sit, sīmus, sītis, sintRogat ubi Catilīna sit. — "…where he is."
imperfect subj.essem, essēs, esset, essēmus, essētis, essentRogāvit ubi Catilīna esset. — "…where he was."
perfect subj.fuerim, fuerīs, fuerit, fuerīmus, fuerītis, fuerintRogat ubi Catilīna fuerit. — "…where he has been."
pluperfect subj.fuissem, fuissēs, fuisset, fuissēmus, fuissētis, fuissentRogāvit ubi Catilīna fuisset. — "…where he had been."
💡 Read that table twice. The first two rows are the words you will spend all week attaching to participles. The last two rows are what happens when sum is the verb of its own clause. So captus esset and fuisset are both pluperfect subjunctives — one is capiō borrowing sum, the other is sum on its own account. Knowing which is which is just knowing what the verb of the clause is.

📖 PART 6 · VOCABULARY FOR THIS MODULE

Module 13 is the module of looking back and passing judgment, and its words follow. Notice how many are deponent — that is not a coincidence. The verbs Romans used for confessing, avenging and forgetting are almost all deponent, so every one of them turns up in this week's work as a two-word subjunctive.

EntryMeaning & note
iūdicō, iūdicāre, iūdicāvī, iūdicātumto judge, decide, form a judgment — iūdicātum sit, "it has been judged"
pūniō, pūnīre, pūnīvī, pūnītumto punish — 4th conjugation, so the PPP is pūnītus
cōnservō, cōnservāre, cōnservāvī, cōnservātumto preserve, save, keep safe — Cicero's own word for what he did in 63 BC
necō, necāre, necāvī, necātumto kill, put to death
iūrō, iūrāre, iūrāvī, iūrātumto swear, take an oath
vetō, vetāre, vetuī, vetitumto forbid — takes an accusative + infinitive: eum venīre vetuit, "he forbade him to come"
cōnfiteor, cōnfitērī, cōnfessus sum (dep.)to confess, admit, own up to
ulcīscor, ulcīscī, ultus sum (dep.)to avenge, take vengeance on, punish
oblīvīscor, oblīvīscī, oblītus sum (dep.)to forget — takes the genitive of what is forgotten: oblītus est perīculī
factum, factī n.a deed, an act, a thing done — literally the PPP of faciō used as a noun
iūdicium, iūdiciī n.a judgment; a trial, a court — sine iūdiciō, "without a trial"

💡 Etymology minute. Iūdicium gives English judicial, judiciary and prejudice — a pre-judgment, made before the trial. Auctōritās gives authority, and behind it sits auctor, the person who starts a thing, which is also where author comes from. Oblīvīscor gives oblivion and oblivious. Factum survives almost unchanged as fact, and more obviously in the legal phrase de facto — "by the thing actually done," as opposed to de iure, "by law." Those two Latin phrases are the whole Module 13 reading in miniature, and you will meet the argument on Friday.
🃏 FLIP CARDS · SAY THE FORM AND THE MEANING BEFORE YOU CLICK
cōnfessus sit
form? meaning?
Perfect subjunctive of the deponent cōnfiteor. "He has confessed" — active. Primary sequence, finished column.
pūnītī essent
form? meaning?
Pluperfect subjunctive passive, 3rd plural, masculine. "They had been punished." Real passive — pūniō is an ordinary active verb.
cōnservāta sit
what gender, and why?
Perfect subjunctive passive, feminine singular — because its subject is something like rēs pūblica or urbs. "It has been saved."
ultus esset
deponent or passive?
Deponent. Ulcīscor, ulcīscī, ultus sum — "he had avenged," active. It is not "he had been avenged."
factum esset
form? meaning?
Pluperfect subjunctive passive, neuter singular. "It had been done." The single most useful form in this module — keep it.
oblītus sit
and what case follows it?
Perfect subjunctive of the deponent oblīvīscor: "he has forgotten." What is forgotten goes in the genitive.
vetuisset
one word — why?
Pluperfect subjunctive active of vetō. An ordinary active verb, so the active tenses are one word: perfect stem vetu- + -isset.
iūrāvisset vs iūrātum esset
what is the difference?
Both pluperfect subjunctive. The first is active — "he had sworn." The second is passive — "it had been sworn." One verb, two voices, both in the same cell of the table.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

The helper word carries the mood; the participle carries the completion and the agreement. Captus est is indicative, captus sit is subjunctive, and nothing in the passive is ambiguous. Deponents use these same two-word forms and keep their active meaning. Semi-deponents switch from one word to two exactly halfway down the column.
