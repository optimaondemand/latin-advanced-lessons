The Easiest Form, and the Most Dangerous
Lesson · the pluperfect subjunctive, and one honest collision
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two jobs today: one that takes ninety seconds, and one that takes real care.
The pluperfect subjunctive is the easiest tense in the Latin verb system — you already know the rule and just have not been told that it applies. Then, having finished the easy half of the lesson, you will look straight at the one place where Latin genuinely does not tell you what mood it is in: amāverit is both a perfect subjunctive and a future perfect indicative. That is not a mistake in your textbook. It is the language. You will learn a test that works anyway.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to form the pluperfect subjunctive active for any verb in Latin, including sum and possum, from one rule.
2
I will be able to say exactly which persons of the perfect subjunctive and future perfect indicative are identical, which differ only by a macron, and which differ outright.
3
I will be able to decide which mood I am reading by looking at the clause, not at the word.
📚 PART 1 · THE NINETY-SECOND TENSE
Pluperfect subjunctive active = the perfect active infinitive + -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt.

You have already met that rule — in Lesson 6.03, where the present infinitive plus the same endings gave you the imperfect subjunctive. Latin does the identical trick twice:

Take this infinitiveAdd the endingsand you get
present infinitive — amāre-m, -s, -t…amārem — imperfect subjunctive (Module 6)
perfect infinitive — amāvisse-m, -s, -t…amāvissem — pluperfect subjunctive (today)

Where does the perfect infinitive come from? Perfect stem + -isse. So amāv- gives amāvisse, and amāvisse gives amāvissem. If you prefer to skip the middle step, the shortcut is: perfect stem + -issem, -issēs, -isset, -issēmus, -issētis, -issent.

✍️ Same macron rule as always. Long ē before -s, -mus, -tis; short e before -m, -t, -nt. Amāvissem but amāvissēs; amāvisset but amāvissētis. You have now applied that one pattern to four different subjunctive tenses.

And once again, the irregular verbs simply obey:

VerbPerfect infinitivePluperfect subjunctive
sum, esse, fuīfuissefuissem, fuissēs, fuisset, fuissēmus, fuissētis, fuissent
possum, posse, potuīpotuissepotuissem, potuissēs, potuisset, potuissēmus, potuissētis, potuissent
dīcō, dīcere, dīxīdīxissedīxissem, dīxissēs, dīxisset…
faciō, facere, fēcīfēcissefēcissem, fēcissēs, fēcisset…
🎉 The best news in the module. Nothing else in the Latin verb system ends in -issem, -issēs, -isset, -issēmus, -issētis, -issent. The pluperfect indicative is amāveram, amāverās, amāverat — nowhere near it. So the pluperfect subjunctive is completely unambiguous: if you see -isset or -issent, you have found a pluperfect subjunctive, full stop. Hold on to that, because the next section is not like this.
📚 PART 2 · THE COLLISION, STATED HONESTLY

Here is the perfect subjunctive you built yesterday, set beside the future perfect indicative you have known since 0706010. Same verb, same six persons. Read the right-hand column before you read anything else.

PersonPerfect subjunctiveFuture perfect indicativeHow different?

⚠️ And now the part nobody tells you. Romans did not write macrons. Not on stone, not on papyrus, not in any manuscript. Macrons are a modern teaching aid, added by editors so that you can hear the word correctly. So in a real Latin text, the "macron only" rows are identical too. Five of the six persons of these two tenses are indistinguishable on the page. Latin lived with that for a thousand years without confusion, because Latin readers were not looking at the word. They were looking at the clause.
📚 PART 3 · THE TEST THAT ACTUALLY WORKS
Do not ask "what form is this?" Ask "what kind of clause am I in?" The clause decides the mood, and then the mood tells you what the form must be.

You already own the whole list. Every construction below requires a subjunctive, so a -erit inside one of them is a perfect subjunctive, no argument:

If the clause is introduced by…the construction is…and the mood is…
ut / nē after a verb of intending or orderingpurpose or indirect command (m08)subjunctive
ut / ut nōn after tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō, sīc, itaresult (m09)subjunctive
cum meaning "when / since / although" in narrativecum clause (m10)subjunctive
a question word (quid, cūr, ubi, quam, num, utrum) after a verb of asking, knowing, or wonderingindirect question (m11)subjunctive
nē / ut after timeō, vereor, metuōfear clause (m12)subjunctive

A future perfect indicative turns up somewhere quite different: in a main clause, or in a plain time clause pointing forward, where the main verb is future. Watch the same six letters do two jobs:

FUTURE PERFECT INDICATIVE
Cum Catilīna ex urbe discesserit, cīvēs tūtī erunt.
"When Catiline has left the city, the citizens will be safe."
The main verb is future (erunt) and the cum clause is pure clock-time, pointing forward. No narrative, no cause, no concession — so cum takes the indicative, exactly as Module 10 taught you.
PERFECT SUBJUNCTIVE
Cicerō rogat cūr Catilīna ex urbe discesserit.
"Cicero asks why Catiline has left the city."
Cūr after rogat is an indirect question, and an indirect question is always subjunctive. Primary sequence, finished action — top-right cell.
💡 A second clue, when the clause type is genuinely unclear: look at the main verb's tense. A future perfect indicative is describing something that will be finished in the future, so the main verb is normally future or future perfect. A perfect subjunctive in primary sequence answers a present or future main verb but reports something already over. If the main verb is rogat, sciō, mīrantur — verbs of asking and knowing — you are in an indirect question, and you are in the subjunctive.
✍️ What this means for your own writing. When you write Latin for this course, you know which mood you intend, so write the macrons that belong to it: amāverīs for the subjunctive, amāveris for the future perfect. On the module quiz, macron-free spellings are accepted — but if you type a macron, type the right one.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Perfect infinitive + -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt gives the pluperfect subjunctive, and -isset can be nothing else in Latin. The perfect subjunctive is the risky one: -erit and -erint are shared with the future perfect indicative, and macrons do not exist in real texts. So read the clause, not the word — if it is a purpose, result, cum, indirect-question, or fear clause, it is subjunctive.
