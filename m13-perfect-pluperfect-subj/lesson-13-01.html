📚 PART 2 · WHAT "ALREADY FINISHED" ACTUALLY MEANS

Start in English, where you already feel this without thinking about it. Two questions, same speaker, same moment:

SAME TIME — still going on
He asks what Catiline is doing.
Rogat quid Catilīna faciat.
faciat = present subjunctive
ALREADY FINISHED — over and done
He asks what Catiline has done.
Rogat quid Catilīna fēcerit.
fēcerit = perfect subjunctive

The main verb did not move. Rogat is present in both, so both are primary sequence. What changed is whether the asking is about something still unfolding or about something over. That second question — the completed one — is the sentence you have not been able to write since Module 7. You can write it after today.

Sequence has two questions, not one. First: is the main verb past or non-past? That picks the row. Second: is the subordinate action still going on, or already finished? That picks the column.

📚 PART 3 · THE FORM: PERFECT STEM + -ERIM
Perfect subjunctive active = the perfect stem + -erim, -erīs, -erit, -erīmus, -erītis, -erint.

The perfect stem is the third principal part with its final -ī chopped off. You have been using it since 0706010 to build the perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect indicative. Nothing new here except where it is going next.

✍️ Where the macron goes — and you have seen this pattern twice already. The i of the ending is long before -s, -mus, -tis and short before -m, -t, -nt. So amāverim but amāverīs; amāverit but amāverītis. This is exactly the shortening you learned for amem / amēs in 6.01 and for amārem / amārēs in 6.03. One rule, three tenses.

Notice what did not happen: the conjugations stopped mattering. Once you are on the perfect stem there is no first-conjugation -ē-, no -a- for everybody else, no -i- in the io-stems. One set of endings, glued onto whatever stem the third principal part hands you. That includes the two verbs you meet most:

VerbPerfect stemPerfect subjunctive
sum, esse, fuī — to befu-fuerim, fuerīs, fuerit, fuerīmus, fuerītis, fuerint
possum, posse, potuī — to be ablepotu-potuerim, potuerīs, potuerit, potuerīmus, potuerītis, potuerint
💡 Compare the present subjunctive of sum: sim, sīs, sit… That one had to be memorised because there was nothing to derive it from. Fuerim is derived, like every other perfect subjunctive in Latin. The irregular verbs stop being irregular the moment you get onto the perfect stem — the same relief you got with essem in 6.03.
⚠️ One warning, then we leave it until tomorrow. Amāverit is also the future perfect indicative 3rd singular — the same six letters, a different mood. That collision is real, it is the single most confusing thing in this module, and it gets a whole section of Lesson 13.02. For today: do not panic when a form looks like two things. Note it and keep going.

★ CŌNSTANTIA · PERSEVERANCE

Cōnstantia is built from cōn- ("together") and stō ("to stand") — standing firm, staying put, holding a shape under pressure. Romans used it for the person who does not revise their principles every time the situation turns awkward. It gives us constant, constancy, and consistent.

This module is about the completed past, and that is where cōnstantia lives. A finished action cannot be edited. Whatever Cicero did in 63 BC, he did; whatever Catiline's soldiers did at Pistoria, they did. The perfect and pluperfect subjunctive are the forms Latin reaches for when it stops narrating and starts assessing — quid factum esset, "what had been done." And there is a smaller, closer cōnstantia in front of you today: you were promised these two tenses six modules ago and told to wait. You waited. The endings you are learning have been sitting there the whole time, unchanged, in exactly the shape the table said they would be. A language that keeps its promises is worth the patience it costs.
