Lesson 13.04
All Four Cells, No Labels
Lesson · the sequence grid, and the drill
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
This is the drill lesson. It should feel like a drill.
All four subjunctive tenses now exist for you, active and passive, one-word and two-word. Nothing new gets built today. Instead you learn to choose — because from now on every subordinate subjunctive in Latin is one of exactly four forms, and picking the right one is two questions with two answers each. Four cells, two yes-or-no decisions, done. Today is the grid, the procedure, and then a pile of sentences with the labels taken off.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to draw the four-cell sequence grid from memory and name the tense in every cell.
2
I will be able to pick the row from the main verb and the column from the English, in that order, without guessing.
3
I will be able to say which constructions really use all four cells and which ones only ever use two.
📚 PART 1 · THE GRID, COMPLETE

One verb, faciō, facere, fēcī, factum, in all four cells, with a whole sentence in each so you can hear the difference. The main verb is rogō throughout, and the only thing changing is its tense and the English inside the clause.

The row is about the main verb. The column is about the subordinate action. The two questions are independent, which is why there are four answers and not three.

And the same grid with the passive and deponent forms from yesterday dropped in, so that you can see the whole thing at once. Same verb for the passive (faciō → factum), and loquor for the deponent:

✍️ Two things to notice in that second table. First: the finished row is always two words in the passive and always one word in the active. That is a free clue when you are reading. Second: faciō is the one verb whose present-system passive is irregular — Latin uses fīō for it, which is Module 15, so the two greyed cells are not yours yet. Every other verb in the language behaves normally: capiātur, caperētur, captus sit, captus esset. Use capiō as your model verb until Module 15 fills the gap.
⚠️ Macrons, one last time, because this is the lesson where you choose forms. The perfect subjunctive is fēcerim, fēcerīs, fēcerit — long ī before -s, -mus, -tis. The future perfect indicative is fēcerō, fēceris, fēcerit — short i throughout. In 2nd singular, 1st plural and 2nd plural the macron is the only difference between the two tenses: fēcerīs is a subjunctive, fēceris is an indicative. When you write, you are choosing a cell of this grid, so write the macron that says so.
📚 PART 2 · THE PROCEDURE — THREE STEPS, IN THIS ORDER

Step 1 is free: you read a tense off the page. Step 2 costs a moment's thought about meaning. Step 3 is pure mechanics. Notice that you never have to think about which construction the clause is — the grid does not care whether it is an indirect question, a cum clause or a fear clause.

1
What tense is the MAIN verb? That picks the row.
Present or future → PRIMARY, the top row.
Imperfect, perfect or pluperfect → SECONDARY, the bottom row.
Two rows, not three. There has never been a third.
2
Was the subordinate action still going on, or already over? That picks the column.
Still going on at the moment of the main verb → SAME TIME, the left column.
Finished before the main verb → ALREADY FINISHED, the right column.
The English usually tells you: see Part 3.
3
Read the cell, then build the form.
Present → present stem + the vowel change. Imperfect → present infinitive + -m, -s, -t. Perfect → perfect stem + -erim. Pluperfect → perfect stem + -issem. And if the verb is passive or deponent, the last two cells become PPP + sim and PPP + essem, with the participle agreeing.
Worked example. "The consul did not know whether the letters had been read."
1. Main verb "did not know" = nesciēbat, imperfect → secondary, bottom row.
2. "had been read" is over before the not-knowing → finished, right column.
3. Bottom right = pluperfect subjunctive. The verb is passive, so PPP + essem: legō, legere, lēgī, lēctum, subject epistulae (feminine plural) → lēctae essent.
Cōnsul nesciēbat num epistulae lēctae essent.
💡 Do the steps in order and step 2 gets easier. Students who try to decide the tense in one move end up matching Latin tense to English tense, which does not work — "had been read" is a pluperfect in English and a pluperfect subjunctive in Latin here, but "he asked what he did" is a simple past in English and a pluperfect subjunctive in Latin. The grid is about relative time: earlier than the main verb, or alongside it. English tense names are a clue, not the rule.
📚 PART 3 · WHAT THE ENGLISH IS TELLING YOU

Step 2 is where the marks are lost, so here is the English side of it laid out flat. Read the clause inside the reported bit — not the main verb, which you already used for step 1.

If the English inside the clause says…the column is…Example
is doing · is being done · doessame timeRogat quid faciat. — "He asks what he is doing."
was doing · was being donesame timeRogāvit quid faceret. — "He asked what he was doing."
has done · has been donefinishedRogat quid fēcerit. — "He asks what he has done."
had done · had been donefinishedRogāvit quid fēcisset. — "He asked what he had done."
did · was done (the tricky one)finishedRogāvit quid fēcisset. — "He asked what he did." English "did" here means "had done."
⚠️ The one English shape the grid cannot handle: "would." "He asked what Cicero would do" points forward from the main verb, and none of these four cells does that. Latin has a way of saying it — a future participle plus a form of esse — and this course does not teach it. If an English sentence you are translating says "would" or "was going to," rewrite the English into something the grid can say ("what Cicero was doing," "what Cicero had done") and note in the margin what you changed. Never invent a form to fill a cell that is not there.
🧠 PART 4 · THE UNLABELLED PILE

Six sentences. Nobody tells you the construction, nobody tells you the cell. You get a main verb, an English meaning, and the principal parts. Run the three steps.

📚 PART 5 · NOT EVERY CONSTRUCTION USES ALL FOUR

Row 4 of the pile hid a real fact about Latin, and it is worth stating plainly. The grid describes what the four tenses can do. What a given construction actually needs depends on whether it looks forward or backward.

ConstructionCells it usesWhy
Indirect question m11all fourYou can report a question about now, or about what has already happened. Both are ordinary.
Cum clause m10all fourNarrative cum lives on the finished column: cum haec dīxisset, "when he had said this." This is the commonest pluperfect subjunctive in Latin.
Fear clause m12all fourYou can fear that something is happening or that it has already happened — often the worse fear of the two.
Purpose clause · indirect command m08same-time column only (present or imperfect)A purpose has not happened yet when the main action is taken. There is nothing for it to be finished before.
Result clause m09usually same-timeNormally follows sequence like everything else. Roman authors sometimes put a perfect subjunctive after a past main verb to stress that the result actually came about — you may meet it; you are never asked to write it.
🎉 Why this makes your life easier, not harder. If you are staring at an ut clause after a verb of intending, you do not have four choices — you have two, and step 1 decides between them. The four-cell decision only really bites on indirect questions, cum clauses and fear clauses. Those are also exactly the three constructions in Friday's reading.

rogat + "has done"
which cell?
Primary, finished → perfect subjunctive. Rogat quid fēcerit.
rogāvit + "was doing"
which cell?
Secondary, same time → imperfect subjunctive. Rogāvit quid faceret.
rogāvit + "had done"
which cell?
Secondary, finished → pluperfect subjunctive. Rogāvit quid fēcisset.
rogābit + "has done"
careful with the row
Primary, finished → perfect subjunctive. Future is primary. Rogābit quid fēcerit.
rogāverat + "had done"
careful with the row
Secondary, finished → pluperfect subjunctive. A pluperfect main verb is just another past tense. Rogāverat quid fēcisset.
timet + "has been captured"
passive — how many words?
Primary, finished, passive → perfect subjunctive passive. Timet nē captus sit. Two words.
timēbat + "had been captured"
passive — how many words?
Secondary, finished, passive → pluperfect subjunctive passive. Timēbat nē captus esset. Two words.
vēnit + ut + "to see"
how many cells are even available?
Purpose → same-time column only. Secondary sequence, so imperfect subjunctive: vēnit ut vidēret. There is no finished option here at all.
rogāvit + "would do"
trap
No cell. "Would do" points forward from the main verb, and the grid has no forward column. Recast the English; do not invent a form.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Main verb picks the row. Subordinate action picks the column. Read the cell. Present or future is the top row; imperfect, perfect or pluperfect is the bottom row. Still going on is the left column; already over is the right column. Two questions, four answers, and every subordinate subjunctive in Latin is in there somewhere.
