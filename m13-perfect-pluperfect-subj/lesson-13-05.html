📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The story is over. Now comes the part that lasted twenty years: arguing about it.
Every reading this semester has moved the conspiracy forward. Today's does not move anything — it looks back. Sallust stands on the battlefield at Pistoria and describes what he found there. Cicero, in the last days of his consulship, is asked to account for what he did in December and answers in one sentence. Both men are talking about actions that are over, which is why almost every subordinate verb in the passage sits in the right-hand column of your grid. The pluperfect subjunctive is not a rare form. It is the ordinary grammar of hindsight.

📚 PART 1 · BEFORE YOU READ
1. Two writers, two distances. Sallust wrote the Bellum Catilīnae roughly twenty years after the events, having left public life. Cicero was in the events, and he wrote and spoke about them for the rest of his life. So one of them is remembering and the other is defending. Both are using the completed past, and they are using it for opposite purposes.
2. What happened at Pistoria. In January 62 BC Catiline's army was caught in the ager Pistoriēnsis, in northern Etruria, and destroyed. Sallust reports that Catiline drew up his line in a narrow place, had the horses taken away so that everyone's danger would be equal, and fought on foot. Afterwards Catiline was found a long way in front of his own men, among the enemy dead, still faintly breathing. Module 12 gave you that battle; today's first six lines are Sallust writing it down.
3. What happened to Cicero. A consul leaving office normally addressed the people. At the end of December 63 BC the newly installed tribune Metellus Nepos blocked Cicero from doing so, permitting only the customary oath that a magistrate had obeyed the laws. Cicero swore something else instead. Lines 9–11 are that moment.
💡 What to watch for in the Latin. Count them as you go: seven pluperfect subjunctives, three perfect subjunctives, one imperfect subjunctive, and one indirect statement with a perfect infinitive from 0706010. Four cum clauses (m10), six indirect questions — one of them a double question with utrum…an (m11) — one fear clause (m12), and one ablative absolute. Three of the subjunctives are passive, two-word forms with the participle agreeing. This is the densest passage of the semester so far, and every construction in it is one you own.
📜 PART 2 · THE READING
Adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 59–61 (lines 1–6), and from Cicero's own later accounts of the end of his consulship — In Pisōnem 6 and Ad Familiārēs 5.2 (lines 7–12). This is an adaptation, not the Latin of either author: the sentences are ours, written to the grammar you have been taught, and the two writers have been set side by side in a way neither of them did.

1Postquam proelium ad Pistōriam factum est, Sallustius ipse dē eō diē scrīpsit.

2Cum Catilīna cōpiās suās in valle angustā īnstrūxisset, omnēs equīs remōtīs pedibus pugnāvērunt.

3Sallustius nārrat quantā virtūte Catilīna ipse tum pugnāverit.

4Catilīna enim longē ā suīs inter hostium cadāvera inventus est, paulum etiam spīrāns.

5Nōn dubitat igitur Sallustius dīcere quam ferōx animus eius fuerit.

6In extrēmō tamen librō nihil iūdicat: nārrat sōlum quid quisque fēcerit.

7Cicerō autem, cum adhūc in urbe manēret, saepe rogābātur quid ipse fēcisset.

8Nam quīdam cīvēs verēbantur nē coniūrātī sine iūdiciō necātī essent.

9Cum igitur Metellus tribūnus eum ōrātiōnem habēre vetuisset, Cicerō hoc ūnum iūrāvit:

10sē rem pūblicam urbemque cōnservāvisse.

11Populus autem, cum haec audīvisset, magnō clāmōre cōnsēnsit.

12Nēmō ignōrābat quid factum esset; sed multī adhūc rogābant utrum rēctē an contrā lēgem factum esset.

⚠️ Four honest notes about this passage. (1) Lines 2 and 4 follow Sallust closely in content — the narrow ground, the horses sent away, Catiline found far in front of his men among the enemy dead and still faintly breathing are all his — but the Latin is ours, simplified to your grammar. (2) Line 6 is a claim about the last chapter only. Sallust is severe about Catiline elsewhere in the book; what he declines to do is deliver a verdict at the end, where he simply describes the field. Do not read line 6 as "Sallust had no opinion." (3) The oath in lines 9–11 is real and Cicero told the story himself more than once, but his exact words that day are not preserved. Line 10 is our summary of what he swore, not a quotation. (4) Line 12 is the adaptation's own framing of the argument that followed — it is not a sentence from either author.

🏛️ THE ROMAN WORLD · THE OATH, AND THE TWENTY YEARS AFTER IT

A Roman consul stepping down at the end of December swore a short formal oath and then, by custom, spoke to the people about his year. Cicero never got the speech. Metellus Nepos, who had just taken up a tribuneship, used a tribune's power of veto to allow the oath and nothing else. Cicero swore — and instead of the ordinary formula he swore that the republic and the city had been saved. He records that the whole assembly shouted the same thing back at him, and he was still telling the story years later. It is a small scene, and it is the shape of his whole later life: a man who could not stop having to say, in the perfect and pluperfect, what he had done.

The argument outlasted the oath. Five years on, in 58 BC, the tribune Clodius carried a measure aimed at anyone who had put a Roman citizen to death without trial; Cicero left Italy rather than face it, and came back the following year. So quid factum esset was not a grammar exercise for these people. It was the question a generation could not settle: whether the executions of 5 December had saved the state or broken its own law. Notice how differently the two writers handle it. Cicero insists on the verdict. Sallust ends his book on a battlefield, describing what he found and refusing to sum up. Both reach for the completed past — and cōnstantia, this module's virtue, turns out to mean two different things: standing by your judgment, and standing by the facts when you will not judge.

C · EVERYTHING ELSE WORTH KEEPING
PieceWhat to remember
The macron rule
13.01–13.03		Long vowel before -s, -mus, -tis; short before -m, -t, -nt. amem/amēs · amārem/amārēs · amāverim/amāverīs · amāvissem/amāvissēs · sim/sīs · essem/essēs. One rule, every tense.
The one collision
13.02		Perfect subjunctive vs. future perfect indicative: amāverit and amāverint are identical; amāverīs / amāveris, amāverīmus / amāverimus, amāverītis / amāveritis differ by a macron only — and Romans wrote no macrons. Only amāverim vs. amāverō is safe. Read the clause, not the word.
The one safe form
13.02		Nothing else in Latin ends -issem, -issēs, -isset, -issēmus, -issētis, -issent. If you see -isset, you have a pluperfect subjunctive, full stop.
The passive never lies
13.03		captus est / captus erat are indicative; captus sit / captus esset are subjunctive. Different words, no ambiguity. The helper carries the mood; the participle carries the completion and agrees in gender, number and case.
Deponents and semi-deponents
13.03		Deponents use the two-word forms and stay active in meaning: locūtus sit = "he has spoken." Semi-deponents switch halfway: audeat, audēret (one word) but ausus sit, ausus esset (two).
Which constructions use all four
13.04		Indirect questions (m11), cum clauses (m10) and fear clauses (m12) use all four cells. Purpose and indirect command (m08) look forward, so they only ever use the same-time column. Result clauses (m09) normally follow sequence.
Statements are differentAn indirect statement reports completed action with a perfect infinitive (sē cōnservāvisse), not with a subjunctive. Line 10 of the reading is there to keep the two systems apart.
What is still not yoursThe subjunctive as a main verb (Module 14). Conditions with the subjunctive, and the irregular verbs ferō, eō, volō, nōlō, mālō, fīō (Module 15). And the "would do" future inside a reported question, which this course does not teach at all — recast the English instead.

Module 14 — Jussive, Hortatory, Optative
For eight modules the subjunctive has lived inside a subordinate clause, hanging off a main verb. In Module 14 it walks out and becomes the main verb itself: veniat — "let him come." Dīcāmus — "let us speak." Utinam vēnisset — "if only he had come." No new forms; the four tenses you finished this week are all it needs. The reading turns to Cicero defending a consul-elect in court, where every other sentence is an appeal.
💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Quid factum esset. Three words, and they are the last thing this module leaves you: a pluperfect subjunctive passive inside an indirect question — "what had been done." Romans argued about that question for twenty years, in exactly this grammar. You can now read the argument in the tense they had it in. That is what cōnstantia bought you: six modules of waiting for two cells of a table, and the table is finished.
