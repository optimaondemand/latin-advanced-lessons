Lesson 2.03
The Perfect System, and Your Deponent Vocabulary
Grammar Core · Vocabulary
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Locūtus sum and laudātus sum look like twins. They are not.
One means "I was praised." The other means "I spoke." Same shape, opposite voice — and the only thing that tells you which is which is knowing the verb. So today you also lock in the twelve deponents you will meet again and again all year.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to form and translate the perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect of a deponent verb.
2
I will be able to make the deponent participle agree with its subject in gender and number.
3
I will be able to give the meaning and principal parts of twelve core deponent verbs.
📚 PART 1 · PERFECT, PLUPERFECT, FUTURE PERFECT

The recipe is the one you learned for the perfect passive last year: participle + a form of esse. Only the meaning is active.

TenseForm (3rd sg. masculine)Meaning
Perfectlocūtus esthe spoke, he has spoken
Pluperfectlocūtus erathe had spoken
Future perfectlocūtus erithe will have spoken

Full perfect of loquor

SingularPlural
1stlocūtus / locūta sum — I spokelocūtī / locūtae sumus — we spoke
2ndlocūtus / locūta es — you spokelocūtī / locūtae estis — you (pl.) spoke
3rdlocūtus / locūta / locūtum est — he/she/it spokelocūtī / locūtae / locūta sunt — they spoke
✍️ The participle is an adjective, so it agrees. A woman says locūta sum; a man says locūtus sum; a group of men says locūtī sumus. This is not decoration — in Sallust it is often how you know who is speaking. Coniūrātī secūtī sunt ("the conspirators followed") has a masculine plural participle because coniūrātī is masculine plural.
⚠️ PART 2 · THE TWINS THAT ARE NOT TWINS
ORDINARY VERB · laudō
laudātus sum
"I was praised."
Real perfect passive. Somebody else did the praising.
DEPONENT · loquor
locūtus sum
"I spoke."
Perfect tense, active meaning. I did the speaking.
⛔ Never translate a deponent perfect as a passive. Locūtus sum is not "I was spoken." Profectus est is not "he was set out." Mortuus est is not "he was died" — it means "he died." If the English comes out as nonsense, that is your signal that you are looking at a deponent.
💡 A bonus Latin usually cannot offer. Ordinary Latin has no active participle meaning "having done" something — you cannot say "having praised" in one word. Deponents can: profectus means "having set out", actively. That is why Sallust reaches for deponents when he wants to stack events tightly: Catilīna, ex urbe profectus, ad castra contendit — "Catiline, having set out from the city, hurried to the camp."

📜 DERIVATIVE MINUTE

Deponents are unusually generous with English words — which is a gift, because the derivative reminds you of the meaning:

LatinEnglish descendants
sequor — followsequence, sequel, consequence, prosecute (prō + sequor, "follow through")
patior — suffer, endurepatient, patience, passion, passive — all about what is undergone
morior — diemortal, immortal, mortuary, mortality
nāscor — be bornnatal, native, innate, nation, nature
ūtor — useuse, utility, utensil, usual

Notice passion. It reaches English through patior, "to suffer" — which is why the sufferings of a martyr are called a "passion" and why a "passive" verb is one that has something done to it. The word did not originally mean strong feeling at all.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A deponent perfect is participle + esse, exactly like a perfect passive — and it means the opposite. The participle still agrees with the subject. Locūta sum = "I spoke," said by a woman. Mortuus est = "he died," never "he was died."
