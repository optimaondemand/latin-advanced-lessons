Lesson 2.04
Deponent Participles
Grammar in Use
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Deponents can say something no other Latin verb can say.
Ordinary Latin cannot express "having praised" in a single word — it has no active past participle. Deponents have one. That single fact is why Sallust's most compressed sentences are full of them, and it is why today's forms show up on every page of this semester.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to form and translate the present active participle of a deponent verb.
2
I will be able to translate a deponent perfect participle actively — "having done," not "having been done."
3
I will be able to recognize and translate an ablative absolute built on a deponent participle.
📚 PART 1 · THE PRESENT ACTIVE PARTICIPLE

Here deponents behave completely normally: present stem + -ns, -ntis, exactly like laudāns, laudantis. Active in form, active in meaning — no surprises at all.

VerbPAPMeaning
loquor — speakloquēns, loquentisspeaking
hortor — urgehortāns, hortantisurging
sequor — followsequēns, sequentisfollowing
morior — diemoriēns, morientisdying
Catilīna, ad coniūrātōs loquēns, manum sustulit.
"Catiline, while speaking to the conspirators, raised his hand."
Loquēns is nominative singular, agreeing with Catilīna. Same time as sustulit, so "while speaking." Gloss: sustulit — "raised, lifted" (perfect of tollō).
💡 Why this participle matters in Sallust. A PAP lets you attach a second action to a person without starting a new clause. Sallust's style is famously compressed — short, dense, no wasted words — and participles are the main tool he uses to get that effect.
📚 PART 2 · THE PARTICIPLE ONLY DEPONENTS HAVE

Now the interesting one. Take the participle out of the third principal part and use it on its own:

ORDINARY VERB
laudātus
"having been praised"
Passive. Something was done to this person.
DEPONENT
profectus
"having set out"
Active. This person did it, and finished doing it.
DeponentPerfect participleMeaning
proficīscor — set outprofectus, -a, -umhaving set out
sequor — followsecūtus, -a, -umhaving followed
loquor — speaklocūtus, -a, -umhaving spoken
patior — sufferpassus, -a, -umhaving suffered
hortor — urgehortātus, -a, -umhaving urged
Coniūrātī, Catilīnam secūtī, in locum occultum vēnērunt.
"The conspirators, having followed Catiline, came to a hidden place."
Note Catilīnam: an accusative object, taken by the participle itself. A passive participle could never do that.

Deponents also form a future active participle in the ordinary way — locūtūrus, -a, -um, "about to speak"; secūtūrus, "about to follow." You will meet these occasionally in reading; they need no new rule.

📚 PART 3 · DEPONENTS IN AN ABLATIVE ABSOLUTE

Put a deponent participle into the ablative next to an ablative noun and you get an ordinary ablative absolute — with the deponent's active sense intact. The structure is exactly what you reviewed in Module 0.

Catilīnā hortante, mīlitēs fortius pugnābant.
"With Catiline urging them on — while Catiline urged them on — the soldiers fought more fiercely."
Both words ablative; Catilīnā has no other job in the sentence. Present participle, so the urging happens at the same time as the fighting.
Catilīnā ex urbe profectō, senātōrēs tandem quiēvērunt.
"Once Catiline had set out from the city, the senators at last grew calm."
Perfect participle, so the departure happens before the calming. Gloss: quiēvērunt — "grew quiet, rested" (perfect of quiēscō).
⚠️ Same trap as Module 0, new costume. Catilīnā hortante is an ablative absolute. But Catilīna, coniūrātōs hortāns, surrēxit is not — there hortāns is nominative and agrees with the subject Catilīna, so it just describes him: "Catiline, urging the conspirators on, stood up." Check the participle's case before you decide what you are looking at. Gloss: surrēxit — "rose, stood up" (perfect of surgō).

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A deponent's perfect participle is active: profectus = "having set out," and it can take an object. That is the one participle ordinary Latin verbs do not have — and it is why an author who wants density reaches for deponents.
