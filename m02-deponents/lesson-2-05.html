📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Twelve deponents in one speech — and every one of them chosen.
Sallust gives Catiline a speech to his inner circle. Read it for what he says — then look at which verbs he is given. Sallust does not have to call Catiline a fraud. He can let the grammar do it.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to parse every deponent form in an adapted passage of Sallust's Bellum Catilīnae.
2
I will be able to translate deponents in several tenses inside continuous prose.
3
I will be able to explain how Sallust's choice of verbs shapes the reader's judgment of Catiline.
📜 PART 1 · THE READING

Adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 20. A simplified Latin retelling of Catiline's address to the inner circle — not Sallust's own sentences. Every deponent is marked in red. Read it once straight through for sense.

Catilīna in locum occultum vēnit et ad coniūrātōs suōs loquēbātur. "Amīcī," inquit, "vōs mē secūtī estis et in hunc locum vēnistis. Grātiās vōbīs agō. Ego semper vōs hortātus sum fortiter agere et bonum animum habēre.

"Aliī hominēs ā senātū et ā dīvitibus oppressī sunt. Aliī magnā paupertāte queruntur. Nōs autem nōn querēmur amplius. Nunc tempus est agere. Nōs rem pūblicam capere cōnābimur, et omnēs aliī nōs sequentur.

"Arbitror nōs esse fortissimōs omnium. Quisque nostrum patriam novam aedificāre cōnābitur. Vōs, amīcī, mē secūtī, novam Rōmam vidēbitis."

Coniūrātī ōrātiōnem Catilīnae magnā cūrā audiēbant. Multī mīrābantur verba eius — tam audācia, tam nova erant. Tandem omnēs clāmāvērunt: "Secūtī sumus et sequēmur!"

📖 VOCABULARY GLOSS · every word not on your running list
  • occultus, -a, -um — hidden, secret
  • inquit — "he says / he said," inserted inside a quotation
  • hunc — acc. sg. m. of hic, "this"
  • grātiās agō — "I give thanks, I thank" (idiom; the person thanked goes in the dative: grātiās vōbīs agō)
  • fortiter (adv.) — bravely, boldly (from fortis)
  • bonum animum habēre — "to keep up your courage" (literally "to have a good spirit")
  • dīvitibus — abl. pl. of dīves, dīvitis, "rich" — here "the rich"
  • oppressī sunt — "have been crushed" (perfect passive of opprimō) — a real passive, for contrast
  • paupertās, paupertātis f. — poverty, need
  • amplius (adv.) — any longer, any further
  • tempus est agere — "it is time to act"
  • rem pūblicam — acc. of rēs pūblica, the republic, the state
  • fortissimōs omnium — "the bravest of all" (superlative + partitive genitive)
  • quisque nostrum — "each one of us" (nostrum = genitive of nōs)
  • aedificō, aedificāre, aedificāvī, aedificātum — to build, construct
  • tam (adv.) — so (with an adjective)
  • tandem (adv.) — at last, finally
  • clāmō, clāmāre, clāmāvī, clāmātum — to shout, cry out
✍️ One note about the adaptation. In hortātus sum…agere, this passage uses an infinitive after hortor. Full classical Latin normally introduces the command with ut and a subjunctive verb instead — which is exactly what you learn in Module 8. The adapter used the infinitive so that this reading stays inside the grammar you already have. When you meet the real construction later, you will recognize what it replaced.

🔍 Compare one line. Aliī hominēs…oppressī sunt is a real perfect passive: "other men have been crushed" — something was done to them. Three words later, queruntur is a deponent: "they complain" — something they do. Identical endings, opposite direction. This passage puts both in the same paragraph on purpose.

🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · WHY SALLUST GETS TO WRITE CATILINE'S SPEECH

Sallust was not in that room. Nobody wrote the speech down. So where does it come from?

Ancient historians composed speeches on purpose, and said so. The Greek historian Thucydides, writing centuries earlier, explained his method openly: he aimed at the general sense of what was actually said, shaped by what seemed most fitting for that speaker in those circumstances. Roman historians took over the practice wholesale. When you read Catiline speaking in Sallust, you are reading Sallust's judgment of what kind of man Catiline was. It is a portrait, not a transcript.

Which is why the verbs matter. Catiline's grievances are not invented — plenty of Romans in the 60s BC really were crushed by debt and shut out of advancement, and Sallust does not pretend otherwise. But look at what Catiline is given to say: cōnābimur, "we will try." Cōnābitur, "each of us will try." Hortātus sum, "I have urged." These are verbs of straining toward something not yet in hand. A man certain of victory does not talk like this. Sallust lets Catiline sound persuasive on the surface while planting the word choices that tell you it will fail.

The Roman word for it: virtūs. Catiline performs virtūs for his followers — he speaks boldly, promises victory, claims to act for their freedom. Sallust frames it as counterfeit. In the Roman tradition, real virtūs serves the rēs pūblica; Catiline's serves Catiline. Keep the distinction in view all semester: this whole course is two men arguing about what the word means.

2.01A deponent is passive in form, active in meaning, with three principal parts.
2.02Present system = the passive endings you already know. Watch loqueris (now) vs. loquēris (later).
2.03Perfect = participle + esse, and the participle agrees with the subject. Mortuus est = "he died."
2.04The deponent perfect participle is active: profectus = "having set out," and it can take an object.
2.05In real prose, deponents carry the narration — and an author's choice of them is an argument.

Module 3 — Semi-Deponents
Verbs that are half in and half out: active in the present system, deponent in the perfect (audeō…ausus sum, "I dare…I dared"). The reading follows Cicero's intelligence network — how the consul found out what Catiline had said in that room.
💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Deponents are not a footnote — in real Latin narrative they carry the action. And an author's verbs are never neutral: Sallust hands Catiline cōnābimur, "we will try," and lets you draw the conclusion. Grammar is also rhetoric.
