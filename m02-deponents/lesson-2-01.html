📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What do you do with a verb that wears the wrong clothes?
Loquitur has passive endings. Every rule you know says it means "he is spoken." It means "he speaks." This is not an error and not an exception you can ignore — it is a whole class of ordinary Latin verbs called deponents, and Sallust's narrative runs on them.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what a deponent verb is and why its passive forms carry active meaning.
2
I will be able to read a deponent's principal parts and say what each one gives me.
3
I will be able to name a deponent's conjugation from its infinitive, and tell a deponent apart from a true passive.
📚 PART 1 · THE TRAP

You have met this before without knowing it. In Module 0 you read Cicero's opening line: Quō ūsque tandem abūtēre…? The form looked passive. The meaning was active: "will you go on abusing." That was a deponent.

WHAT THE ENDING SAYS
loquitur
"he is spoken"
WHAT IT ACTUALLY MEANS
loquitur
"he speaks"

📚 PART 2 · THE DEFINITION, AND THE NAME
A deponent verb has passive forms and active meaning.

The name explains itself once you see it: dē- ("away") + pōnere ("to put") — a verb that has put away its active forms. They are gone. There is no *loquō, no *loquit. If you want to say "he speaks," loquitur is the only option Latin gives you.

⚠️ The consequence students always forget: a deponent has no passive meaning at all. You cannot use loquitur to say "he is spoken about." A deponent's passive-looking form is doing active duty, and there is nothing left over to carry a passive sense.

Principal parts: three, not four

A regular verb gives you four parts: laudō, laudāre, laudāvī, laudātum. A deponent gives you three, because its perfect is already built out of the participle:

loquor, loquī, locūtus sum
to speak
PartLooks likeActually means — and gives you
1 · loquor"I am spoken""I speak." The dictionary form.
2 · loquīa passive infinitive, "to be spoken""to speak." This part names the conjugation — see Part 3.
3 · locūtus suma perfect passive, "I was spoken""I spoke / I have spoken." This is the perfect tense, and the participle locūtus inside it is the stem for the other perfect tenses.
💡 Why there is no fourth part. For a regular verb, part 4 is the PPP, used to build the perfect passive. A deponent has no perfect passive to build — so its participle has already been folded into part 3. Three parts is not less information; it is the same information with nothing wasted.
📚 PART 3 · WHICH CONJUGATION? ASK THE INFINITIVE

Deponents belong to the same four conjugations as everything else, and they follow those vowel patterns exactly. The second principal part tells you which:

ConjugationInfinitive ends inExample
1st-ārīhortor, hortārī, hortātus sum — to urge, encourage
2nd-ērīvereor, verērī, veritus sum — to fear, respect (you will use this one in Module 12)
3rd-īloquor, loquī, locūtus sum — to speak
3rd io-stem-ī (stem in -i-)patior, patī, passus sum — to suffer, endure, allow
4th-īrīpotior, potīrī, potītus sum — to gain possession of
✍️ Compare with the passives you already know. A regular 1st-conjugation passive infinitive is laudārī, "to be praised." A 1st-conjugation deponent infinitive is hortārī, "to urge." Identical shape, opposite meaning. The only way to know which you are looking at is to know the verb — which is why deponents get their own vocabulary list this week.

★ VIRTŪS · COURAGE

Virtūs comes from vir, "man," and for Romans it meant courage first and excellence generally — the quality that made a person worth counting on. Deponents are a small lesson in it. Everything about their surface says "passive," and the reader who trusts the surface gets the sentence backwards. Reading them right takes the nerve to hold two things at once: this looks like X, and I know it is doing Y. That is not a language trick. It is most of what careful reading is.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A deponent verb is passive in form, active in meaning, and it has three principal parts because its perfect is already built from a participle. When part 1 ends in -or and there are only three parts, stop translating passively.
