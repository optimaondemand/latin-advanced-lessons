The Question Inside the Sentence

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A question that has stopped being a question.
Back in Module 7 you were shown a sentence and told, honestly, that you were not ready for it: Cicerō rogāvit cūr Catilīna manēret. You are ready now. It is an indirect question — a question folded inside another sentence, keeping its question word, losing its question mark, and putting its verb into the subjunctive. This module is the payoff for Modules 6 and 7.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define an indirect question and name the two things that always mark one.
2
I will be able to turn a direct question into an indirect one in four mechanical steps.
3
I will be able to spot an indirect question inside a longer sentence and say which verb introduced it.
📚 PART 1 · THE SAME QUESTION, TWICE

Here is a question Cicero could have shouted across the Senate floor, and here is the same question as a historian would report it afterward. Look at what changes.

DIRECT QUESTION — ASKED OUT LOUD
Quid Catilīna facit?
"What is Catiline doing?"
facit = indicative · a real question · question mark
INDIRECT QUESTION — REPORTED
Cicerō rogat quid Catilīna faciat.
"Cicero asks what Catiline is doing."
faciat = subjunctive · embedded · full stop

The question word quid survives untouched. The word order barely moves. Two things change, and they are the whole construction: the verb goes into the subjunctive, and the sentence is no longer asking anybody anything — it is reporting that somebody asked.

An indirect question is a question embedded inside another sentence. It is introduced by an interrogative word and its verb is in the subjunctive, with the tense set by sequence of tenses.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Before you read the rule: why would Latin bother switching the mood? The reported question means exactly the same thing. What is the subjunctive for here?
📚 PART 2 · FOUR STEPS, EVERY TIME

This is one of the most mechanical transformations in Latin. Learn the four steps and you can do it to any question you meet.

StepDo thisWorked example
1Keep the question word exactly as it is.Cūr Catilīna manet? → … cūr …
2Add a main verb of asking, knowing, or perceiving.Cicerō rogāvit cūr …
3Change the verb from indicative to subjunctive, and let the main verb pick its tense (Module 7).manet (present indic.) → manēret (imperfect subj., because rogāvit is past)
4Delete the question mark. Nobody is asking anymore.Cicerō rogāvit cūr Catilīna manēret.
Cicerō rogāvit cūr Catilīna manēret.
"Cicero asked why Catiline was remaining."

Notice that step 3 is doing two jobs at once. It changes the mood (indicative to subjunctive) and it sets the tense by sequence. Pull the sequence rule back now, because you will use it in every sentence this week:

If the main verb is…SequenceThe subjunctive is…
present or future — rogat, rogābit, nescit, scietPRIMARYPRESENT subjunctive
imperfect, perfect, or pluperfect — rogābat, rogāvit, nesciēbatSECONDARYIMPERFECT subjunctive
💡 Same sentence, two sequences. Cicerō rogat cūr Catilīna maneat — "Cicero asks why Catiline is remaining." Cicerō rogāvit cūr Catilīna manēret — "Cicero asked why Catiline was remaining." Nothing in the reported question changed. Only the main verb moved, and the subjunctive followed it.
✍️ Two tenses now, four later — and you should know that up front. Indirect questions can also report a question about something already finished: "he asked what you had done." That needs the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive, which are built in Module 13. Until then, every indirect question in this course asks about something happening at the same time as the main verb, and the present and imperfect subjunctive cover it completely. Nothing in this module's quiz or translation will ask you for a Module 13 form, and you should not offer one.
📚 PART 3 · THE VERBS THAT OPEN THE DOOR

An indirect question does not float free. Something has to introduce it — a verb of asking, knowing, not knowing, perceiving, wondering, or finding out. Most of these you already own.

GroupVerbs
Askingrogō (ask) · quaerō (inquire, seek) · petō (request)
Knowing / not knowingsciō (know) · nesciō (not know) · ignōrō (be ignorant of) · dubitō (doubt, hesitate)
Perceivingintellegō (understand) · sentiō (perceive, be of the opinion) · videō (see → grasp) · audiō (hear)
Wonderingmīror (wonder at — deponent, Module 2)
Finding out / reportingcognōscō (learn, come to know) · nūntiō (report) · dīcō (say — when it reports information)

Read these four and watch the pattern hold in every one:

Nesciō ubi Catilīna sit.
"I do not know where Catiline is." — nesciō present → primary → present subj. of esse.

Cicerō intellegēbat quantum perīculum esset.
"Cicero understood how great the danger was." — intellegēbat imperfect → secondary → imperfect subj.

Mīror cūr senātōrēs taceant.
"I wonder why the senators are silent." — mīror is a deponent, present in meaning as well as form → primary.

Cognōscet quot coniūrātī in urbe maneant.
"He will find out how many conspirators are remaining in the city." — cognōscet is future, and future is primary, so the subjunctive is present.

📖 GLOSS FOR THE SENTENCES ABOVE
  • quantum perīculum — "how great a danger" (quantus, -a, -um agreeing with perīculum)
  • taceant — present subjunctive of taceō, tacēre, tacuī, "to be silent, say nothing"
  • quot (indeclinable) — how many
  • coniūrātī, coniūrātōrum m. pl. — the conspirators

★ TEMPERANTIA · SELF-GOVERNMENT

Temperantia comes from temperō, "to mix in the right proportion" — the same root that gives English temper, temperature, and temperate. Romans did not mean by it only restraint at the dinner table. They meant right measure in everything, including how much you claim to know.

That is why the indirect question belongs to this virtue. An indicative sentence asserts: hoc est vērum — "this is true." An indirect question does something harder: it names, out loud and in grammatical form, the exact shape of what you have not settled. Nesciō quid optimum sit — "I do not know what is best." Saying that costs something, especially if people are waiting on you for an answer. This module you will read a consul saying it in front of the Roman Senate on the worst morning of his career. Learn the grammar of acknowledged uncertainty carefully; it is a tool for thinking honestly, not just for parsing.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

An indirect question keeps its interrogative word, loses its question mark, and puts its verb in the subjunctive at the tense the main verb dictates. Present or future main verb → present subjunctive. Past main verb → imperfect subjunctive.
