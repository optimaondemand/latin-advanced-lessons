📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have read this meeting twice already. Today you hear the man who was chairing it.
Module 9 gave you Sallust's account of the Senate debate of 5 December 63 BC. Module 10 gave you what happened that evening. Today the course rewinds a few hours and puts you in the room with Cicero's own words — the fourth and last speech against Catiline, delivered while the vote was still open. It is built out of questions, because a man who is presiding cannot simply announce what will happen. He has to ask.

📚 PART 1 · BEFORE YOU READ
1. Where. Not the Cūria. On 5 December 63 BC the Senate met in the aedēs Concordiae, the Temple of Concord, at the foot of the Capitoline — a building whose name is worth a moment's thought given what was decided inside it.
2. What Cicero could and could not do. He was consul, so he was presiding. A presiding consul put the question and counted the votes; he did not get a vote of his own. So his speech cannot say “do this.” It can only say: here are the two proposals, here is what each one means, now decide. That constraint is why the passage is full of indirect questions — the grammar and the political position match.
3. Two proposals — and the winner was neither of them yet. When Cicero rose, the Senate had heard Sīlānus, the consul-elect, propose the death penalty, and Caesar propose confiscation of property and life imprisonment in the Italian towns. Cicero weighs those two. Cato spoke after him, and it was Cato's speech — the one Sallust gives you in Module 9 — that carried the vote for death. Keep the order straight: Sīlānus, Caesar, Cicero, Cato.
💡 Watch for the whole week at once. The passage carries single indirect questions with question words, one double question with utrum…an, two indirect statements from 0706010, two relative clauses, an indirect command from m08, and a cum clause from m10 in the last line. This is the first reading this semester that asks you to hold five constructions at the same time, which is exactly what Lesson 11.04 was for.
📜 PART 2 · THE READING
Adapted from Cicero, In Catilīnam IV (5 December 63 BC). Lines 1 and 6 are close to Cicero's own words; the rest has been shortened and simplified to the grammar you have been taught, and the sequence of proposals is his. This is an adaptation, not Cicero's own Latin.

1Videō, patrēs cōnscrīptī, in mē omnium vestrum ōra atque oculōs esse conversōs.

2Nōn ignōrō quantum perīculum mihi ipsī immineat.

3Sed dē mē nunc nōn loquor: quaerō quid dē coniūrātīs cōnstituātis.

4Rēs enim nōn est obscūra; manifesta est atque dēprehēnsa.

5Vidētis quot hominēs in hāc coniūrātiōne sint et quam grave sit hoc scelus.

6Videō adhūc duās esse sententiās.

7Ūnam Sīlānus dīcit, quī supplicium postulat; alteram Caesar, quī cēnset ut coniūrātī in custōdiam trādantur.

8Nunc igitur cōnstituere dēbētis utrum lēnitās an sevēritās reī pūblicae magis ūtilis sit.

9Ego autem, quaecumque cōnstituētis, faciam; et vōs rogō ut hunc diem memoriā teneātis.

10Quae cum ita sint, dē salūte patriae statuite.

📖 GLOSS — EVERY WORD NOT ON YOUR RUNNING LIST, IN THE FORM IT APPEARS
  • 1 · patrēs cōnscrīptī — “senators” (the formal address to the Senate; literally “enrolled fathers”) · vestrum — of you (genitive plural) · ōra — faces (accusative plural of ōs, ōris n.) · atque — and · conversōs — turned (perfect passive participle of convertō); ōra…esse conversōs is an accusative + infinitive — an indirect statement
  • 2 · immineat — present subjunctive of immineō, imminēre (+ dative): to hang over, threaten · mihi ipsī — over me myself (dative)
  • 3 · dē mē — about myself · cōnstituātis — present subjunctive, 2nd plural, of cōnstituō: to decide
  • 4 · enim — for, in fact (never stands first) · obscūrus, -a, -um — unclear · manifesta — plain, caught in the open · dēprehēnsa — caught, detected (perfect passive participle of dēprehendō)
  • 5 · hominēs — men, people (nominative plural of homō, hominis m.) · hāc coniūrātiōne — in this conspiracy · quam grave — how serious (quam + adjective; gravis, grave — heavy, serious) · scelus, sceleris n. — crime
  • 6 · adhūc — so far, up to now · duās esse sententiās — another accusative + infinitive: “that there are two proposals”
  • 7 · ūnam…alteram — the one…the other · Sīlānus, Sīlānī m. — Silanus, consul-elect for 62 BC · postulō, postulāre — to demand, call for · cēnset ut…trādantur — “moves that they be handed over” (cēnseō + ut + subjunctive is an indirect command, m08) · custōdia, custōdiae f. — custody, confinement · trādantur — present subjunctive passive of trādō, trādere: to hand over
  • 8 · igitur — therefore · lēnitās, lēnitātis f. — mildness, mercy · sevēritās, sevēritātis f. — strictness, severity · reī pūblicae — to the republic (dative) · magis — more · ūtilis, ūtile — useful, in one's interest
  • 9 · autem — however, but (never stands first) · quaecumque — whatever things (neuter accusative plural) · cōnstituētis — you will decide (future indicative) · memoriā teneātis — “that you hold in memory,” i.e. that you remember (an indirect command after rogō ut, m08)
  • 10 · Quae cum ita sint — a fixed Ciceronian phrase: “since these things are so.” A causal cum clause (m10) with the present subjunctive sint · salūs, salūtis f. — safety, welfare · statuite — decide! (imperative plural of statuō, statuere)
⚠️ Two honest notes. First: Cicero's real speech reports the two proposals using perfect and future infinitives and one tense of the subjunctive you do not own until Module 13; the adaptation moves everything into the present and imperfect so you can read it now. Second: line 8 is the adaptation's own framing of Cicero's argument, not a sentence he wrote. He does weigh mercy against severity at length in this speech — and worries aloud about being thought either cruel or slack — but the neat utrum…an question is ours, built to show you the construction in its natural home.

🏛️ THE ROMAN WORLD · THE SPEECH SALLUST LEFT OUT

Cicero published this speech himself, some years later, which means we have his voice for that meeting in a way we have almost no one else's. And here is the thing worth noticing: Sallust does not. In the Bellum Catilīnae Sallust writes out Caesar's speech at length and Cato's at length — and gives Cicero, the presiding consul, nothing. Not a paraphrase. Not a line.

That silence is a judgment. Sallust was writing twenty years later, under a very different Rome, and he was interested in Caesar and Cato as two kinds of Roman greatness. Cicero interested him much less. So the historian and the orator hand you two incompatible versions of one afternoon: in Cicero's, the consul frames the choice and the Senate decides; in Sallust's, two great men argue and the consul is furniture. Neither is lying. Both are choosing. When you read a primary source you are always reading someone's decision about what mattered — which is the vēritās problem Module 9 raised, arriving now from the other direction.

📚 PART 4 · THE WHOLE MODULE IN ONE TABLE

If you can reconstruct this from memory, you are ready for the quiz.

PieceWhat to remember
The four steps
11.01		Keep the question word · add a main verb of asking/knowing · indicative → subjunctive, tense set by sequence · delete the question mark.
Sequence
m07, used every day this week		Main verb present or future → present subjunctive. Main verb imperfect, perfect or pluperfect → imperfect subjunctive.
The question words
11.02		cūr, ubi, quō, quandō, quōmodo (adverbs, no endings) · quantus, -a, -um and uter, utra, utrum (agree like adjectives) · quot (indeclinable) · quis, quid (declines) · quam + adjective.
Yes-or-no reported
11.03		num or -ne = “whether” — and indirect num has lost its “surely not?” bias.
Double questions
11.03		utrum…an (or -ne…an) = “whether…or…” · …necne = “or not” · an is never the ordinary “or” — that is aut.
The look-alikes
11.04		Accusative + infinitive → indirect statement. Subjunctive → read the opening word: cum / question word / ut–nē (then check for a signpost). Quī with an antecedent and an indicative → relative clause.
What is not yet yoursThe perfect and pluperfect subjunctive (Module 13). Until then every indirect question in this course reports something happening at the same time as the main verb — and the present and imperfect cover that completely. Do not offer a m13 form on the quiz.

Module 12 — Fear Clauses
“I am afraid that he will come” — and Latin writes nē, the word you have been reading as “so that…not” since Module 8. It is the strangest reversal in the subjunctive system, and there is a reason for it. The reading follows Catiline north to Pistoria, and to the end.
💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Cicero could not tell the Senate what to do, so he asked them — and Latin has a whole construction for a question that has stopped being a question. Temperantia is the virtue of this module for a reason: naming precisely what you do not yet know is a discipline, not a weakness.
