Lesson 11.03
Whether: Yes-or-No Questions Reported
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday's kit had nine question words in it. Today's questions have none.
“Is Catiline in the city?” has no cūr, no ubi, no quot — nothing for the four steps to keep. So how does Latin report it? The answer is a small set of words that mean, roughly, “whether”: the enclitic -ne, the word num, and for a choice between two things, utrum…an. Everything else about the construction — subjunctive, sequence, no question mark — stays exactly as it was.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognise -ne, nōnne and num as the three ways Latin asks a direct yes-or-no question.
2
I will be able to report a yes-or-no question with num or -ne meaning “whether,” keeping the subjunctive and the sequence.
3
I will be able to build and translate a double question with utrum…an and …necne.
📚 PART 1 · DIRECT YES-OR-NO: THREE FLAVOURS

Start with the direct question, because you have to know what is being reported before you can report it. English asks a yes-or-no question by moving the verb: “Catiline is in the city” → “Is Catiline in the city?” Latin cannot do that — its word order is too free for a rearrangement to signal anything. So Latin adds a word. And the word it picks tells you what answer the speaker expects:

WordExpectsExample
-ne
glued to the first word		nothing — a neutral questionEstne Catilīna in urbe?
“Is Catiline in the city?” A real question; the speaker does not know.
nōnneyes — “surely…?”Nōnne Catilīna in urbe est?
“Catiline is in the city, isn't he?” The speaker is confident and wants agreement.
numno — “surely not…?”Num Catilīna in urbe est?
“Catiline isn't in the city, is he?” The speaker expects to be told no.
💡 -ne is an enclitic — a word too small to stand alone, so it rides on the back of another one, like the -que in senātus populusque. It attaches to whichever word the question is really about, and that word goes first: Estne…? asks about the being; Catilīnane…? would ask whether it is Catiline (rather than someone else). You met the same trick in mēcum — small words in Latin like to travel attached.

📚 PART 2 · REPORTED: “WHETHER”

Now fold one of those questions inside a bigger sentence. Run the same four steps from Lesson 11.01 — and step 1 is where the change happens, because there is no question word to keep. You supply one instead. Latin's usual choices are num and -ne, and in an indirect question both simply mean “whether.”

Direct: Estne Catilīna in urbe? — “Is Catiline in the city?”
Reported: Cicerō rogāvit num Catilīna in urbe esset.
“Cicero asked whether Catiline was in the city.”

Check the four steps against it. 1 — supply num in place of the missing question word. 2 — the main verb of asking is rogāvit. 3 — est becomes subjunctive, and because rogāvit is perfect the sequence is secondary, so esset. 4 — no question mark. Nothing new; only step 1 behaves differently.

LatinEnglish
Nesciō num coniūrātī adhūc in urbe sint.“I do not know whether the conspirators are still in the city.” Present nesciō → primary → present subjunctive.
Cicerō quaesīvit num Lentulus haec probāret.“Cicero inquired whether Lentulus approved of these things.” Perfect quaesīvit → secondary → imperfect subjunctive.
Rogō possitne senātus hodiē cōnstituere.“I ask whether the Senate can decide today.” Here -ne does the job instead, glued to possit. Same meaning.
⚠️ The one place num quietly changes meaning. In a direct question, num carries an expectation: “surely not?” In an indirect question that expectation drops away and num means a plain, neutral “whether.” Nesciō num vēnerit is not “I don't know — surely he hasn't come?” It is simply “I do not know whether he has come.” This catches good students, because they have learned num's bias well and then keep applying it where it no longer holds.
💡 A note on one idiom you will see and should not imitate yet. After nesciō and dubitō, Roman writers sometimes use an by itself — and nesciō an often means not “I don't know whether” but something closer to “I rather think.” It is a real idiom and it is genuinely confusing, so it will always be glossed for you when it appears. Do not use it in your own Latin this year.
📚 PART 3 · TWO CHOICES: UTRUM…AN

The commonest yes-or-no question in Roman politics was not really yes-or-no at all. It was this or that — imprisonment or death, mercy or severity, act now or wait. Latin has a dedicated frame for it, and you will meet it in Cicero on Friday:

utrum … an …  =  “whether … or …”
Utrum introduces the first option; an introduces the second. Both verbs go into the subjunctive if both halves are reported — though very often the two options share one verb, as in the example below, and then there is only one subjunctive to worry about.
Cicerō rogāvit utrum lēnitās an sevēritās reī pūblicae magis ūtilis esset.
“Cicero asked whether mercy or severity was more useful to the republic.” Gloss: lēnitās, lēnitātis f. — mildness, mercy; sevēritās, sevēritātis f. — strictness, severity; magis — more; ūtilis, ūtile — useful (Module 11 vocabulary).

Two variations you should recognise:

FrameWhat it does
-ne … an …The same thing, with the enclitic doing utrum's job: Rogāvit lēnitāsne an sevēritās… Read it exactly as utrum…an.
… necne“or not.” Cicerō nesciēbat utrum coniūrātī fugerent necne. — “Cicero did not know whether the conspirators were fleeing or not.” In a reported question the “or not” is necne, one word, sitting at the end.
✍️ Do not confuse an with aut. Aut is the ordinary “or” you have used since your first year: gladiō aut hastā, “with a sword or a spear.” An is the “or” that lives inside a question. If you find an, there is a question in the sentence, direct or reported — which makes an a signal word worth as much to you as tamen was in Module 10.

📜 PART 5 · THE WORD LIST · CLICK EACH CARD

Six small words, and they are the whole lesson. Say what each one does before you flip.

🃏 FLIP CARDS · SAY IT FIRST
-ne
direct? reported?
Both. Direct: a neutral yes-or-no question. Reported: “whether.” An enclitic — it must ride on the back of another word.
nōnne
what does it expect?
Direct questions, expecting “yes.” “Surely…?” / “…isn't he?”
num
careful — two jobs
Direct: expects “no” — “surely not…?” Reported: a neutral “whether,” with the expectation gone.
utrum…an
what shape of question?
“Whether…or…” — a choice between two options. Utrum opens, an introduces the alternative.
necne
where does it sit?
“Or not” — one word, at the end of a reported question. …utrum fugerent necne.
an vs aut
which is which?
An = the “or” inside a question. Aut = the ordinary “or.” Finding an tells you a question is in the sentence.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A yes-or-no question has nothing to report with, so Latin lends it a word: num or -ne for one option, utrum…an for two, necne for “or not.” The subjunctive and the sequence never change. Only step 1 of the four steps does.
