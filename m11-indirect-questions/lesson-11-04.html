Question, Statement, or Relative?

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You now own five ways to hang a clause off a main verb. On the page, three of them start with the letters qu.
Indirect statement came from 0706010. Purpose arrived in Module 8, result in Module 9, cum in Module 10, and the indirect question this week. Individually each one is clear. Together they are the single biggest source of wrong translations at this level — because the clue is never the general shape of the sentence, it is always one small word plus one verb ending. Today: the quis/quī trap, the statement-versus-question distinction, and a procedure that runs on any of the five.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell an indirect question from an indirect statement by looking at two things: the linking word and the verb form.
2
I will be able to tell interrogative quis from relative quī in a sentence, using the antecedent and the mood.
3
I will be able to name which of five constructions a subordinate clause belongs to, on sight.
📚 PART 1 · THE FIVE, IN ONE PLACE

Every one of these is something you have been taught. Read the middle column first — that is the identifier, and it is always short.

💡 Notice what is not in the middle column: meaning. Not one of these five is identified by what the sentence is about. Every one is identified by a linking word and a verb form — things you can see without understanding the sentence. That is the whole reason a procedure works.
📚 PART 2 · THE QUIS / QUĪ TRAP

Latin has two qu- words that both come out in English as “who,” and they belong to two completely different constructions. Lesson 11.02 named the problem; here is how to settle it.

WordWhat it isHow to spot it
quis, quidthe interrogative pronoun
“who? what?”		It asks. It has no antecedent — nothing earlier in the sentence for it to point back to. In an indirect question its verb is subjunctive.
quī, quae, quodthe relative pronoun
“who, which, that”		It points back. It has an antecedent — a noun just before it that it agrees with in gender and number. Its verb is normally indicative.
Indirect question: Cicerō rogāvit quis haec faceret.
“Cicero asked who was doing these things.” — no antecedent, subjunctive verb, main verb of asking.

Relative clause: Vir quī haec faciēbat erat Lentulus.
“The man who was doing these things was Lentulus.” — antecedent vir, indicative verb, no asking involved.
✍️ The one-question test. Look immediately before the qu- word. Is there a noun it could be pointing back to? If yes, you are almost certainly in a relative clause. If there is nothing there — if the qu- word is hanging off a verb of asking or knowing — it is an indirect question. Then confirm with the mood: relative usually indicative, indirect question always subjunctive.
⚠️ One honest complication. Quī can also be an interrogative adjective: quī cōnsul vigilābat? means “which consul was keeping watch?” So quī is not a guarantee of a relative clause. The rule that holds is the one above — look for an antecedent, then check the mood — and not “quis means question, quī means relative.” Latin is rarely that tidy, and a rule you have to break is worse than a test you can apply.
📚 PART 3 · STATEMENT OR QUESTION? THE REAL CONFUSION

This is the pair that trips up students who have understood everything. Both constructions report something someone said or knew. In English they look like cousins. In Latin they share nothing at all:

Indirect statementIndirect question
The linking wordnone — English supplies “that,” Latin supplies nothinga question word, or num / -ne / utrum
The subjectaccusative — Catilīnamnominative — Catilīna
The verbinfinitive — esse, fuisse, futūrum essesubjunctive — sit, esset
Sequence of tensesdoes not apply — the infinitive's tense shows time relative to the main verbapplies fully — primary or secondary, Module 7
Cicerō dīxit Catilīnam in urbe esse. — “Cicero said that Catiline was in the city.” Accusative + infinitive. A statement.
Cicerō rogāvit num Catilīna in urbe esset. — “Cicero asked whether Catiline was in the city.” Nominative + subjunctive. A question.

The two sentences describe the same moment. One is Cicero telling the Senate something; the other is Cicero asking. Latin does not leave that ambiguous for a second — it is written into the case of the subject and the form of the verb.

💡 And dīcō can do both. Dīxit Catilīnam vēnisse — “he said that Catiline had come.” Dīxit cūr Catilīna venīret — “he told them why Catiline was coming.” The main verb does not decide it. The linking word and the verb form do, which is why the middle column of Part 1's table never mentions the main verb except as a hint.
🧠 PART 4 · THE PROCEDURE, AND THE PILE

Three questions, in order, and they identify all five constructions.

1
Is the subordinate verb an infinitive with an accusative subject?
Yes → indirect statement. Stop.
2
If it is a subjunctive: what word opens the clause?
cum → a cum clause (m10 — then run the four steps from 10.04).
a question word, or num / -ne / utrum → an indirect question.
ut or nē → purpose or result. Go to step 3.
3
Is there a signpost word in the main clause — tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō?
Yes → result. No → purpose (and the negative confirms it: nē is purpose, ut nōn is result).

Six sentences, unlabelled. Run the three steps.

📜 PART 5 · ONE WORD, ONE CONSTRUCTION · CLICK EACH CARD

Each card is a linking word. Name the construction it belongs to before you flip.

🃏 FLIP CARDS · NAME THE CONSTRUCTION
accusative + infinitive
which construction?
Indirect statement (0706010). No subjunctive, no sequence rule — the infinitive's tense carries the time.
nē + subjunctive
which construction?
Negative purpose (m08) — or an indirect command after imperō, rogō. Never a result: negative result is ut nōn.
tantus…ut + subjunctive
which construction?
Result (m09). The signpost in the main clause is the giveaway.
cum + subjunctive
which construction?
Cum clause (m10) — temporal, causal, or concessive. Run the four steps from 10.04; tamen settles the third.
cūr + subjunctive
which construction?
Indirect question (m11). A question word plus a subjunctive, hanging off a verb of asking or knowing.
utrum…an + subjunctive
which construction?
Indirect double question (m11, from 11.03). “Whether…or…”
quī + indicative, with an antecedent
which construction?
Relative clause (0706010). Points back to a noun; not a question at all.
quis + subjunctive, no antecedent
which construction?
Indirect question (m11). Asks; points back at nothing.
quot vs tot
which is which?
Quot = “how many” → indirect question. Tot = “so many” → result signpost. Both indeclinable, and easy to swap by accident.
an vs aut
which is which?
An = the “or” inside a question. Aut = the ordinary “or.” An tells you a question is present.
