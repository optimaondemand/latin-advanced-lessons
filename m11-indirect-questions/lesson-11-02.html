📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The construction is one rule. The vocabulary is the actual work.
You learned the whole grammar of the indirect question yesterday in four steps. What you do not have yet is the list of question words, and without it you will not recognize an indirect question when it walks past you. Today you learn the kit — nine interrogatives, one of which declines — and the ten words this module's reading needs.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to give the meaning of cūr, ubi, quō, quandō, quōmodo, quantus, quot, and uter on sight.
2
I will be able to decline the interrogative quis, quid and keep it apart from the adjective quī, quae, quod.
3
I will be able to choose the right interrogative for a given English question.
📚 PART 1 · THE KIT

Every example below is a complete indirect question. Read the Latin, then check the English — and note that every subjunctive here is either present or imperfect, exactly the two you own.

InterrogativeAsksExample
cūrwhyCicerō rogāvit cūr coniūrātī tacērent.
"Cicero asked why the conspirators were silent."
ubiwhereNesciō ubi Catilīna sit.
"I do not know where Catiline is."
quōto where, whitherNesciēbant quō Catilīna proficīscerētur.
"They did not know where Catiline was setting out for."
quandōwhenRogat quandō senātus conveniat.
"He asks when the Senate is meeting."
quōmodohow, in what wayCicerō nesciēbat quōmodo coniūrātī litterās mitterent.
"Cicero did not know how the conspirators were sending letters."
quantus, -a, -umhow great, how largeIntellegimus quantum perīculum sit.
"We understand how great the danger is."
quot (indeclinable)how manyRogāvit quot coniūrātī in urbe manērent.
"He asked how many conspirators were remaining in the city."
uter, utra, utrumwhich of twoRogāvit uter cōnsul vigilāret.
"He asked which of the two consuls was keeping watch."
quam + adj./adv.how (great, serious, quickly…)Nōn ignōrō quam gravis sit haec rēs.
"I am well aware how serious this matter is."
💡 Three of these agree, and three do not. Quantus and uter are adjectives: they take the gender, number, and case of the noun they belong to (quantum perīculum, neuter; uter cōnsul, masculine). Quot never changes at all — it is indeclinable, like tot from Module 9. And cūr, ubi, quō, quandō, quōmodo are plain adverbs with no endings to worry about. That is most of the work already done for you.
📖 GLOSS FOR THE EXAMPLES ABOVE
  • tacērent — imperfect subjunctive of taceō, tacēre, tacuī, "to be silent, say nothing"
  • proficīscerētur — imperfect subjunctive of the deponent proficīscor (Module 2), "to set out"
  • conveniat — present subjunctive of conveniō, convenīre, "to come together, assemble, meet"
  • vigilāret — imperfect subjunctive of vigilō, vigilāre, "to keep watch, stay awake" (Module 7)
  • nōn ignōrō — literally "I am not ignorant of," i.e. "I know full well." Latin likes this double negative; keep it in your English as "I am well aware."
  • coniūrātī, coniūrātōrum m. pl. — the conspirators
📚 PART 2 · THE ONE THAT DECLINES: QUIS, QUID

"Who" and "what" are not adverbs. They are a pronoun, and a pronoun has to take the case its job in the clause requires. Learn the singular; the plural simply borrows the relative pronoun's forms, which you already know from 0706010.

CaseMasculine / feminineNeuterJob in the clause
Nominativequisquidsubject — who? what?
Genitivecuiuscuiuswhose? of what?
Dativecuicuito whom? for what?
Accusativequemquiddirect object — whom? what?
Ablativequōquōwith / from whom or what?

Rogō quis in cūriā adsit.
"I ask who is present in the Senate House." — quis is nominative: it is the subject of adsit.

Sciō quid Catilīna faciat.
"I know what Catiline is doing." — quid is accusative: it is the object of faciat.

Rogāvit quem senātus audīret.
"He asked whom the Senate was listening to." — quem is accusative masculine: the object of audīret, with senātus as subject.

⚠️ quis is not quī. Two different words that look related and behave differently:
quis, quid
pronoun — stands alone. Rogō quis veniat. "I ask who is coming."
quī, quae, quod
adjective — sits next to a noun. Rogō quī senātor veniat. "I ask which senator is coming."
Same test both times: is there a noun for it to modify? If yes, use the quī, quae, quod forms; if it is standing on its own, use quis or quid. And do not confuse either one with the relative pronoun quī, quae, quod from 0706010 — identical forms, completely different job. The relative points back at a noun already named (senātor quī venit, "the senator who is coming"); the interrogative asks for information nobody has given yet.

The interrogatives above, plus these. They are the words Cicero needs on 5 December 63 BC, and every one of them turns up in Thursday's and Friday's work.

📜 Etymology moment. Ignōrō is in- ("not") + the root of nōscō ("know") — and it gives English ignorant, ignore, ignorance. Dubitō comes from duo, "two": to doubt is to be pulled two ways, which is exactly what dubious and indubitable still mean. Manifestus is manus ("hand") + a root meaning "struck" or "seized" — what is manifest has been caught red-handed, and a manifesto is a claim held up in plain view. And sententia gives you sentence and sentiment: for a Roman senator, your sententia was both your opinion and the motion you moved.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Most interrogatives are adverbs with nothing to learn but the meaning. Three need care: quantus and uter are adjectives and must agree with their noun, and quis, quid is a pronoun that must take the case its job in the clause requires — quis for a subject, quem for an object.
