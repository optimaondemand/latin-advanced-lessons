📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Eleven gerunds, and every one of them is an argument.
Read the morning of 8 November 63 BC: a consul who has not slept, a chamber where nobody will sit near one man, and a speech that opens with a question instead of a charge. Then look at the gerunds. Cicero uses the same construction twice to force a choice between two purposes — and that is not grammar decorating rhetoric. That is grammar doing rhetoric.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to find and parse every gerund in an adapted passage from Cicero's In Catilīnam I.
2
I will be able to translate gerunds in several cases inside continuous prose.
3
I will be able to explain how a repeated gerund construction turns a description into an accusation.
📜 PART 1 · THE READING

Adapted from Cicero, In Catilīnam I. A simplified Latin retelling of the morning of the speech — not Cicero's own sentences. Every gerund is marked in red. Read it once straight through for sense before you parse anything.

Māne, ante lūcem, Cicerō in domō suā sōlus sedēbat. Cōnsul erat, et illō diē ōrātiōnem habēre parābat. Tōtam noctem cōgitandō ēgerat, quia perīculum reī pūblicae maximum erat.

Ars dīcendī Cicerōnī nōn nova erat. Iuvenis in Graeciā studuerat; multōs annōs in Forō Rōmānō dīxerat. Sed hoc perīculum maius erat quam omnia alia.

Tandem stilum cēpit et pauca verba scrīpsit; nam ōrātōrēs Rōmānī memoriā ūtēbantur. Deinde dīcendī causā festīnāvit — nōn ad Cūriam, sed ad templum, quia illō diē senātus in templō convēnerat.

In templō senātōrēs iam ad audiendum sedēbant. Catilīna ipse aderat. Nēmō prope eum sedēbat: omnēs ab eō discesserant, et sellae circum eum vacuae erant. Illud silentium ipsum ōrātiō erat.

Cicerō surrēxit. Tempus tacendī iam nōn erat; tempus agendī vēnerat. Sed nōn statim Catilīnam accūsāvit: prīmum interrogāvit, nam interrogandō ōrātor animōs audientium movet.

"Quō ūsque tandem, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā abūtēris? Ars tua fallendī iam omnibus nōta est. Tū in hōc templō fallendī grātiā sedēs, nōn dē salūte reī pūblicae cōgitandī causā. Ego autem semper ad dēfendendum parātus sum."

Catilīna nihil respondit. Senātōrēs tacēbant. In illō silentiō omnēs intellegēbant Cicerōnem vēra dīcere.

📖 VOCABULARY GLOSS · every word not on your running list, in the form it appears
  • māne (adv.) — in the morning, early
  • ante lūcem — before daylight (literally "before the light")
  • sōlus, -a, -um — alone
  • illō diē — on that day (ablative of time when)
  • ōrātiōnem habēre — to deliver a speech (idiom)
  • parābat — was preparing, was getting ready (parō, parāre; here with an infinitive)
  • tōtam noctem…ēgerat — "he had spent the whole night" (agō with noctem means "spend, pass"; ēgerat is pluperfect)
  • quia — because
  • maximum — very great, greatest (superlative of magnus)
  • Cicerōnī — dative, "to Cicero" — a dative of reference: "the art of speaking was not new to him"
  • iuvenis — "as a young man" (iuvenis, iuvenis m., a young man)
  • studuerat — had studied (pluperfect of studeō, studēre)
  • multōs annōs — for many years (accusative of duration)
  • maius…quam — greater than (neuter comparative of magnus)
  • tandem (adv.) — at last, finally
  • stilum — stylus, writing tool (stilus, stilī m.)
  • pauca verba — a few words
  • nam — for, because
  • memoriā ūtēbantur — "used memory" — ūtor is a deponent from Module 2 and takes the ablative
  • deinde (adv.) — then, next
  • Cūria, Cūriae f. — the Senate House, the Senate's usual meeting building
  • templum, templī n. — temple
  • convēnerat — had assembled (pluperfect of conveniō)
  • ipse — himself, in person (intensive pronoun)
  • aderat — was present (adsum, adesse)
  • nēmō — no one
  • prope (prep. + acc.) — near
  • discesserant — had moved away, had withdrawn (pluperfect of discēdō)
  • sellae — seats, benches (sella, sellae f.)
  • circum (prep. + acc.) — around
  • vacuae — empty (vacuus, -a, -um)
  • silentium, silentiī n. — silence
  • surrēxit — rose, stood up (perfect of surgō)
  • tacendī — gerund of taceō, tacēre, to be silent
  • agendī — gerund of agō, agere, to do, act, drive
  • statim (adv.) — at once, immediately
  • accūsāvit — accused (accūsō, accūsāre)
  • prīmum (adv.) — first, at first
  • interrogāvit, interrogandō — questioned / by questioning (interrogō, interrogāre)
  • animōs audientium — "the minds of those listening" (audientium is the genitive plural of the participle audiēns)
  • quō ūsque tandem — "how long, in the end?" — a set phrase, and the most famous opening in Latin oratory
  • patientiā nostrā — ablative, "our patience" — abūtor governs the ablative, not the accusative
  • abūtēris — "will you go on abusing?" (2nd singular future of abūtor, a deponent from Module 2)
  • nōta est — is known (nōtus, -a, -um, with a dative of the person who knows it: omnibus)
  • hōc templō — in this temple (ablative after in)
  • fallendī — gerund of fallō, fallere, fefellī, falsum, to deceive, cheat
  • autem — however, on the other hand (postpositive — it never comes first)
  • vēra dīcere — "to be telling the truth" (literally "to say true things")
✍️ Three notes about the adaptation, so you know exactly what you are reading.
• The sentences are not Cicero's. This is a simplified retelling built to stay inside the grammar you have. Cicero's real sentences in this speech are far longer, and they use the subjunctive mood constantly — which you begin in Module 6.
• One form was regularized. Cicero actually wrote abūtēre, using an older alternative ending for the 2nd person singular. This retelling writes the regular abūtēris so the form matches what you learned in Module 2. Both are correct Latin; you met the real one in Module 0.
• The temple is historically right. The Senate normally met in the Cūria, but for this session Cicero summoned it to a temple instead — see the callout below.
📜 PART 2 · FOUR REAL WORDS OF CICERO

A few lines into the speech, Cicero interrupts his own argument to cry out. These are his own words, unadapted — four of the most quoted words in Latin:

Ō tempora, ō mōrēs!
Cicero, In Catilīnam I.2 (unadapted)
WordWhat it is
ōAn exclamation — "oh!" Latin uses it to open a cry of outrage or grief.
temporaFrom tempus, temporis n. — not "times" on a clock but "the age we live in." Plural.
mōrēsFrom mōs, mōris m. — "customs, morals, the way people behave." Plural. The Romans' own word for their standards of conduct; mōrēs maiōrum, "the ways of our ancestors," was practically a national creed.
the caseBoth nouns are accusative — Latin's accusative of exclamation, used after ō. There is no verb; there does not need to be one.
"What an age! What behavior!" — or, in the old translation everyone quotes, "O the times, O the morals!"
💡 Why this is worth noticing. Cicero has been building a careful argument, and then he stops and simply shouts. Four words, no verb, no subordination — the opposite of everything else in the speech. That contrast is the point: after a long stretch of controlled prose, a bare exclamation lands like a struck table. Rhetoric is not only about what you build; it is about knowing when to stop building.

🔍 Look at what the passage does twice. Ars dīcendī in paragraph 2 is Cicero's skill. Ars tua fallendī in paragraph 6 is Catiline's. Identical construction, identical shape, opposite content — and the reader is quietly invited to put the two men side by side and compare their arts. Nobody had to say "Catiline is a fraud." The grammar said it.
✍️ PART 4 · TRANSLATE, THEN CHECK

Write your English out on paper before flipping. These four are good practice for the translation assignment.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Tōtam noctem cōgitandō ēgerat.
click to compare
"He had spent the whole night in thinking." Or: "…by thinking." Cōgitandō is a bare ablative gerund — ablative of means. Pluperfect ēgerat puts it before everything else in the story.
Tempus tacendī iam nōn erat; tempus agendī vēnerat.
click to compare
"It was no longer the time for keeping silent; the time for acting had come." Two genitive gerunds, both hanging off tempus, in matched positions. Notice how much shorter the Latin is than the English.
Nam interrogandō ōrātor animōs audientium movet.
click to compare
"For by questioning, a speaker moves the minds of those listening." Interrogandō = ablative of means. Audientium is a participle used as a noun, genitive plural — "of the ones listening."
Tū in hōc templō fallendī grātiā sedēs, nōn dē salūte reī pūblicae cōgitandī causā.
click to compare
"You are sitting in this temple for the sake of deceiving, not for the sake of thinking about the safety of the republic." The whole sentence is one purpose construction set against itself. Remember that grātiā and causā come after their genitives — read backwards from them.
🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · THE EMPTY SEATS

Two details about that morning are worth pausing on, because both of them are arguments made without words.

First, the building. The Senate normally met in the Cūria. For this session Cicero summoned it to a temple instead — the temple of Jupiter Stator, on the slope below the Palatine. The stated reason was security; he had just survived an attempt on his life. But the choice of venue also says something. A meeting held under a god's roof, in a hastily changed location, tells every senator walking in that the situation is not normal before a single word is spoken.

Second, the seats. Catiline came. He was still, technically, a senator, and he took his place. According to Cicero, the men around him got up and moved, leaving the benches beside him bare and empty. We have this detail because Cicero puts it into the speech itself and points at it — which is worth remembering: our source for the scene is the man arguing the case. Still, the picture he gives is a real Roman instrument. Rome did much of its politics through visible, physical signals: where you sat, who walked with you through the Forum, whose door you were seen at in the morning. An empty bench was public information.

The Roman word for it: gravitās. Cicero does not begin by shouting; he begins by asking a question the whole room already knows the answer to. That restraint is gravitās — weight carried, not thrown. And the gerunds are part of the same discipline: fallendī grātiā, cōgitandī causā. He does not call Catiline a traitor. He simply names, in exactly parallel grammar, the two possible reasons a man might be sitting in that room, and lets the empty seats finish the sentence.

LessonThe one thing to keep
4.01A gerund is a verbal noun: infinitive minus -re, plus -nd-, plus neuter singular endings. No nominative — the infinitive does that.
4.02Genitive "of doing" · accusative purpose after ad · ablative "by doing" or "while doing" after in · dative rare.
4.03Two purpose constructions: -ndī causā / grātiā (postpositive!) and ad -ndum. A third, ut + subjunctive, arrives in Module 8.
4.04Not neuter singular, or something agrees with it → gerundive. Otherwise gerund. Recognition only this week.
4.05In real oratory a repeated gerund construction becomes an argument. Grammar is a rhetorical choice.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A gerund turns an action into a thing — and a thing can be weighed. Cicero puts fallendī grātiā and cōgitandī causā in the same shape on purpose, so that the room has to choose between them. That is what gravitās looks like in grammar: naming what you are doing plainly enough that you can be held to it.
