📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
English uses one word for two different jobs. Latin refuses to.
"I like reading." "The reading man." Same English word, two completely different grammatical jobs — one is a noun, one is an adjective. Latin keeps them in separate forms, and the noun one is called the gerund. Once you can see the difference, a whole layer of Cicero opens up.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what a gerund is, and tell it apart from a participle in English before I meet it in Latin.
2
I will be able to build the gerund of a verb in any of the four conjugations.
3
I will be able to say why the gerund has no nominative, and what fills that gap instead.
📚 PART 1 · TEST IT IN ENGLISH FIRST

Before any Latin, get your ear working. English -ing words do at least two jobs, and English does not mark which:

DOING THE JOB OF A NOUN
"I like reading."
Swap in a plain noun and it still works: "I like books." So reading is a noun here. That is a gerund.
DOING THE JOB OF AN ADJECTIVE
"the reading man"
Swap in a plain adjective and it still works: "the tall man." So reading is an adjective here. That is a participle — Latin legēns, which you already know.
✍️ The swap test. If you can replace the -ing word with a noun, it is a gerund. If you can replace it with an adjective, it is a participle. English leaves you guessing; Latin hands you two different forms so you never have to.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
You already own an active participle in Latin (legēns, "reading" as an adjective). What is your first guess about why Latin would also need a noun form of the verb?
📚 PART 2 · THE DEFINITION, AND THE HOLE IN IT
A gerund is a verbal noun — a verb turned into a noun so it can fill a noun's slot in the sentence. It is always neuter, always singular, and it has no nominative.

Three of those facts are easy. The fourth — no nominative — sounds like a defect. It is not. Latin already has a verb-as-noun that handles the nominative: the infinitive.

Dīcere est difficile.
"Speaking is difficult." — or "To speak is difficult."
Dīcere is the subject of est. That is nominative work, and the infinitive does it. Note that difficile is neuter — Latin treats a verbal noun as neuter singular, which is exactly what the gerund is too.

So the two forms are partners, not rivals. The infinitive owns the nominative. The gerund owns every other case:

💡 What the name means. Gerund comes from gerendum, from gerō — "to carry on, to conduct, to do." A gerund is literally "the doing of it." Keep that phrase; it is the fastest translation crutch you will get all module. Dīcendī = "of the doing-of-speaking" → "of speaking."
📚 PART 3 · HOW TO BUILD ONE — THREE STEPS

This is one of the cheapest paradigms in Latin. You need the second principal part and nothing else.

STEP 1
Take the infinitive — the 2nd principal part.
dīcere
STEP 2
Drop -re and add -nd-.
dīce- → dīcend-
STEP 3
Add 2nd-declension neuter singular endings.
dīcendī, dīcendō, dīcendum, dīcendō

The same three steps run through all four conjugations. The only thing that changes is the vowel you are left with after you drop -re — and the 3rd io-stem and 4th conjugations both give you -ie-:

ConjugationInfinitiveGerund stemAccusative (the ad form)
1stamāreamand-ad amandum — for loving

3rd io-stemcaperecapiend-ad capiendum — for seizing
4thaudīreaudiend-ad audiendum — for listening
⚠️ Watch the macron you do not write. Amāre has a long ā. The gerund does not: it is amandī, with a short a, because a vowel shortens before -nd-. Same for monendī from monēre and audiendī from audīre. Writing amāndī is a real error, not a typo — it claims a vowel length Latin does not have there.

And deponents behave normally here. A deponent's second principal part looks passive (loquī, sequī), but its gerund is built the ordinary way from the present stem, and it means what you expect:

★ GRAVITĀS · RESPONSIBILITY

Gravitās comes from gravis, "heavy," and for Romans it named the quality of carrying weight properly — being serious about serious things, and not frivolous with what matters. The gerund is a small grammar lesson in it. A verb is an action rushing past; a gerund freezes that action into a thing — "the doing of it" — and a thing can be picked up, examined, weighed, argued about, and answered for. When Cicero says ars dīcendī, "the art of speaking," he has turned his own talking into an object he can be held responsible for. That is what gravitās asks of you too: stop treating what you do as a blur of events, and name it as a thing you own.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A gerund is a verbal noun: infinitive minus -re, plus -nd-, plus neuter singular endings. It is neuter, singular, and has no nominative — because the infinitive already does that job. Translate it as "the doing of it," then tidy the English.
