Lesson 4.04
Gerund or Gerundive?
Recognition Skill
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
There is a second -nd- form. It is not a noun.
Latin's -nd- suffix builds two different things: the gerund (a noun — "the doing of it") and the gerundive (an adjective — "to be done"). They share a suffix and nothing else. Today's job is recognition only: you learn to tell which one you are looking at. Deploying the gerundive is Module 5's business.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state the one grammatical difference between a gerund and a gerundive.
2
I will be able to apply the agreement test to any -nd- form I meet in a sentence.
3
I will be able to explain why Latin swaps to a gerundive when an object gets involved.
📚 PART 1 · TWO SPECIES, ONE SUFFIX
GERUND · A NOUN
dīcendī, dīcendō, dīcendum
• Neuter singular only
• No nominative
• Agrees with nothing — it has its own case for its own reason
• Means "the doing of it"
ars dīcendī — the art of speaking
GERUNDIVE · AN ADJECTIVE
dīcendus, dīcenda, dīcendum
• All three genders, both numbers, all cases
• Declines exactly like bonus, bona, bonum
• Agrees with a noun in gender, number, and case
• Means "to be done," "needing to be done"
ad urbem dēfendendam — for the city to be defended = for defending the city
💡 You already met the gerundive yesterday, in English. Agenda = "things to be done." Memorandum = "a thing to be remembered." Those are gerundives — feminine plural and neuter singular respectively — and the "to be ___ed" in the translation is the gerundive's fingerprint. A gerund never translates that way.
🔍 PART 2 · THE THREE-SECOND TEST

Run these in order. You will almost always stop at step 1 or step 2.

1
Is the form anything other than neuter singular? Plural? Feminine? Masculine? Then it is a gerundive — instantly, no further thought. A gerund has exactly four forms and all of them are neuter singular.
Examples: dēfendendam (fem. sg.), scrībendīs (pl.), vincendōs (masc. pl.) — all gerundives.
2
Is there a noun right there in the same gender, number, and case? Then it is agreeing with that noun, which makes it a gerundive.
Example: cōnsilium urbis capiendae — capiendae matches urbis (feminine genitive singular). Gerundive.
3
Still nothing to agree with? Then it is standing on its own as a noun — a gerund. Translate it "the doing of it," then tidy up.
Example: ad discendum — neuter singular, nothing to agree with, governed by ad. Gerund.

✍️ An honest exception. A few phrases are genuinely undecidable from the form alone — ad bellum gerendum, "for waging war," could be a neuter gerundive agreeing with bellum or a gerund with bellum as its object. Latinists argue about it. The good news: the meaning is the same either way, so it never costs you a translation. Do not lose sleep over the ambiguous cases; the test handles the other ninety-five percent.
📚 PART 3 · WHY LATIN SWAPS TO THE GERUNDIVE

Here is the reason the two forms keep bumping into each other. Suppose you want to say "for the sake of defending the city." The gerund is a noun, and a noun cannot comfortably take a direct object — so Latin does something clever: it drops the gerund and puts the gerundive on the object instead.

WHAT YOU MIGHT EXPECT
urbem dēfendendī causā
Gerund plus an accusative object. Latin avoids this.
WHAT LATIN WRITES
urbis dēfendendae causā
The object urbs takes the case the phrase needs (genitive), and the gerundive agrees with it. Same meaning: "for the sake of defending the city."

Look at what happened: the words swapped jobs. The action word became an adjective; the object word took over the case. English cannot do this at all, which is why the literal translation ("for the sake of the city to-be-defended") sounds so strange. Translate it as if it were the ordinary gerund phrase and you will be right every time.

⚠️ Your rule for Module 4. When you write Latin this week, use gerunds without direct objects — ad dīcendum, audiendī causā, legendō. Nothing on this module's quiz or translation requires you to produce a gerundive. You are only being asked to recognize one when it walks past.
💡 And one more gerundive job, coming next week. Put a gerundive together with a form of esse and it stops meaning "to be done" and starts meaning "must be done" — the shape -ndus/-nda/-ndum + est. That is the gerundive of obligation, and it is the whole of Module 5. Not yet studied; you will not meet it on this module's quiz.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A gerund is a neuter singular noun that agrees with nothing. A gerundive is an adjective that agrees with a noun in gender, number, and case. So: if it is not neuter singular, it is a gerundive — and if something matches it, it is a gerundive. Everything else is a gerund.
