Lesson 4.03
Two Ways to Say Purpose
Grammar Core · Vocabulary
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
"In order to" — the phrase Latin has three different ways of saying.
Two of the three use a gerund, and you can learn both today: causā + genitive and ad + accusative. Together they cover most expressions of purpose you will meet in Cicero's prose. The third way is coming, and you should know now that it exists.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to build and translate a purpose phrase with causā or grātiā plus a genitive gerund.
2
I will be able to build and translate a purpose phrase with ad plus an accusative gerund.
3
I will be able to give the meaning of eleven new words from Cicero's political and legal vocabulary.
📚 PART 1 · CAUSĀ AND GRĀTIĀ — "FOR THE SAKE OF"

Two words, one meaning. Causā ("for the reason of") and grātiā ("for the favor of") both mean "for the sake of." Both take the genitive — and both do something unusual with word order:

Causā and grātiā are postpositive: they come after the genitive they govern, not before it.
WHAT ENGLISH WORD ORDER WANTS
causā dīcendī
Not how Latin normally writes it.
WHAT LATIN ACTUALLY WRITES
dīcendī causā
"for the sake of speaking."
✍️ The reading strategy this gives you. When your eye lands on causā or grātiā in a sentence, do not read forward — look back. The genitive you need is the word immediately before it, and if that word ends in -ndī, you are holding a purpose phrase.
Cicerō ad Cūriam dīcendī causā festīnāvit.
"Cicero hurried to the Senate House for the sake of speaking" — that is, in order to speak.
Note the two ad-free layers: ad Cūriam is plain motion toward a place; dīcendī causā is the purpose. Gloss: Cūria, Cūriae f. — the Senate House; festīnō, festīnāre — to hurry.
Catilīna fallendī grātiā in senātū sedēbat.
"Catiline was sitting in the Senate for the sake of deceiving."
Grātiā works exactly like causā; Cicero uses both. Gloss: fallō, fallere, fefellī, falsum — to deceive, cheat.
📚 PART 2 · AD + ACCUSATIVE — "FOR THE PURPOSE OF"

The other one is easier, because ad behaves like the ordinary preposition you have always known: it takes the accusative and it goes in front.

Cicerō ad dīcendum surrēxit.
"Cicero rose to speak" — literally "for the purpose of speaking."
Gloss: surrēxit — "rose, stood up" (perfect of surgō).
Mīlitēs ad pugnandum parātī erant.
"The soldiers were ready for fighting."
Adjectives of readiness and fitness — parātus, aptus, prōmptus — regularly take ad + gerund. Learn this pairing; it is everywhere.
ConstructionCase of the gerundFeel of it
dīcendī causā / grātiāGenitive, word afterLeans toward motive — why someone is doing this at all.
ad dīcendumAccusative, word beforeLeans toward immediate aim — what this particular action is for.
💡 Do not oversell the difference. In real Latin the two overlap heavily, and often either would do. The nuance above is a tendency, not a rule — useful for choosing when you write, not for scoring points when you read. Both translate comfortably as "in order to."
📚 PART 3 · THE THIRD WAY, AND WHY WE ARE TELLING YOU EARLY

Latin has one more way of expressing purpose, and it is actually the most common of the three: a full clause with ut and a verb in the subjunctive mood. Something like Cicerō surrēxit ut dīceret — "Cicero rose so that he might speak."

⚠️ Not yet studied — and not on this module's quiz. Dīceret is an imperfect subjunctive, and the subjunctive mood arrives in Module 6. Purpose clauses with ut and nē are taught in Module 8, which is a mastery gate. You will not be asked to read or write one before then, and nothing on the Module 4 quiz or translation requires it.

We are telling you now for one reason: so that when Module 8 arrives you recognize it as the third member of a family you already know, not as a bolt from the blue. Latin lets a speaker choose — a tight gerund phrase, or a full clause with its own verb. Cicero chooses deliberately, and by Module 8 you will be able to see why.

📜 DERIVATIVE MINUTE · THE -ND- FAMILY IN ENGLISH

You have been reading Latin -nd- forms in English your whole life without knowing it. English borrowed them whole, endings and all:

Latin formLiterallyEnglish word
agenda — from agō, to do"things to be done"agenda — still exactly a list of things to be done
memorandum — from memorō, to recall"a thing to be remembered"memorandum, shortened to memo
referendum — from referō, to carry back"a thing to be referred back"referendum — a question referred back to the voters
legenda — from legō, to read"things to be read"legend — originally a saint's life, read aloud
addendum — from addō, to add"a thing to be added"addendum, plural addenda
dīvidendum — from dīvidō, to divide"a thing to be divided up"dividend — profit to be divided among shareholders

Notice the translations. Every one of them came out as "a thing to be done / remembered / read." That is not what a gerund means — a gerund is "the doing of it." These English words are actually borrowed gerundives, the gerund's twin. Which is exactly tomorrow's lesson, and it is the best possible reason to learn to tell the two apart.

🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · CICERO THE ORATOR — NOT THE LETTER-WRITER

You have met Cicero before. Last year, in Latin Intermediate, you read his letters — a man writing privately to a friend, complaining about the weather, worrying about money, dropping Greek phrases the way you might drop slang. That Cicero is chatty, unguarded, and often funny. Keep him; he is real. But he is not the Cicero of this semester.

This semester you meet Cicero on his feet, in public, with the state at stake. It is a genuinely different register — not just louder, but built differently. Long sentences that hold their shape to the end. Questions instead of accusations. Direct address to a man sitting in the room. Sound patterns and rhythm chosen so the ear cannot look away. If a letter is a person talking, an ōrātiō is a person building.

He earned it. As a young man he went east and studied rhetoric and philosophy in Greece and on Rhodes; back in Rome he built a career on the strength of his voice alone, in a city where almost everyone who mattered had inherited their position and he had not. He later wrote treatises about how the whole craft works — Dē Ōrātōre is the most famous. When Romans said ars dīcendī, the art of speaking, Cicero was the name attached to it.

Why this matters for grammar. A speech that has to land in one hearing cannot afford loose joints. That is why oratory is full of gerunds: dīcendī causā, ad dēfendendum — compact, weighted, balanced, and easy for a listener to hold. The grammar you learn this week is not decoration on the history. It is the machinery of the history.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Two purpose constructions, both meaning "in order to": -ndī causā or -ndī grātiā (genitive, and the governing word comes after), and ad -ndum (accusative, preposition in front). A third way — ut + subjunctive — is real, is coming in Module 8, and is not your problem yet.
