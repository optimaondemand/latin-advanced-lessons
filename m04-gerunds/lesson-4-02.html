Lesson 4.02
The Gerund in All Its Cases
Grammar Core
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Four cases. Three endings. Nothing new to memorize.
A gerund declines exactly like bellum in the singular — you have known those endings since your first year. What is new is what each case means when the noun in question is an action. Today you learn one job per case, with one worked sentence each.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to decline a gerund through its four cases and say what each case is for.
2
I will be able to translate ars dīcendī, ad dīcendum, and dīcendō without stopping to think.
3
I will be able to use context to decide whether -ndō is dative or ablative.
📚 PART 1 · THE WHOLE PARADIGM, ON ONE LINE

Model verb: dīcō, dīcere — "to speak, to say." Neuter singular, second declension, no nominative:

CaseFormCore jobWorked example
Nominative— none —Use the infinitive insteadDīcere est difficile. — Speaking is difficult.
Genitivedīcendī"of speaking" — and purpose with causā/grātiāars dīcendī — the art of speaking
Dativedīcendō"for speaking" — rare; see Part 4Recognize it; you will not have to produce it.
AccusativedīcendumAfter a preposition — nearly always ad, for purposead dīcendum — for the purpose of speaking
Ablativedīcendō"by speaking" (means), or after in, dē, exdīcendō — by speaking; in dīcendō — in the act of speaking
🎵 MEMORY WORK · SAY IT ALOUD THREE TIMES
dīcendī, dīcendō, dīcendum, dīcendō —
of speaking, for speaking, for speaking, by speaking

Say the Latin and the English together. Four slots, and only three different sounds — which is your warning that the fourth slot is going to need context.

📚 PART 2 · THE GENITIVE — "OF DOING"

This is the gerund's home. It attaches to a noun and says what that noun is about:

Ars dīcendī est difficilis.
"The art of speaking is difficult."
Dīcendī is genitive, depending on ars. Grammarians call this an objective genitive: the art is directed at speaking. Same shape as amor patriae, "love of country."
Tempus tacendī iam nōn erat; tempus agendī vēnerat.
"It was no longer the time for keeping silent; the time for acting had come."
Two genitive gerunds hanging off tempus, perfectly balanced. This is how Cicero thinks. Gloss: taceō, tacēre — to be silent; agō, agere — to do, act, drive.
✍️ Nouns that love a genitive gerund. ars (art, skill) · tempus (time) · cupiditās (desire) · studium (enthusiasm) · occāsiō (opportunity) · facultās (capacity). When you meet one of these and then hit an -ndī, you already know what is happening: cupiditās pugnandī, "a desire for fighting."

The genitive has a second, even more common job — expressing purpose with the words causā and grātiā. That gets a whole lesson of its own tomorrow, because it is one of the two constructions you will meet on every page of Cicero.

📚 PART 3 · ACCUSATIVE AND ABLATIVE

Accusative — almost always after ad

A gerund in the accusative shows up as the object of a preposition, and the preposition is overwhelmingly ad. Ad + gerund means purpose: "for the purpose of doing," "in order to do."

Senātōrēs ad audiendum convēnērunt.
"The senators gathered to listen." (Literally: "for the purpose of listening.")
Gloss: conveniō, convenīre, convēnī, conventum — to come together, assemble.
Cicerō ad discendum in Graeciam nāvigāvit.
"Cicero sailed to Greece for learning — in order to learn."
True, by the way: Cicero really did study rhetoric in Greece and on Rhodes as a young man. Gloss: discō, discere, didicī — to learn; nāvigō, nāvigāre — to sail.

Ablative — means, or after a preposition

A bare ablative gerund is an ablative of means — exactly the same idea as gladiō, "with a sword." Only here the instrument is an action:

Cicerō dīcendō senātōrēs mōvit.
"Cicero moved the senators by speaking."
No preposition, so read it as means. Compare Cicerō verbīs senātōrēs mōvit, "by words" — identical grammar, ordinary noun.
In legendō multa discimus.
"In reading we learn many things."
With in, the sense shifts from "by means of" to "in the course of, while." Watch for in, dē, and ex in front of an -ndō.
💡 English does this too, and you never noticed. "You get better by practicing." "In writing this letter I realized something." Those are ablative-of-means and in-plus-ablative gerunds in English clothing. Latin just marks them with an ending instead of a preposition.
📚 PART 4 · THE DATIVE, BRIEFLY AND HONESTLY

The dative gerund exists, and it is rare. You should be able to recognize the ending and move on; this course will not ask you to produce one. It turns up mostly in fixed official and legal phrases, where it means "for the purpose of":

Ille solvendō nōn erat.
"He was not up to paying" — the Roman legal way of saying he was insolvent.
A set idiom, solvendō esse, with a dative gerund. Treat it as vocabulary, not as a pattern to copy. Gloss: solvō, solvere — to loosen, pay, settle a debt.
⚠️ Dative and ablative share the form -ndō. That is not a problem you solve by staring harder at the word — it is a problem you solve by reading around it:
in dīcendō
Preposition in in front → must be ablative.
dīcendō senātōrēs mōvit
No preposition, and the sense is "by" → ablative of means.
Ninety-nine times out of a hundred, an -ndō in your reading is an ablative. Start there.
📜 One famous Roman title, and a warning. Rome once appointed a commission called the decemvirī lēgibus scrībendīs — "the ten men for writing the laws." (Our oldest sources spell it with an older ending, scrībundīs; the classical spelling is the one above.) It looks like a dative gerund, but look closer: scrībendīs is plural, agreeing with lēgibus. A gerund is always neuter singular, so this cannot be one. It is the gerundive — the adjective twin you meet on Thursday and study properly in Module 5. File the phrase away; on Thursday you will be able to explain it.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

One job per case: genitive = "of doing," hanging off a noun · accusative = purpose, after ad · ablative = "by doing," or "while doing" after in · dative = rare, recognize and move on. And a gerund is never plural — if it looks plural, it is not a gerund.
