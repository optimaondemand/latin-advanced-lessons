1
I will be able to run the five-step sorting procedure, in order, on any Latin sentence I meet.
2
I will be able to name all four jobs of nē and all four jobs of ut, and say which negative belongs to which construction.
3

📚 PART 1 · THE FIVE-STEP PROCEDURE

Run these in order on every sentence. Notice that steps 1 to 3 need no understanding of the sentence at all — they are pure reading of forms and words. Only step 4 asks anything about meaning, and by then you have usually already finished.

1
Find every verb. Is any of them subjunctive?
No → you are in indicative territory and nothing from Modules 8–15 applies. Look for: indirect statement (accusative + infinitive), a relative clause, cum/quamquam/quia + indicative, or a simple condition. Stop.
Yes → go to step 2, once for each subjunctive.
2
Is there a subordinating word in front of this subjunctive? (ut, nē, cum, sī, nisi, or a question word.)
No → it is a main-clause use. Read the person: 3rd person → jussive. 1st plural → hortatory. Utinam anywhere → optative. A question → deliberative. Stop.
Yes → go to step 3.
3
Read the subordinating word. Three of the four possibilities finish the job here:
sī or nisi → a condition. The tense names the type: present subjunctive = future less vivid; imperfect = contrary to fact now; pluperfect = contrary to fact in the past. Stop.
cum → a cum clause. Tamen in the main clause → concessive. Otherwise ask whether the clause gives the reason: yes → causal, no → temporal. Stop.
a question word — quis, quid, cūr, ubi, quandō, quōmodo, quot, quantus, uter, num, -ne, utrum…an → an indirect question. Stop.
ut or nē → go to step 4. This is where the work is.
4
It is ut or nē. Look at the MAIN VERB before you look at anything else. Then, in this order:
(a) Is the main verb a verb of fearing — timeō, vereor, metuō, or the phrase metus est? → fear clause, and nē means “that [it will happen].” Stop.
(b) Is it a verb of ordering, urging or asking — imperō, persuādeō, moneō, rogō, ōrō, hortor, cēnseō? → indirect command. Stop.
(c) Is there a signpost in the main clause — tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō? → result clause. Stop.
(d) None of the above? → purpose clause. This is the default, and it is right most of the time.
5
Only now look at the tense. The tense of a subordinate subjunctive tells you when, never what: run it against the sequence grid from 16.03 and read off same-time or already-finished. A student who tries to identify a construction from the tense of its verb will be wrong roughly as often as they are right, because all five subordinate constructions use all four tenses.
✍️ Why step 4 reads the main verb first. Because nē alone is ambiguous and the main verb is what disambiguates it. Nē veniat means “so that he may not come” after tacuit, “not to come” after monuit, “that he will come” after timuit, and “let him not come” after nothing at all. The two letters do not change. The verb above them does.
📚 PART 2 · THREE WORKED WALK-THROUGHS

Watch the steps run. Say them under your breath as you read; the habit is the point, not the answers.

Cum senātus cōnstituisset, cōnsul timēbat nē quis coniūrātōs līberāret.
1. Verbs: cōnstituisset, timēbat, līberāret. Two subjunctives (cōnstituisset, līberāret) and one indicative main verb.  2. Both subjunctives have a word in front of them, so neither is a main-clause use.  3. First word: cum → a cum clause. No tamen; does it give the reason? The Senate's decision is why he was afraid → causal. Second word: nē → go to step 4.  4a. Main verb timēbat — a verb of fearing → fear clause. Stop.  5. Tenses: cōnstituisset pluperfect (already finished, secondary row), līberāret imperfect (same time, secondary row). Both correct against timēbat.
“Since the Senate had decided, the consul was afraid that someone would set the conspirators free.” Gloss: līberō, līberāre — to set free; quis after nē = anyone.
Faciāmus quod officium poscit; nē rēs pūblica pereat.
1. Subjunctives: faciāmus, pereat. (Poscit is present indicative, inside a relative clause.)  2. Faciāmus has nothing in front of it → main-clause use. Person? 1st plural → hortatory. Stop. Now pereat: nē in front of it → go on.  3. Nē → step 4.  4. Main verb above it is faciāmus: not a verb of fearing, not a verb of ordering, no signpost → purpose clause (d).
“Let us do what duty demands, so that the republic may not perish.” Gloss: poscō, poscere — to demand; pereō, perīre — to perish (a compound of eō).
Cicerō sēnsit coniūrātōs manifestōs esse, nec dubitāvit quid faciendum esset.
1. Verbs: sēnsit (perfect indicative), esse (infinitive), dubitāvit (perfect indicative), esset (imperfect subjunctive). So one subjunctive only — and the first half of the sentence has none at all.  1, first half. No subjunctive → indicative territory: accusative coniūrātōs manifestōs + infinitive esse → indirect statement. Stop.  2, second half. Esset has quid in front of it → not a main-clause use.  3. Quid is a question word → indirect question. Stop. And faciendum esset is a gerundive of obligation from Module 5, sitting inside it.
“Cicero realised that the conspirators were caught in the act, and did not hesitate about what had to be done.” Gloss: manifestus, -a, -um — plain, caught in the act; nec — and… not.
💡 Notice what all three had in common. Each sentence held two different constructions, and in every case the procedure handled them one at a time, in the order they appeared, without ever needing to know what the sentence as a whole was about. Long Latin sentences are not harder than short ones. They are just more of the same job.
📚 PART 3 · EVERY JOB THAT NĒ AND UT DO

This is the table. If you can reproduce it on a blank sheet of paper, you will not lose points to a nē or an ut for the rest of your life in Latin. Nine rows — four for nē, four for ut, and one for the negatives that are neither.

WordConstructionWhat tells you it is this oneWhat it means, with an example
nē1. Negative purpose
m08		ordinary main verb; no signpost“in order that… not, so that… not”
Tacuit nē Catilīna suspicārētur. — He kept quiet so that Catiline would not suspect.
nē2. Negative indirect command
m08		main verb of ordering, urging, asking“told/urged someone not to…”
Cīvēs monuit nē timērent. — He warned the citizens not to be afraid.
nē3. Fear clause — the reversal
m12		main verb of fearing: timeō, vereor, metuō, metus est“afraid that [it will happen]” — the opposite of row 1
Timēbat nē venīret. — He was afraid that he would come.
Timēbat nē nōn venīret. — He was afraid that he would not come.
nē4. Negative main-clause subjunctive — but only three of the four uses
m14		nothing subordinates the verb, and the verb is a jussive, hortatory or optative — something willed“let him not…, let us not…, I wish… not”
Nē veniat. — Let him not come. · Nē cūnctēmur. — Let us not hesitate. · Utinam nē vēnisset. — If only he had not come.
The deliberative question is the exception — see row 9.
ut5. Purpose
m08		ordinary main verb; no signpost in the main clause“in order that, so that, to…”
Vēnit ut populus audīret. — He came so that the people might hear.
ut6. Indirect command
m08		main verb of ordering, urging, asking“told/urged someone to…”
Senātōribus imperāvit ut cōnstituerent. — He ordered the senators to decide. Imperō takes the dative.
ut7. Result
m09		a signpost in the main clause: tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō“so… that [it actually happened]”
Tanta erat audācia ut nēmō tacēret. — So great was the boldness that no one kept silent.
ut8. Fear clause (rare)
m12		main verb of fearing“afraid that… not” — yes, the reverse of row 5
Timeō ut veniat. — I am afraid that he will not come. Uncommon; nē nōn is the usual way.
nōn9. Everything else's negative — including the deliberative question
m09–m11, m14, m15		—ut nōn = negative result, and only result. Plain nōn inside the clause negates a cum clause, an indirect question, a condition — and a deliberative question.
Tam foedus erat ut nēmō aspicere posset. · Cum nōn respondēret… · Rogāvit cūr nōn vēnisset. · Sī senātus nōn cōnstituisset… · An ego nōn venīrem? — “Was I not to come?”
Compress the whole table into three lines and you have the exam answer:
1. Purpose and indirect command negate with nē. Result negates with ut nōn. Never swap them.
2. After a verb of fearing, nē flips: it means “that,” and “that… not” becomes nē nōn.
3. A main-clause subjunctive that wills something — jussive, hortatory, optative — negates with nē. A deliberative question wills nothing, so it negates with nōn.
💡 Line 3 is worth understanding rather than memorising, because the reason is short. Nē is the negative of wanting. Every construction in rows 1 to 4 has somebody wanting something: a purpose is wanted, an order is wanted, a fear is a want turned inside out, and a jussive, hortatory or optative is a want said straight out. A deliberative question wants nothing — it is asking what is to be done, and it has not decided yet. With no wish inside it, there is nothing for nē to negate, so it takes the ordinary nōn like any other question: An ego nōn venīrem?, “Was I not to come?” Three uses plus one exception, and the exception is the one that is only asking.
⚠️ One honest gap in the table, so that you are not ambushed later. In real Latin ut also appears with the indicative, meaning “as” or “when” — ut dīxī, “as I said.” That use is not taught in this course and will not appear on any assessment in it. If you meet it in a reading passage next semester it will be glossed for you. The test that keeps you safe is the one you already have: ut + indicative is never any of rows 5 to 8, because every construction in this table requires a subjunctive.
🧠 PART 4 · THE UNLABELLED PILE — ALL OF MODULES 8 TO 15

Nine sentences. No labels, no order, no warning. Every construction from Module 8 to Module 15 is in here somewhere, and one of them has no subjunctive in it at all. Run the five steps on each sentence before you read the options — reading the options first is how you learn to recognise instead of learning to sort.

📜 PART 5 · SPEED SORT · CLICK EACH CARD

Fragments, not sentences. Name the construction out loud, then flip. The target is about a second each — and the Lūdus tomorrow will ask for exactly that speed.

🃏 FLIP CARDS · NAME THE CONSTRUCTION BEFORE YOU FLIP
tacuit nē suspicārētur
which job of nē?
Negative purpose (row 1). Ordinary main verb, no signpost.
timēbat nē venīret
which job of nē?
Fear clause (row 3). “Afraid that he would come.” The reversal.
monuit nē timērent
which job of nē?
Negative indirect command (row 2). Moneō is a verb of warning.
nē cūnctēmur
which job of nē?
Negative hortatory (row 4). Nothing subordinates it; 1st plural.
tam foedus ut nēmō posset
which job of ut?
Result (row 7). Tam is the signpost; the negative is nōn inside.
vēnit ut audīrent
which job of ut?
Purpose (row 5). No signpost, ordinary main verb.
imperāvit ut cōnstituerent
which job of ut?
Indirect command (row 6). And imperō takes the dative.
timeō nē nōn veniat
what does this mean?
Fear that… not. “I am afraid he will not come.” Two negatives, one fear.
utinam vēnisset
which construction?
Optative, past. “If only he had come.” Pluperfect = a regret.
quid faciam?
which construction?
Deliberative. “What am I to do?” A question, main clause, no subordinator.
an ego nōn venīrem?
why nōn and not nē?
Deliberative — the one main-clause use that takes nōn. “Was I not to come?” Nē negates something willed; a deliberative only asks, so there is no wish for nē to negate.
sī dīxisset, errāvisset
which condition?
Contrary to fact, past. Pluperfect subjunctive both halves. He did not say it.
sī dīcat, erret — or sī dīcat, errat?
which is right, and which condition?
Sī dīcat, erret — future less vivid needs the present subjunctive in both halves. Mixing a subjunctive with an indicative is the commonest condition error.
cum nōn respondēret
which construction, and why nōn?
Causal or temporal cum. A cum clause negates with nōn, never nē and never ut nōn.
dīxit coniūrātōs fūgisse
which construction?
Indirect statement. Accusative + infinitive, no subjunctive at all. Step 1 stops you.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Subjunctive? Subordinated? Which word? Then the main verb. Then the tense. Five questions, always in that order. Four of the five are answered by reading words off the page, which means four of the five cannot be got wrong by someone who actually asks them. The whole skill of reading Latin prose is asking cheap questions before expensive ones.
