📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
There is no new grammar this week. That is not a holiday — it is the harder job.
Fifteen modules ago you could not read a subjunctive. You now own five constructions that put a subjunctive in a subordinate clause, four that put one in a main clause, four subjunctive tenses in two sequences, conditions, deponents, gerunds, gerundives — and one construction that reports without any subjunctive at all. This week you put all of it in one place, and then you show it. Today: what you actually have, what the gate at the end of the week is, and how to revise in a way that works.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to say exactly what the Module 16 gate asks of me, what score it needs, and how many attempts I get.
2
I will be able to name the four blocks of Semester 1 — the forms, the subordinate subjunctive, the main-clause subjunctive, and the constructions that use no subjunctive — without looking at a list.
3
I will be able to choose a revision method that makes me produce Latin instead of one that only makes me re-read it.

💡 Four blocks, not twelve modules. Everything in that table sorts into four piles, and this is the shape the rest of the week uses: (1) the forms — nouns, verbs, deponents, gerund and gerundive, reviewed in 16.02. (2) The subordinate subjunctive — purpose, result, cum, indirect question, fear, reviewed in 16.03. (3) The main-clause subjunctive — jussive, hortatory, optative, deliberative, also 16.03. (4) The constructions with no subjunctive in them at all — indirect statement, relative clauses, quamquam and quia, simple conditions. That fourth pile is the one students forget exists, and it is where half the mistakes on the gate come from.
📚 PART 2 · THE GATE, PLAINLY

You met one of these at Module 8. Here is the second and last one in this semester, stated with nothing hidden:

The Module 16 Quiz is a mastery gate. You need 32 out of 40 — 80% — to move on to Module 17.
· Attempts: unlimited. There is no cap and no penalty.
· Canvas keeps your highest score. A weak first attempt cannot hurt you.
· Answers and feedback are shown after your last attempt, with an explanation of every distractor.
· Until the 32 is met, Module 17 — the capstone and the Semester Final — stays locked.

Why it exists, honestly. The Semester Final in Module 17 is cumulative and it is worth 10% of your grade. Nobody should walk into a cumulative exam without knowing whether they are ready for it. The gate is the check that happens while you can still do something about it. A first attempt that comes back 26/40 is not a bad grade — it is a list, in order, of what to spend Wednesday and Thursday on. That is worth more to you than a comfortable 40.

✍️ What the quiz is not. It is not twenty hard sentences. Twenty items, forty points, and they are spread deliberately thin across the whole semester: every construction from Module 2 to Module 15 shows up somewhere, most of them once. It rewards breadth, which means it rewards exactly the kind of revision this week is built around — and it means you cannot pass it by knowing one construction extremely well.

📚 PART 3 · HOW TO REVISE — FOUR MOVES

Re-reading a lesson page feels like revision and is almost worthless. The reason is simple: when you read an explanation you already half-know, your brain recognises it and reports back “yes, I know this” — and recognising is not the same skill as producing. Every one of the four moves below forces production instead.

1
Produce before you look. Cover the answer. Say the form out loud. Then check. If you looked first, you did not revise — you read.
Example: say the 3rd singular pluperfect subjunctive of loquor out loud right now, then look: locūtus esset.
2
Sort things that are not labelled. The reason 16.04 exists. A drill that tells you “here are six fear clauses” teaches you almost nothing, because real Latin never announces the construction before the sentence. Practise on unlabelled piles only.
3
Say paradigms aloud, in rhythm. Amem, amēs, amet — amēmus, amētis, ament. This is the oldest study technique in the subject and it works because sound is stored differently from sight. Four tenses of one verb takes ninety seconds.
4
Work from your own table, not from the page. By Friday you should be able to draw the nē/ut table from 16.04 on a blank sheet. If you can draw it, you own it. If you can only recognise it, you do not — and the gate is built to tell the difference.
⚠️ One thing not to do this week. Do not try to revise all thirteen modules evenly. You do not need to. Take the diagnostic below, find your two or three weak spots, and spend the week on those — then use the Lūdus on Thursday for breadth. A student who revises everything a little revises nothing enough.

Where: the battlefield in the ager Pistoriēnsis, the narrow valley in the hills north of Rome where Catiline drew up his line and died in January 62 BC — the end of the story you have been reading all semester. [ARTHUR: scene link and entry steps]

Do this: label at least six things in the scene in Latin, with the case ending right for what the label is doing — and write a short Latin reflection that contains one contrary-to-fact condition: “if Catiline had not…” That is the Module 15 grammar, and this is where it goes to work. Full instructions, the vocabulary list and the rubric are on the assignment itself. Start it early in the week; it is the one piece of graded work this module that is not a test.

★ CŌNSTANTIA · PERSEVERANCE

Cōnstantia, cōnstantiae f. is steadiness — standing firm, holding a course, being the same person on Thursday that you were on Monday. It is built from cum (“with, together”) plus stō, stāre (“to stand”): literally standing together with yourself. English gets constant, constancy, and the mathematician's constant from it, and Module 1 named it at the beginning of this semester — which is why it comes back now, at the halfway mark, rather than at the end.

The Romans did not treat cōnstantia as a heroic virtue. It was the unglamorous one: the quality of a man who kept turning up. And it is exactly the virtue a review week asks for, because nothing in this module is exciting. There is no new construction to be surprised by. There is a table to draw from memory, four paradigms to say out loud, and one pile of unlabelled sentences to sort until sorting them is boring. Fifteen weeks ago you could not read a subjunctive. You did not become able to by having a good week — you became able to by having fifteen ordinary ones. That is cōnstantia, and it is the whole reason there is anything to review.

Recognising is not producing. Every hour of revision this week should end with Latin you made — a form said aloud, a sentence written, a table drawn on a blank sheet. If you finish a study session having only read, do one of those three things before you close the tab. It takes two minutes and it is the difference between the gate and the gate on the third try.
