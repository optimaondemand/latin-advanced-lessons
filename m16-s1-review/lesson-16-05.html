📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
All semester you have read two men on one year. Today they are on the same page.
Sallust wrote about 63 BC roughly twenty years after it happened, as a historian with his own view of what had gone wrong with Rome. Cicero spoke during 63 BC as the consul who had to decide what to do that week. Today's passage sets the two voices side by side — and because they were doing different things with language, between them they use nearly every construction you own. The reading is the review. Then the whole semester in one table, one last mixed self-check, and the handoff to your graded work — including the gate.

📚 PART 1 · BEFORE YOU READ

Three things about the two voices. The passage assumes all three.

1. They are not the same kind of writing. Cicero's speeches were delivered, to persuade a room, and then published by him. Sallust's monograph was written, decades later, to explain to readers what had gone wrong with the republic. An orator asks questions, urges, warns and appeals — which is why Cicero's half of the passage is full of commands, wishes and reported questions. A historian reports, judges and weighs — which is why Sallust's half is full of indirect statement and looking back.
2. Sallust wrote after both men were dead. Cicero was killed in 43 BC. Sallust's Bellum Catilīnae was written after that, in the early years of the next civil war. So Sallust could see how the story ended and Cicero could not — and that difference in vantage point is a difference you can hear in the grammar. Looking back at a finished past is what the pluperfect subjunctive is for.
3. Sallust admired Caesar and Cato, and gave Cicero almost nothing. In the middle of his account of the debate he stops to compare the two men he thought were the last Romans of real quality, Caesar and Cato — different in character, alike in virtūs. The consul who chaired that meeting gets no speech at all. Cicero, meanwhile, published his own speeches from the same week. Two accounts survive of one December, and neither of them is neutral.
💡 Read for the constructions, and count them. There are at least twelve different ones in fourteen lines: cum clause, indirect question, indirect statement, quamquam, gerund, gerundive of obligation with a dative of agent, past contrary-to-fact condition, fear clause, indirect command, result clause, hortatory, deliberative question, future less vivid, optative. Every one of them was a module. That is the point of the passage.
📜 PART 2 · THE READING
A synthesis passage composed for this course, adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 5, 53–54 and 60–61 and Cicero, In Catilīnam III–IV. Neither half is Sallust's or Cicero's own Latin. The judgments in Part I are Sallust's and the appeals in Part II are Cicero's, but the sentences have been written from scratch for the grammar you have been taught, and two lines (5 and 14) are the course author's own additions and are flagged below. This is an adaptation, not a text.

I · SALLUSTIUS SCRĪBIT — the historian, twenty years later

1Cum Sallustius bellum Catilīnae scrīberet, plūrimī iam annī praeterierant.

2Nōn ignōrābat quantum perīculum rēs pūblica illō annō passa esset.

3Duōs virōs magnā virtūte fuisse scrībit, Caesarem et Catōnem, quamquam mōribus dissimilēs erant.

4Catilīnam autem cupiditāte rēgnandī captum esse ait; neque eum laudat neque excūsat.

5Nōs tamen hoc cōgitāre possumus: sī ille hanc vim animī reī pūblicae dedisset, magnam glōriam adeptus esset.

6Sed verērī dēbēmus nē tantum scelus excūsēmus.

II · CICERŌ CŌNSUL DĪXIT — the consul, at the time

7Cicerō autem, cum cōnsul esset, aliter loquēbātur.

8Rogābat utrum lēnitās an sevēritās reī pūblicae magis ūtilis esset.

9Quid faciam? Quō mē vertam?

10Cīvēs monuit nē timērent; senātōribus imperāvit ut fortiter cōnstituerent.

11Tanta erat coniūrātōrum audācia ut nēmō iam dē perīculō dubitāret; neque quisquam eōs dēfendere ausus est.

12Dīxit igitur rem pūblicam omnibus bonīs cīvibus dēfendendam esse.

13Dēnique cīvēs hortātus est: “Faciāmus quod officium poscit; nē cūnctēmur. Sī quis cūnctētur, rēs pūblica pereat.”

14Volēbat enim omnēs cīvēs ūnā īre. Utinam nōs ipsī illō diē adfuissēmus!

📖 GLOSS — EVERY WORD NOT ON YOUR RUNNING LIST, IN THE FORM IT APPEARS
  • 1 · Sallustius, Sallustiī m. — Sallust · plūrimī — very many · praeterierant — had gone by, had passed (pluperfect indicative of praetereō, praeterīre, praeteriī, a compound of eō)
  • 2 · nōn ignōrābat — he was well aware (the Latin double negative from Module 11: ignōrō, ignōrāre — not to know) · illō annō — in that year · passa esset — had suffered, had undergone (pluperfect subjunctive of the deponent patior, patī, passus sum; feminine, agreeing with rēs pūblica)
  • 3 · magnā virtūte — of great courage (ablative describing what kind of men they were) · mōribus — in character (ablative plural of mōs, mōris m. — custom, character) · dissimilēs — unlike, dissimilar (dissimilis, dissimile)
  • 4 · autem — however, on the other hand · cupiditāte — by a desire (ablative of cupiditās, cupiditātis f.) · rēgnandī — of ruling as a king (genitive gerund of rēgnō, rēgnāre) · captum esse — to have been seized (perfect passive infinitive of capiō) · ait — he says (a defective verb; it has no full set of forms) · excūsō, excūsāre — to excuse, make excuses for
  • 5 · vim animī — force of mind, energy (vīs f., accusative vim) · dedisset — pluperfect subjunctive of dō, dare, dedī · glōria, glōriae f. — glory, renown · adeptus esset — would have won (pluperfect subjunctive of the deponent adipīscor, adipīscī, adeptus sum — to obtain, win)
  • 6 · verērī — to fear (present infinitive of the deponent vereor, verērī, veritus sum) · scelus, sceleris n. — crime, wickedness · excūsēmus — present subjunctive of excūsō. Note that tantum here is the ordinary adjective “so great” agreeing with scelus, not a result signpost — there is no ut clause anywhere near it.
  • 7 · aliter (adv.) — otherwise, differently · loquēbātur — was speaking (imperfect of the deponent loquor)
  • 8 · lēnitās, lēnitātis f. — mildness, mercy · sevēritās, sevēritātis f. — strictness, severity · magis — more · ūtilis, ūtile — useful, in one's interest
  • 9 · quō — to where, whither · vertam — present subjunctive of vertō, vertere, vertī — to turn; mē vertam — “am I to turn myself”. Note the voice shift: line 8 reports Cicero in the third person, and then line 9 breaks into his own words in the first person, with no “he said.” Latin does this freely and it is worth recognising — the sudden faciam and vertam are the signal. Both are deliberative questions: not requests for information, but “what am I to do?”
  • 10 · moneō, monēre, monuī (+ accusative) — to warn · fortiter (adv.) — bravely, firmly · cōnstituō, cōnstituere, cōnstituī — to decide
  • 11 · audācia, audāciae f. — boldness, recklessness · dubitō, dubitāre — to doubt, hesitate · quisquam — anyone (after a negative) · ausus est — dared (perfect of the semi-deponent audeō, audēre, ausus sum)
  • 12 · igitur — therefore · bonīs cīvibus — by good citizens (dative of agent with the gerundive of obligation, Module 5) · dēfendendam esse — that it must be defended (gerundive of obligation inside an indirect statement, so rem pūblicam and dēfendendam are both accusative)
  • 13 · dēnique (adv.) — finally · hortātus est — urged (perfect of the deponent hortor, hortārī, hortātus sum) · poscō, poscere, poposcī — to demand · cūnctēmur, cūnctētur — present subjunctive of the deponent cūnctor, cūnctārī, cūnctātus sum — to hesitate, delay · sī quis — if anyone · pereat — present subjunctive of pereō, perīre, periī — to perish (a compound of eō)
  • 14 · ūnā (adv.) — together · īre — to go (infinitive of eō) · utinam — if only, would that · illō diē — on that day · adfuissēmus — pluperfect subjunctive of adsum, adesse, adfuī — to be present
⚠️ Two lines are not Sallust's or Cicero's, and you should know which. Line 5 — the counterfactual about what Catiline might have achieved — is a question we are invited to ask; Sallust does not ask it. He grants Catiline great force of mind and condemns him, and he leaves it there. Line 14's wish is the course author's, not either writer's: utinam nōs ipsī illō diē adfuissēmus, “if only we ourselves had been present on that day.” Both lines are in the passage because they are the two best sentence shapes in the semester — the past contrary-to-fact condition and the optative wish about the past. Both are honest as inventions and would be dishonest as quotations, which is why they are labelled.

🏛️ THE ROMAN WORLD · TWO ACCOUNTS OF ONE DECEMBER

Almost everything anyone knows about the Catilinarian conspiracy comes from two men who had something at stake in it. Cicero was the consul; he made the decisions, he published the speeches, and for the rest of his life he defended what he did that December. Five years later a tribune named Clodius carried a law against anyone who had put citizens to death without trial, and Cicero left Italy. He came back the following year, but he never entirely stopped arguing the case. Sallust was a much younger man in 63 BC, and by the time he sat down to write, both Catiline and Cicero were dead and Rome had fallen into another civil war. He was not defending a decision. He was explaining a decline.

So the two accounts are not two halves of one truth. Cicero shows you a consul acting in an emergency, asking the room what is to be done. Sallust shows you a republic whose ruling class had lost the qualities that once held it together — and, in the middle of his narrative of the Senate's debate, stops to praise Caesar and Cato as the last two men of real virtūs he could find, while giving the consul who chaired the meeting no speech at all. That silence is a judgment, and it is the kind of judgment historians make by choosing what to include. When you read a primary source next semester, this is the habit worth carrying: ask not only what the writer says, but what he is for, and what he has left out.

m02–m03Deponents; semi-deponentsPassive form, active meaning — check the principal parts, and remember the four semi-deponents are audeō, soleō, gaudeō, fīdō.
m04GerundA verbal noun, neuter singular, no nominative. Rēgnandī causā = purpose.
m05Gerundive; obligation; dative of agentA verbal adjective — it agrees. With esse it means “must be,” and its agent is dative, not ā/ab.
m06Present and imperfect subjunctivePresent markers -e-, -ea-, -a-, -ia-; imperfect = the present infinitive whole + -m, -s, -t…
m07Sequence of tensesTwo rows. Present, future and future perfect are primary; imperfect, perfect and pluperfect are secondary. Future is primary.
m08 gatePurpose; indirect commandNegative is nē, never ut nōn. Imperō and persuādeō take the dative; iubeō takes accusative + infinitive.
m09ResultA signpost in the main clause — tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō — and the negative is ut nōn.
m10Cum clausesVerb? Mood? Tamen? Reason? — and cum + indicative is plain “when” and nothing more.
m11Indirect questionsA question word + a subjunctive after a verb of asking or not knowing. Num = “whether”; utrum…an; necne. Watch quot/tot and quantum/tantum.
m12Fear clausesNē reverses after a verb of fearing: “afraid that it will.” “Afraid that it will not” is nē nōn.
m13Perfect and pluperfect subjunctivePerfect stem + -erim / + -issem; passive and deponent = PPP + sim / PPP + essem. The grid now has four boxes.
m14Jussive, hortatory, optative, deliberativeNo subordinating word → main-clause use. 3rd person = jussive, 1st plural = hortatory, utinam = optative, a question = deliberative. Negative nē for the first three, because they will something; nōn for the deliberative, which only asks.
m15Conditions; six irregular verbsIndicative = simple; present subj both halves = future less vivid; imperfect = contrary to fact now; pluperfect = contrary to fact in the past. Ferō, eō, volō, nōlō, mālō, fīō.
0706010Indirect statement — the one with no subjunctiveAccusative + infinitive. When you meet a reported sentence, ask first: is there a subjunctive here at all?
✍️ If you can only revise one thing tonight, revise the negatives. Purpose and indirect command take nē. Result takes ut nōn. Fear clauses take nē nōn. Cum clauses, indirect questions and conditions take plain nōn inside the clause. Jussives, hortatories and optatives take nē — but a deliberative question takes nōn, because nē negates something willed and a deliberative only asks. Five lines, and they carry more marks on this quiz than any other five facts in the semester.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Look back at line 5 and line 13 of the reading. One is a sentence about a past that did not happen; the other calls a room to act and then says what would follow if it did not. Neither could have been written in September, because neither exists in the indicative — they need a mood you did not have. That is what fifteen ordinary weeks bought you: not a longer vocabulary list, but the ability to say things Latin can only say one way. Go and prove it.
