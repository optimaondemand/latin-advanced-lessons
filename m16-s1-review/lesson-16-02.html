📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Constructions get all the attention. They are also not what people get wrong.
When a translation goes badly, it is almost never because a student failed to spot a purpose clause. It is because a case ending was read as the wrong case, or a deponent was translated as a passive, or a gerundive was read as a gerund. Today is pile one: the forms. Cases and their jobs, the six indicative tenses at recognition speed, the deponent and semi-deponent block from Modules 2 and 3, and the gerund-versus-gerundive test from Modules 4 and 5 that settles in one look. No constructions today — only the material they are built out of.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name the job of every case and identify a noun's declension from its genitive singular alone.
2
I will be able to tell a deponent from a true passive, and a semi-deponent from both, by looking at the principal parts.
3
I will be able to decide gerund or gerundive with a single question, and name the four jobs the -nd- forms do.
📚 PART 1 · THE CASES, AND THE ONE QUESTION THAT FINDS THE DECLENSION

You have known all five cases since your first year. What matters at this level is not reciting them but reading a word and knowing what it is doing in the sentence:

CaseIts jobsA word from this semester doing it

Genitivepossession; description; the gerund of purpose with causāsella cōnsulis · audācia coniūrātōrum · dīcendī causā

The move that never fails: look at the genitive singular. It is the second word of every dictionary entry precisely because it names the declension, and the declension tells you every other ending.

⚠️ The three collisions worth guarding. (1) -ae is 1st-declension genitive singular and dative singular and nominative plural — three jobs, one spelling; the sentence decides. (2) senātūs is 4th-declension genitive singular and nominative plural. (3) reī pūblicae is genitive and dative — and with a gerundive of obligation anywhere nearby, try dative first, because that is the dative of agent from Module 5.
📚 PART 2 · THE SIX INDICATIVE TENSES AT RECOGNITION SPEED

You are not being asked to conjugate anything here. You are being asked to read a marker and stop. This is the table the whole subjunctive system sits on top of, because the indicative is what you have to rule out before you can call a form subjunctive.

TenseThe marker you look forActive example
Presentstem + personal ending, no markerdīcit — he says
Imperfect-bā- (1st, 2nd) · -ēbā- (3rd, 4th)dīcēbat — he was saying
Future-bi- (1st, 2nd) · -e- (3rd, 4th)dīcet — he will say
Perfectperfect stem + -ī, -istī, -it, -imus, -istis, -ēruntdīxit — he said
Pluperfectperfect stem + -erā-dīxerat — he had said
Future perfectperfect stem + -eri-dīxerit — he will have said

The passive splits in half, and the split is worth saying out loud once:

Present, imperfect, future passive: one word, with the passive endings -r, -ris, -tur, -mur, -minī, -ntur.  dīcitur, dīcēbātur, dīcētur.
Perfect, pluperfect, future perfect passive: two words — the PPP plus a form of sum.  dictum est, dictum erat, dictum erit.
💡 Why that split matters tomorrow. The subjunctive does exactly the same thing in exactly the same place: present and imperfect subjunctive passive are one word (dīcātur, dīcerētur), and perfect and pluperfect subjunctive passive are two (dictum sit, dictum esset). Once you see that the system has the same shape in both moods, Module 13 stops being a list of new forms and becomes a pattern you already owned.
📚 PART 3 · THE DEPONENT BLOCK — MODULES 2 AND 3

A deponent is a verb with passive forms and active meaning. You know that. What is worth twenty seconds of revision is the test, which lives in the principal parts and nowhere else:

TypePrincipal parts look likeHow to read it
Ordinary verbdīcō, dīcere, dīxī, dictum
four parts, first in -ō		Active forms mean active; passive forms mean passive. Nothing unusual.
Deponentloquor, loquī, locūtus sum
three parts, first in -or, second in -ī not -re		Passive form throughout, active meaning throughout. Loquitur = “he speaks,” never “he is spoken.”
Semi-deponentaudeō, audēre, ausus sum
first two parts active, third part deponent		Active in the present system, deponent in the perfect system. Audet — he dares. Ausus est — he dared.
The four semi-deponents, in full. There are only four. Learn them as a list.
audeō, audēre, ausus sum — to dare · soleō, solēre, solitus sum — to be accustomed · gaudeō, gaudēre, gāvīsus sum — to rejoice · fīdō, fīdere, fīsus sum — to trust

And the deponent point that catches people in reading rather than in drills: a deponent's perfect participle is active in meaning. Locūtus is “having spoken,” not “having been spoken.” Profectus is “having set out.” This is the one place where Latin hands you an active perfect participle, and it is a gift — ordinary verbs have no such thing.

✍️ Deponents in the subjunctive, since that is where they will meet you this week. They behave exactly as they always have — passive in shape, active in meaning — through all four tenses: loquātur (present), loquerētur (imperfect), locūtus sit (perfect), locūtus esset (pluperfect). Those mean he may speak, he might be speaking, he has spoken, he had spoken. None of them means anything passive.
📚 PART 4 · GERUND OR GERUNDIVE — ONE QUESTION, AND FOUR JOBS

Both are built on -nd-, which is why they get confused. They are different parts of speech, which is why the confusion is worth ending:

 Gerund (Module 4)Gerundive (Module 5)
What it isa verbal noun — “the doing of it”a verbal adjective — “to be done”
Formsneuter singular only, 2nd declension: dīcendī, dīcendō, dīcendum, dīcendō. No nominative — the infinitive does that job.a full three-gender adjective: dīcendus, dīcenda, dīcendum, all cases, singular and plural
The testIs there a noun for it to agree with? No → gerund. Yes, and the endings match that noun in case, number and gender → gerundive. That is the whole test, and it takes one look.

The -nd- forms do four jobs in this course. All four are in the semester's reading, and all four can appear on the quiz:

1. Genitive gerund + causā or grātiā = purpose.
Cicerō dīcendī causā surrēxit. — “Cicero stood up for the sake of speaking.” Note the word order: causā comes after its genitive.
2. Ad + accusative = purpose.
ad dīcendum — “for speaking” (a gerund, with no noun in sight). But once an object appears, Latin prefers the gerundive agreeing with it: ad urbem dēfendendam — “for defending the city,” with dēfendendam feminine accusative singular to match urbem. Same meaning, different grammar.
3. Gerundive of obligation + esse = “must be done.”
Rēs pūblica dēfendenda est. — “The republic must be defended.” The gerundive agrees with the subject; est carries the tense. Dēfendenda erat = “had to be defended.”
4. Dative of agent — who has to do it.
Rēs pūblica nōbīs dēfendenda est. — “The republic must be defended by us.” Not ā nōbīs. This construction is the one place in the whole course where the agent goes into the dative instead of ā/ab + ablative, which is exactly why it is examinable.
⚠️ One form, three readings — be honest about it. Dēfendenda standing alone could be a gerundive of obligation (“having to be defended”), a gerundive inside an ad or causā phrase, or a plain future passive participle. What decides is what sits around it: a form of esse nearby → obligation; ad, causā or grātiā nearby → purpose. The word never tells you by itself.

gaudeō, gaudēre, gāvīsus sum

Semi-deponent. To rejoice, be glad. Note the odd perfect stem gāvīs-.
cūnctor, cūnctārī, cūnctātus sum
type? meaning?
Deponent. To hesitate, delay. Nē cūnctēmur — “let us not hesitate.”

Deponent. To obtain, win. Adeptus esset — “he would have won.”
dīcō, dīcere, dīxī, dictum

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Two questions cover most of today. For a noun: what is the genitive singular? For a verb: how many principal parts, and what does the first one end in? Neither question requires you to understand the sentence, and between them they decide declension, conjugation, deponency and voice — before meaning enters the picture at all.
