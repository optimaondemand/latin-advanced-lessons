📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have never seen the whole system on one page. Nobody has shown it to you, because until Module 15 it was not finished.
Ten modules built the subjunctive one piece at a time: two tenses, then a sequence rule, then a purpose clause, then result, cum, questions, fear, then two more tenses, then main-clause uses, then conditions. Today it goes on one page in five tables: the four tenses, the sequence grid, the five subordinate constructions, the four main-clause uses, and the four conditions. Nothing here is new. Everything here is examinable.

📚 PART 1 · THE FOUR TENSES, BUILT FROM THE PRINCIPAL PARTS

Every subjunctive form in Latin comes out of a principal part plus a marker. Here is the whole active system:

And the passive, which behaves exactly like the indicative passive did in 16.02 — two tenses in one word, two tenses in two words. Deponents use the passive column and mean it actively:

TensePassiveDeponent (active meaning)

sumpresent sim, sīs, sit, sīmus, sītis, sint · imperfect essem… · perfect fuerim… · pluperfect fuissem… — the commonest subjunctives in Latin. Say all four aloud.
⚠️ The collision Module 13 warned you about, and it has not gone away. Rēxerit is the 3rd singular perfect subjunctive and also the 3rd singular future perfect indicative. Identical spelling, two different moods. There is no ending that separates them, so nothing about the word will help you — only its clause will. If it sits after ut, nē, cum, a question word or a verb of fearing, it is subjunctive. If it is the main verb of its own sentence, it is future perfect indicative. Mood is decided by position, not by spelling.
📚 PART 2 · THE SEQUENCE GRID — FOUR BOXES, AND THAT IS ALL

Module 7 gave you two rows and left two boxes grey because you did not own the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive yet. Module 13 filled them in. This is the finished grid, and it answers every “which tense?” question in the entire course:

Main verb is…Clause happens at the same time (or later)Clause was already finished
PRIMARY
present · future · future perfect
rogat, rogābit, nescit		PRESENT subjunctive
Rogat quid faciat. — He asks what he is doing.		PERFECT subjunctive
Rogat quid fēcerit. — He asks what he did.
SECONDARY
imperfect · perfect · pluperfect
rogābat, rogāvit, rogāverat		IMPERFECT subjunctive
Rogāvit quid faceret. — He asked what he was doing.		PLUPERFECT subjunctive
Rogāvit quid fēcisset. — He asked what he had done.
Read the grid in this order, always: which row does my main verb put me in? Then which column does the meaning put me in? Row first, column second. Row is mechanical — you read the main verb off the page. Column asks one question about meaning: has the clause's action already happened, or not?
⚠️ The one row-error everybody makes at least once. Future is primary. Cognōscet quot coniūrātī maneant — a future main verb takes the present subjunctive, not the imperfect. Students reason that a future main verb must want something other than the present, and there is no other row for it to go in. Two rows, and future sits in the first one.
💡 Where the grid does not apply. Sequence governs subjunctives in subordinate clauses. It has nothing to say about the four main-clause uses in Part 4 — a jussive or an optative has no main verb above it to be sequenced against, so it chooses its tense by meaning instead. And conditions (Part 5) choose their tenses by type. So: five constructions use the grid, and everything else does not. Knowing which is which is half of tomorrow's lesson.
📚 PART 3 · THE FIVE SUBORDINATE CONSTRUCTIONS

Five constructions, each with a signal you can read off the page and a negative you have to know. All five obey the sequence grid.

ConstructionThe signalIts negativeExample, and what the clause is doing
Purpose
m08		ut — and no signpost word in the main clausenē
never ut nōn		Cicerō vēnit ut populus audīret. — answers “why?” Something intended, whether or not it happened.
Indirect command
m08		a verb of ordering, urging or asking + utnēSenātōribus imperāvit ut cōnstituerent. — imperō and persuādeō put the person in the dative; moneō, rogō, ōrō in the accusative; iubeō takes accusative + infinitive and no ut at all.

Cum clause
m10		cum + a subjunctive verb inside the groupnōn inside the clauseThree meanings: temporal “when,” causal “since,” concessive “although.” Tamen in the main clause marks the concessive. Cum + indicative is plain “when” and nothing more.
Indirect question
m11		a verb of asking, knowing or not knowing + a question word: quis, quid, cūr, ubi, quandō, quōmodo, quot, quantus, uter… or num / -ne (whether), utrum…an, necnenōn inside the clauseRogāvit utrum lēnitās an sevēritās ūtilis esset. — reports a question. No antecedent, unlike a relative clause.
Fear clause
m12		a verb of fearing — timeō, vereor, metuō, or the noun phrase metus est — + nēnē nōn
(rarely ut)		Timēbat nē urbem incenderent. — “that they would burn it.” Nē turns around here. Nē nōn veniat = “afraid that he will not come.”
✍️ And the sixth thing on this list, which has no subjunctive at all. Indirect statement — accusative + infinitive, from 0706010 — reports without a subjunctive: Dīxit Catilīnam in urbe esse. It belongs beside these five because it does the same job (reporting) and because after a semester of subjunctives students start reading one into it. When you meet a reported sentence, the very first question is: is there a subjunctive here at all?
📚 PART 4 · THE FOUR MAIN-CLAUSE USES

Module 14's whole point: sometimes the subjunctive is the main verb, with no subordinating word in front of it. Four uses, and the person of the verb usually tells you which:

UseSignatureNegativeExample and translation
Jussive3rd person, no subordinating wordnēVeniat. — “Let him come.”  Nē veniat. — “Let him not come.”
Hortatory1st person pluralnēFaciāmus quod officium poscit. — “Let us do what duty demands.”  Nē cūnctēmur. — “Let us not hesitate.”
Optativeutinam — the one word that gives it awayutinam nēTense carries the meaning: utinam veniat — “I wish he may come” (still possible); utinam venīret — “I wish he were coming” (he is not); utinam vēnisset — “I wish he had come” (he did not).
Deliberativea question, usually 1st personnōnQuid faciam? — “What am I to do?”  Quō mē vertam? — “Where am I to turn?” Not asking for information; asking what is to be done.
Three of the four take nē. The fourth takes nōn. Jussive, hortatory and optative negate with nē — that is nē's fourth job. The deliberative question negates with nōn: An ego nōn venīrem? — “Was I not to come?”
And there is a reason, not just a list to memorise. Nē negates a willed action — something somebody wants to happen, or wants not to happen. A jussive, a hortatory and a wish are all willed: let him not come, let us not hesitate, if only he had not come. A deliberative question wills nothing; it is asking. There is no wish inside it for nē to negate, so it uses the ordinary negative like any other question.
⚠️ Two honest ambiguities in this table. (1) Quid faciam? is spelled identically to the future indicative “what shall I do?” — and in a question the two readings are very close in sense, which is probably how the deliberative arose in the first place. Do not agonise over it. (2) The 3rd-person jussive and a bare purpose clause with the ut omitted can look alike in a long sentence. The test is whether anything subordinates the verb: no ut, no nē, no cum, no question word, and a main clause of its own → main-clause use.
📚 PART 5 · THE FOUR CONDITIONS

A condition has two halves and Module 15 named them: the protasis is the sī clause, the apodosis is the main clause. Nisi means “unless, if… not.” What decides the type is not the meaning of the words but the mood and tense in the two halves, and in the three subjunctive types both halves use the same tense — which makes them the easiest thing in the semester to identify:

TypeBoth halves useExample and translation
Simple / factual
0706010, reviewed in m15		the INDICATIVESī hoc dīcis, errās. — “If you say this, you are wrong.” No claim about likelihood at all; the speaker just lays out a case.
Future less vivid
m15		PRESENT subjunctiveSī hoc dīcās, errēs. — “Should you say this, you would be wrong.” Possible, but held at arm's length.
Contrary to fact,
present
m15		IMPERFECT subjunctiveSī hoc dīcerēs, errārēs. — “If you were saying this, you would be wrong.” You are not saying it. The grammar says so.
Contrary to fact,
past
m15 + m13		PLUPERFECT subjunctiveSī hoc dīxissēs, errāvissēs. — “If you had said this, you would have been wrong.” You did not say it, and the sentence tells you that without saying it.
💡 Why the past contrary-to-fact matters more than the others. It is the grammar of counterfactual history, and it is the one construction that lets you say something about the past that you know did not happen: Sī Catilīna hanc vim animī reī pūblicae dedisset, magnam glōriam adeptus esset. Historians live in that sentence shape. It is also the construction your ARTHUR Project asks you to write, so it is worth being fluent in by Friday: pluperfect subjunctive in both halves, and nothing else.
✍️ And the irregular verbs, which are vocabulary as much as paradigm. Module 15's six show up in conditions constantly, so keep their subjunctives to hand: ferō → feram / ferrem · eō → eam / īrem · volō → velim / vellem · nōlō → nōlim / nōllem · mālō → mālim / māllem · fīō → fīam / fierem. Note the double letters in ferrem, vellem, nōllem, māllem — that is how you tell the imperfect subjunctive from the present.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

The subjunctive is not one thing that means one thing. It is a mood — a signal that the verb is not being asserted as plain fact — and what it means on any given line is decided by where it sits. Same form, different clause, different meaning. That is why every table today had a “signal” column, and why tomorrow's procedure starts by asking where the verb is rather than what it is.
