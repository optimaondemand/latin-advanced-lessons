📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Some verbs change sides halfway through.
Audeō means "I dare," and it looks exactly like any ordinary active verb: audeō, audēs, audet. Then you reach the perfect tense and it turns into ausus sum — participle plus esse, the deponent pattern from last week. Not two verbs. One verb, with a seam in the middle.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define a semi-deponent verb and say exactly where its seam falls.
2
I will be able to tell a semi-deponent from a full deponent by reading its principal parts.
3
I will be able to name the four semi-deponents of Latin and give the meaning of each.
📚 PART 1 · FIRST, RETRIEVE LAST WEEK

A deponent verb is passive in form and active in meaning, all the way through. There is no active version of it anywhere: no *loquō, no *hortāvit. Three principal parts, part 1 in -or.

loquor, loquī, locūtus sum — I speak · I spoke
Passive-looking in the present. Passive-looking in the perfect. Active in both.

Now hold that picture and look at this one:

audeō, audēre, ausus sum — I dare · I dared
Active-looking in the present. Passive-looking in the perfect. Active in both.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Before you read on: audeō looks completely normal in the present but goes deponent in the perfect. What is your first guess about why a language would do that?
📚 PART 2 · THE DEFINITION, AND THE SEAM
A semi-deponent verb is active in the present system and deponent in the perfect system.

You already know both halves. The present system is present, imperfect, and future — ordinary active endings, nothing new. The perfect system is perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect — participle plus a form of esse, exactly the recipe you learned for deponents in Lesson 2.03. The whole lesson is knowing where the border is, and the border never moves: it falls between the future and the perfect.

TenseForm of audeōBehaves likeMeaning
Presentaudetordinary activehe dares
Imperfectaudēbatordinary activehe was daring
Futureaudēbitordinary activehe will dare
Perfectausus estdeponenthe dared
Pluperfectausus eratdeponenthe had dared
Future perfectausus eritdeponenthe will have dared
⚠️ The red line in that table is the whole module. Above it, treat the verb as an ordinary 2nd-conjugation active. Below it, treat it as a deponent. Nothing in between, and no exceptions inside a tense — a tense is either fully above the line or fully below it.

Read the principal parts and the seam shows itself

Kind of verbPrincipal partsWhat the parts are telling you
Ordinarylaudō, laudāre, laudāvī, laudātumFour parts. Part 1 in -ō, part 3 a one-word active perfect. Active throughout.
Deponentloquor, loquī, locūtus sumThree parts. Part 1 in -or, part 3 a participle + sum. Passive-looking throughout.
Semi-deponentaudeō, audēre, ausus sumThree parts. Part 1 in -ō — but part 3 is a participle + sum. The parts themselves show you the seam.
💡 The two-second test. Look at part 1 and part 3 together. -ō and a one-word perfect → ordinary. -or and a participle → deponent. -ō and a participle → semi-deponent. That mismatch is the signature, and it is the only signature you need.
📚 PART 3 · ALL FOUR OF THEM

Latin has essentially four semi-deponent verbs in common use, plus a few compounds of the last one. Four is a small enough number to simply know. Three of them are 2nd conjugation; one is 3rd.

Principal partsConj.MeaningPerfect — "I…"
audeō, audēre, ausus sum2ndto dare, to venture (+ infinitive)ausus sum — I dared
soleō, solēre, solitus sum2ndto be accustomed, to be in the habit of (+ infinitive)solitus sum — I was accustomed
gaudeō, gaudēre, gāvīsus sum2ndto rejoice, to be glad (+ ablative of cause)gāvīsus sum — I rejoiced
fīdō, fīdere, fīsus sum3rdto trust, to rely on (+ dative or ablative — never accusative)fīsus sum — I trusted
✍️ Two of these you have already used without noticing. Soleō is one of the most common verbs in Latin prose — every time a historian says someone "used to" do something, that is solēbat. And fīdō is the verb behind fidēs, "trust, good faith," one of the words Romans used most often to describe themselves. Watch the vowel, though: fīdō has a long ī, while the noun fidēs has a short one.

Four sentences, one for each verb. Read the Latin, then the note underneath.

Nēmō in senātū loquī audēbat.
"No one dared to speak in the Senate."
Imperfect, 3rd singular — above the seam, so ordinary active endings. Audeō takes an infinitive: loquī, the deponent infinitive from Module 2. Gloss: nēmō — "no one."
Fulvia vērum dīcere ausa est.
"Fulvia dared to tell the truth."
Perfect — below the seam, so participle + esse, and the participle agrees with the subject. Fulvia is a woman, so ausa, not ausus. Gloss: vērum, vērī n. — the truth.
Catilīna nocte venīre solēbat.
"Catiline was accustomed to come at night." — or, more naturally, "Catiline used to come at night."
Imperfect of soleō plus an infinitive. This is the single most useful semi-deponent form in Latin prose; learn it as a unit.
Cōnsul indicibus fīsus est et vēritāte gāvīsus est.
"The consul trusted the informers and rejoiced at the truth."
Two perfects, both below the seam. Fīdō puts what it trusts in the dative (indicibus); gaudeō puts the cause of the joy in the ablative (vēritāte). Gloss: index, indicis m./f. — informer; vēritās, vēritātis f. — truth.

★ VĒRITĀS · HONESTY

Vēritās is the Roman word for truth — not truth as a theory, but truth as something a person tells when it costs them. Semi-deponents make an odd little emblem of it. In each half of their life they are exactly what they are: active where they are active, deponent where they are deponent, no false form anywhere. They do not pretend to be regular in the perfect, and they do not pretend to be deponent in the present. What confuses students is not the verb's dishonesty; it is our own hope that things will stay simple. And this week's reading is about the same thing at human scale: a conspiracy came apart because a handful of people told the truth about what they had heard, when silence was safer.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A semi-deponent is active in the present system, deponent in the perfect system, and its principal parts say so out loud: part 1 in -ō, part 3 a participle plus sum. Four verbs — audeō, soleō, gaudeō, fīdō. One seam, and it never moves.
