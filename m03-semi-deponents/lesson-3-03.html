Lesson 3.03
The Other Three, and Your Vocabulary
Grammar Core · Vocabulary
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Learn one semi-deponent and you have learned all four.
Soleō, gaudeō, and fīdō split at exactly the same place audeō does. So today is not really about new machinery — it is about the three things that make each of these verbs behave differently in a sentence: what they take after them, and which case they govern.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to form soleō, gaudeō, and fīdō on either side of the seam.
2
I will be able to use each semi-deponent with the right complement — infinitive, ablative, or dative.
3
I will be able to give the principal parts of all four semi-deponents and the meaning of three new nouns.
📚 PART 1 · SOLEŌ — THE MOST USEFUL ONE

Soleō, solēre, solitus sum — "to be accustomed, to be in the habit of." It is 2nd conjugation, just like audeō, and it almost always carries an infinitive after it. In good English, solēbat plus an infinitive is usually best translated "used to…".

Tense3rd singularMeaning
Presentsolethe is accustomed, he usually does
Imperfectsolēbathe was accustomed, he used to
Futuresolēbit (rare in real Latin)he will be accustomed
Perfectsolitus esthe was accustomed, he became accustomed
Pluperfectsolitus erathe had been accustomed

The future of soleō is formed regularly but almost never used — Romans said "he will usually do it" some other way. It is here for completeness; you will not be asked to produce it.

Coniūrātī in locō occultō convenīre solēbant.
"The conspirators used to meet in a hidden place."
Imperfect, 3rd plural, plus the infinitive convenīre. Notice how much work one verb is doing: it turns a single event into a habit. Gloss: conveniō, convenīre — to come together, meet; occultus, -a, -um — hidden.
✍️ Why historians love this verb. A historian who writes solēbat is not reporting an event; he is reporting a character. "He used to do this" is a claim about the kind of person someone was. Watch for it in Friday's reading — it appears twice, and both times it is telling you something about a man's method rather than his actions on one particular day.
📚 PART 2 · GAUDEŌ AND FĪDŌ — MIND THE CASE

Gaudeō, gaudēre, gāvīsus sum — to rejoice, be glad

2nd conjugation, seam at the perfect: gaudet → gāvīsus est. The thing you are glad about normally goes in the ablative — the ablative of cause, which you already know from phrases like magnā cūrā.

Senātōrēs vēritāte gāvīsī sunt.
"The senators rejoiced at the truth."
Perfect, 3rd plural masculine — gāvīsī, agreeing with senātōrēs. Vēritāte is ablative: the cause of the joy. Gloss: vēritās, vēritātis f. — truth.
⚠️ Watch the perfect participle. It is gāvīsus, not *gaudītus. The stem changes, exactly as audeō changes to ausus. When a semi-deponent crosses the seam, do not assume the stem survives intact — that is what the third principal part is for.

Fīdō, fīdere, fīsus sum — to trust, rely on

The one 3rd-conjugation semi-deponent, and the one with a real trap in it. Fīdō never takes an accusative object. What it trusts goes in the dative when it is a person, and usually in the ablative when it is a thing.

Tense3rd singularMeaning
Presentfīdithe trusts
Imperfectfīdēbathe was trusting, he trusted
Futurefīdethe will trust
Perfectfīsus esthe trusted, he has trusted
Pluperfectfīsus erathe had trusted
Cōnsul indicibus fīdēbat, sed Catilīnae nōn fīdit.
"The consul trusted the informers, but he does not trust Catiline."
Both objects are dative: indicibus (dat. pl.) and Catilīnae (dat. sg.). Compare English "he gave credit to them" — the dative is doing that job. Gloss: index, indicis m./f. — informer.
💡 A macron worth two seconds. Because fīdō is 3rd conjugation, its present 2nd singular is fīdis ("you trust") and its future 2nd singular is fīdēs ("you will trust") — the same short-e-versus-long-ē trap as loqueris / loquēris from Module 2. And do not confuse either of them with the noun fidēs, fideī f., "trust, good faith," which has a short first i. Same family, different words.
📚 Recognition only: fīdō has two compounds you will meet in reading, and both behave identically. Cōnfīdō, cōnfīdere, cōnfīsus sum — "to trust completely, be confident" (+ dat. or abl.); diffīdō, diffīdere, diffīsus sum — "to distrust, despair of" (+ dat.). You do not need to produce them; you do need to recognize the pattern when a cōn- or dif- is stuck on the front.

📚 PART 4 · NEW NOUNS FOR THIS WEEK'S READING

Four nouns, all of them about information: who carries it, what marks it, and what it is when it is true.

EntryMeaningIn a phrase
index, indicis m./f.informer; sign, indicator — literally someone or something that pointsindex loquēns — the informer speaking
nūntius, nūntiī m.messenger; message, report, newsnūntium accēpit — he received a report
signum, signī n.sign, signal, seal; a military standardsigna coniūrātiōnis — signs of a conspiracy
vēritās, vēritātis f.truth, truthfulness — this module's virtuevēritāte gāvīsus est — he rejoiced at the truth
✍️ Nūntius and a verb you already have. You know nūntiō, nūntiāre, "to announce." Nūntius is the noun beside it, and Latin lets it mean either the person who brings word or the word itself — context decides. English does the same thing with "post" and "wire."
📜 DERIVATIVE MINUTE

All four semi-deponents left descendants in English, and in three cases the English word still carries the Latin meaning almost intact:

LatinEnglish descendants
audeō — dareaudacious, audacity — both through the adjective audāx, audācis, "bold"
soleō — be accustomedinsolent (in- + solēns, "not accustomed" → not keeping to normal behavior) · obsolete (something that has grown out of use)
gaudeō — rejoicejoy, rejoice, enjoy — through the noun gaudium, "joy," which became French joie and then English
fīdō — trustconfide, confidence, diffident, fidelity, fiduciary — and, through fidēs, the phrase bona fide, "in good faith"
index · signum · vēritāsindex, indicate · sign, signal, signature, design, insignia · verify, verdict (from vērum dictum, "a true thing said")

A trap worth naming. Audible and audience do not come from audeō. They come from audiō, audīre, "to hear" — a different verb that happens to start with the same three letters. Audeō gives you audacious; audiō gives you audio. This is exactly the kind of near-miss that shows up on quizzes.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

All four semi-deponents split at the perfect, but they do not all build a sentence the same way: audeō and soleō want an infinitive, gaudeō wants an ablative of cause, and fīdō wants a dative — never an accusative. The form is the easy half; the construction is what you have to learn per verb.
