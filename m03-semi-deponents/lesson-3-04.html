📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two questions, asked in this order, will parse any participle in Latin.
What case is it? That tells you the construction — ablative absolute, or a participle describing some noun in the sentence. What kind of verb is it from? That tells you the voice — active for a deponent or semi-deponent, passive for an ordinary verb. Get those two in the right order and Sallust's densest sentences come apart cleanly.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name every participle a deponent or semi-deponent verb can form, and give its meaning.
2
I will be able to tell an ablative absolute from a participle agreeing with the subject, by case alone.
3
I will be able to translate ausus and fīsus actively — "having dared," "having trusted."

💡 The gap in the table is the interesting part. An ordinary verb has a passive perfect participle — laudātus, "having been praised" — and no active one. Deponents and semi-deponents have exactly the reverse: an active perfect participle and no passive one. Between the two verb types, Latin covers the whole grid; no single verb does.
✍️ One honest note about soleō. Its perfect participle solitus, -a, -um is common — but Latin usually uses it as a plain adjective meaning "usual, customary" (solitō mōre, "in the usual way") rather than as a "having been accustomed" participle. Recognize it; do not try to force it into a participial phrase. The present participle solēns exists but is rare, so it is not on your production list.
📚 PART 2 · QUESTION ONE — WHAT CASE IS IT?

The construction is decided entirely by case. There are three possibilities and no fourth:

If the participle is…then the construction is…and you translate it…
Ablative, next to an ablative noun that has no other job in the sentenceAblative absoluteas a whole clause: "when… / after… / since… / with…"
Nominative, matching the subjectA participle describing the subjectas a phrase attached to the subject: "…, having spoken, …"
Any other case (accusative, dative, genitive)A participle describing some other nounattached to whichever noun it agrees with

Three sentences, same verb, three different jobs. Read the Latin, then the note.

Cicerōne loquente, omnēs senātōrēs audiēbant.
"While Cicero was speaking, all the senators were listening."
Ablative absolute with a deponent present participle. Both words ablative; Cicerōne has no other role — the subject is senātōrēs. Present participle, so the speaking and the listening happen together.
Catilīnā locūtō, coniūrātī discessērunt.
"When Catiline had spoken, the conspirators departed."
Ablative absolute with a deponent perfect participle. Both ablative again — but now the participle is perfect, so the speech finished before the departure. And it is still active: "having spoken," never "having been spoken." Gloss: discēdō, discēdere, discessī — to depart, go away.
Catilīna, coniūrātīs locūtus, ex urbe profectus est.
"Catiline, having spoken to the conspirators, set out from the city."
Not an ablative absolute. Locūtus is nominative and agrees with Catilīna, who is the subject of profectus est. The dative coniūrātīs belongs to the participle: loquor speaks to someone.
⚠️ The one thing that decides it. Compare the last two sentences: Catilīnā locūtō versus Catilīna…locūtus. Four letters. If the pair is ablative and the person is not the subject, it is an ablative absolute and you open a new clause. If the pair is nominative and the person is the subject, it is a phrase hanging off that subject. Check the endings before you decide — never the word order.
📚 PART 3 · QUESTION TWO — WHAT KIND OF VERB IS IT FROM?

Case gave you the construction. Now the verb's type gives you the voice, and there is nothing to work out — you either know the verb or you look it up.

Verb typePerfect participleVoice, and what it implies
Ordinary verbaudītus · acceptus · monitusPassive — "having been heard / received / warned." Someone else did it to them.
Deponentlocūtus · secūtus · profectusActive — "having spoken / followed / set out." They did it, and can take an object.
Semi-deponentausus · fīsus · gāvīsusActive — "having dared / trusted / rejoiced." Same as a deponent, because the perfect system is deponent.

Which means the same construction can point in opposite directions depending only on the verb:

ORDINARY VERB · PASSIVE
Omnibus nūntiīs acceptīs, Cicerō gāvīsus est.
"With all the reports received, Cicero rejoiced."
Acceptīs is a real PPP. The reports did not receive anything; they were received.
SEMI-DEPONENT · ACTIVE
Cicerō, indicibus fīsus, in senātū surrēxit.
"Cicero, having trusted the informers, rose in the Senate."
Fīsus looks passive and is active. Note it even governs a dative, indicibus. Gloss: surgō, surgere, surrēxī — to rise.
Coniūrātī Catilīnam hortantem audiēbant.
"The conspirators were listening to Catiline urging them on."
The third possibility from Part 2: hortantem is accusative, agreeing with Catilīnam, the object of audiēbant. Not an ablative absolute, not attached to the subject — the case told you so immediately.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Case first, then verb. Ablative pair with no other job in the sentence → ablative absolute, open a clause. Nominative matching the subject → a phrase on the subject. Then ask what verb it came from: deponent or semi-deponent → active; ordinary verb → passive. Ausus means "having dared." It never means anything else.
