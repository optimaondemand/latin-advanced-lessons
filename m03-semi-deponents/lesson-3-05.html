📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A conspiracy met behind a closed door. Within days the consul knew what had been said.
Module 2 ended in that room, with Catiline talking to men who had followed him. Today you find out how the words got out — and the Latin that carries the story is built almost entirely on the verbs you have spent two weeks learning. Watch the two solēbats in particular: they are Sallust telling you what kind of man each of these people was.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to parse every semi-deponent and deponent form in an adapted passage of Sallust.
2
I will be able to distinguish a real passive from a deponent form inside continuous prose.
3
I will be able to explain how a Roman consul with no police force learned a secret.
📜 PART 1 · THE READING

Adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 23 and 26, with one detail from Cicero, In Catilīnam I.8. A simplified Latin retelling — not Sallust's own sentences, and not Cicero's. Every deponent or semi-deponent form is marked in red; every real passive is underlined in blue, so you can see both kinds in one paragraph. Read it once straight through for sense before you parse anything.

Cicerō cōnsul erat, sed exercitum in urbe nōn habēbat. Coniūrātī in locīs occultīs convēnerant et dē caede cōnsulis locūtī erant. Cicerō tamen dē cōnsiliīs eōrum cognōscere solēbat. Quōmodo? Amīcōs bonōs habēbat, et amīcī eius indicēs invenīre audēbant.

Erat Quīntus Curius, homō audāx, quī inter coniūrātōs numerābātur. Hic Fulviae, mulierī nōbilī, multa dē coniūrātiōne nārrāre solēbat; nam sē magnum vidērī cupiēbat. Fulvia autem tantum perīculum reī pūblicae cēlāre nōn ausa est. Per Fulviam igitur cōnsul cōnsilia coniūrātōrum cognōvit.

Nocte quādam coniūrātī in domum Mārcī Laecae convēnērunt. Ibi Catilīna sociōs suōs hortātus est et dē caede iterum locūtus est. Sed Cicerō, ab indicibus monitus, omnia iam cognōverat. Deinde in senātū surrēxit et Catilīnae dīxit: "Nihil agis quod ego nōn audiō."

Cicerō, omnibus nūntiīs acceptīs, vēritāte gāvīsus est. Indicibus enim fīsus erat, et indicēs quoque cōnsulī fīsī erant. Sīc cōnsul, quī nūllum exercitum in urbe habēbat, coniūrātiōnem cognōvit — nōn armīs, sed verbīs eōrum quī vērum dīcere ausī erant.

📖 VOCABULARY GLOSS · every word not on your running list
  • exercitus, exercitūs m. — army
  • occultus, -a, -um — hidden, secret; in locīs occultīs — "in hidden places"
  • conveniō, convenīre, convēnī — to come together, meet; convēnerant "had met," convēnērunt "they met"
  • caedēs, caedis f. — murder, killing; dē caede — "about the murder"
  • tamen (adv.) — nevertheless, all the same
  • cognōscō, cognōscere, cognōvī — to learn, find out; cognōverat "had learned," cognōvit "learned"
  • quōmodo — "how?" (a question word on its own here)
  • eius · eōrum — "his" · "their" (genitive of is, ea, id)
  • index, indicis m./f. — informer
  • inveniō, invenīre, invēnī — to find
  • Quīntus Curius — Quintus Curius, a Roman who had been expelled from the Senate and joined the conspiracy
  • audāx, audācis — bold, reckless — the adjective built on audeō
  • inter + acc. — among
  • numerō, numerāre — to count, number; numerābātur — "he was counted" — a real passive, for contrast
  • hic — "this man, he" (nom. sg. m. of hic, haec, hoc)
  • Fulvia, Fulviae f. — Fulvia, a Roman noblewoman
  • mulier, mulieris f. — woman; mulierī nōbilī — dative, "to a noblewoman"
  • nōbilis, -e — noble, high-born
  • nārrō, nārrāre — to tell, relate
  • nam — for, because
  • sē magnum vidērī cupiēbat — "he wanted to seem important" — indirect statement: sē + the present passive infinitive vidērī, "to seem / to be seen." Cupiō, cupere — to want
  • autem — however, but (never first in its clause)
  • tantus, -a, -um — so great; tantum perīculum — "so great a danger"
  • rēs pūblica, reī pūblicae f. — the republic, the state (two words); reī pūblicae here genitive, "to the republic"
  • cēlō, cēlāre — to hide, conceal
  • igitur — therefore
  • nocte quādam — "on a certain night" (ablative of time; quīdam, quaedam, quoddam — a certain)
  • domus, domūs f. — house; in domum + acc. — "into the house of"
  • Mārcus Laeca — Marcus Porcius Laeca, a senator; Cicero says the conspirators met at his house
  • socius, sociī m. — ally, companion, partner
  • iterum (adv.) — again, a second time
  • moneō, monēre, monuī, monitum — to warn; monitus — "having been warned," a real perfect passive participle, with its agent ab indicibus
  • omnia — everything, all things (neuter plural)
  • iam (adv.) — already
  • deinde (adv.) — then, next
  • surgō, surgere, surrēxī — to rise, stand up; surrēxit — "he rose"
  • nihil agis — "you do nothing" (agō, agere, ēgī, āctum — to do, act)
  • accipiō, accipere, accēpī, acceptum — to receive; omnibus nūntiīs acceptīs — ablative absolute, "with all the reports received"
  • nūntius, nūntiī m. — report, message; messenger
  • vēritās, vēritātis f. — truth; vēritāte — ablative of cause with gaudeō
  • enim — for, indeed (never first in its clause)
  • quoque — also, too
  • sīc (adv.) — thus, in this way
  • nūllus, -a, -um — no, not any
  • arma, armōrum n. pl. — arms, weapons
  • vērum, vērī n. — the truth, what is true
✍️ Two notes about the adaptation. 1. Cicero's real sentence — the one this passage borrows — puts its relative clause in the subjunctive: nihil agis…quod nōn ego…audiam. That construction arrives in Module 6. The adapter used the indicative audiō so the line stays inside the grammar you already have. When you meet the real version, you will know what it replaced. 2. Hortātus est here simply takes an accusative object, sociōs suōs. Full classical Latin would normally add ut plus a subjunctive verb to say what he urged them to do — also Module 8. Same reason: the adaptation stays inside your grammar.

🔍 Read the last paragraph again slowly. Acceptīs and gāvīsus est sit five words apart and have the same shape. One is a real passive: the reports were received. The other is a semi-deponent perfect: Cicero rejoiced. Nothing on the page tells you which is which except knowing the two verbs. That is why Modules 2 and 3 come with vocabulary lists attached.
✍️ PART 3 · TRANSLATE, THEN CHECK

Write your English out before flipping. These four are good practice for this week's translation assignment.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Cicerō tamen dē cōnsiliīs eōrum cognōscere solēbat.
click to compare
"Cicero, however, used to find out about their plans." Solēbat is imperfect — above the seam, so ordinary active endings — plus the infinitive cognōscere. The sentence is not reporting one discovery; it is reporting a working method.
Fulvia autem tantum perīculum reī pūblicae cēlāre nōn ausa est.
click to compare
"Fulvia, however, did not dare to conceal so great a danger to the republic." Ausa est is below the seam: participle + est, and ausa is feminine because Fulvia is. Note what the sentence actually says — not that she wanted glory, but that she could not bring herself to stay quiet.
Sed Cicerō, ab indicibus monitus, omnia iam cognōverat.
click to compare
"But Cicero, having been warned by the informers, had already learned everything." Monitus is a real PPP — nominative, agreeing with Cicerō, and passive: the warning was done to him. The agent ab indicibus proves it; a deponent participle could never take an ab phrase like this.
Indicibus enim fīsus erat, et indicēs quoque cōnsulī fīsī erant.
click to compare
"For he had trusted the informers, and the informers had trusted the consul too." Two pluperfects of fīdō, one singular and one plural — watch fīsus against fīsī. Both objects are dative: indicibus, cōnsulī. The trust runs in both directions, which is the point of the sentence.
🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · A CONSUL WITH NO POLICE FORCE

Rome in 63 BC had no detectives, no intelligence service, and no police. A consul could not order a search. So when Cicero stood up and announced that he knew what the conspirators had said in a private house, the obvious question is the one this lesson is named for: how?

Sallust's answer is that the information walked in. Among the conspirators was Quintus Curius, a man who had already been thrown out of the Senate by the censors. Curius was involved with a well-born woman named Fulvia, and — Sallust says — he talked. He hinted at great things coming, apparently to make himself sound important to her. Fulvia did not keep it to herself, and through Fulvia the plot reached the consul. Sallust does not turn her into a heroine; he simply records that she did not conceal a danger that size. That restraint is very much his style.

It is worth sitting with how ordinary the mechanism is. Roman politics ran on households, dinners, patrons, clients, and relationships — and so did Roman information. Cicero's advantage was not a spy network in the modern sense but a very large number of people who owed him something and would talk to him. Later in the story he gets luckier still, when written evidence falls into his hands; that is Module 8, and it is what finally makes the case unanswerable in public.

The Roman word for it: vēritās. Notice which grammar Sallust hands these people. Cicero solēbat — he made a habit of finding things out. The informers audēbant — they dared. Fulvia nōn ausa est to hide it. The conspiracy was undone not by an army but by a small number of people who were willing to say what they knew, in a city where saying it was dangerous. Vēritās, for a Roman, was not a feeling about honesty; it was a thing you did out loud, usually at some cost.

🧠 MODULE 3 IN FIVE LINES
LessonThe one thing to keep
3.01A semi-deponent is active in the present system, deponent in the perfect system. Part 1 in -ō, part 3 a participle — that mismatch is the signature.
3.02Above the seam audeō is videō (audet, audēbat, audēbit); below it, audeō is loquor (ausus est, ausus erat). No *auduī, no *audētur.
3.03Audeō and soleō take an infinitive; gaudeō takes an ablative of cause; fīdō takes a dative — never an accusative.
3.04Case first, then verb. Case gives the construction; the verb type gives the voice. Ausus and fīsus are active: "having dared," "having trusted."
3.05In real prose the two kinds sit side by side, and only knowing the verb tells them apart. An author's tenses are also a portrait: solēbat describes a character, not an event.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A semi-deponent is exactly what it is on each side of the seam, with no false form anywhere — and reading it right just means refusing to guess. The people in today's passage did the same thing at much higher stakes. Vēritās is not a feeling; in Rome it was a thing you said out loud, and someone had to dare to say it: ausī erant.
