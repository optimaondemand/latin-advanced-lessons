📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Thirty forms, and you already know every one of them.
The gerundive declines like bonus, bona, bonum — the first adjective you ever learned. So there is nothing new to memorize today. What is new is the habit: reading the gerundive's ending as a pointer to the noun it belongs to. Get that reflex and the rest of the module is downhill.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to give any gerundive form in any gender, number, and case.
2
I will be able to match a gerundive to the noun it agrees with, and spot a mismatch.
3
I will be able to explain why Latin swaps a gerundive in whenever a gerund would have taken a direct object.

💡 Where the gerund lives inside this table. Look at the neuter singular column: dēfendendum, dēfendendī, dēfendendō, dēfendendum, dēfendendō. Those four distinct forms are exactly the gerund you learned in Module 4. The gerund is not a separate word to memorize — it is the neuter singular of this same block, used on its own as a noun. That is why the two are so easy to confuse, and why the agreement test from yesterday is the only reliable way to tell them apart.
📚 PART 2 · THE ENDING IS A POINTER

A gerundive's ending is not decoration. It tells you which noun in the sentence the obligation is attached to — which is often the difference between two completely different readings. Work out the gender, number, and case before you flip each card.

You have lived with this ambiguity since bonus; the noun beside the gerundive is what resolves it. Read the pair, never the ending alone.
📚 PART 3 · THE SWAP: WHEN A GERUND WOULD HAVE HAD AN OBJECT

Module 4 gave you gerunds doing three jobs: genitive with causā, accusative after ad for purpose, and bare ablative of means. All three worked fine as long as the gerund had no direct object.

The moment you want an object — not just "for the sake of defending" but "for the sake of defending the republic" — classical Latin does something unexpected. It refuses to hang an accusative object on the gerund. Instead it promotes the object to be the main noun and turns the verb into a gerundive agreeing with it.

WHAT ENGLISH LEADS YOU TO WRITE
rem pūblicam dēfendendī causā
gerund + accusative object. Understandable, and avoided in classical prose.
WHAT LATIN ACTUALLY WRITES
reī pūblicae dēfendendae causā
the object becomes the noun in the required case; the gerundive agrees with it.

Both phrases mean "for the sake of defending the republic." Notice what moved: rem pūblicam (accusative) became reī pūblicae (genitive, because causā demands the genitive), and dēfendendī changed to dēfendendae to match it. The construction as a whole does the same job; the parts have swapped roles.

💡 Why bother? Because Latin marks relationships with endings, and a gerund with an object gives you two accusatives sitting next to each other doing different jobs — ambiguous, and Latin hates that. The gerundive version has every word's role visible on its ending. It is the same instinct behind the ablative absolute: make the grammar do the work so the reader never has to guess.
📝 A spelling matter you will meet all semester. Rēs pūblica — "the republic," literally "the public thing" — is two words: a 5th-declension noun with an adjective agreeing with it. Both parts decline: rēs pūblica (nom.), reī pūblicae (gen. and dat.), rem pūblicam (acc.), rē pūblicā (abl.). You will sometimes see it printed as one word in older books; write it as two.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

The gerundive declines exactly like bonus, bona, bonum, and its ending points at the noun it belongs to. When a gerund would have taken a direct object, Latin promotes the object and lets the gerundive agree with it: reī pūblicae dēfendendae causā, not rem pūblicam dēfendendī causā.
