Lesson 5.01
Gerund, Meet Gerundive
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Last week a verb turned into a noun. This week it turns into an adjective — and starts giving orders.
The gerund you learned in Module 4 is stuck: neuter, singular, four cases, no nominative. Its twin, the gerundive, has the full run of the language — three genders, every case, singular and plural — because it is an adjective. And out of that one difference comes the sentence Romans used to say this has to happen, and here is who has to do it.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state the one difference between a gerund and a gerundive, and say what follows from it.
2
I will be able to build the gerundive of a verb in any of the four conjugations, including a deponent.
3
I will be able to look at an -nd- form in a sentence and decide whether it is a gerund or a gerundive.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT YOU ALREADY HAVE

Module 4 gave you the gerund: a verb wearing a noun's clothes. It let you put an action into a case slot — ars scrībendī ("the art of writing"), ad pugnandum ("for fighting"), scrībendō ("by writing"), dīcendī causā ("for the sake of speaking"). Always neuter, always singular, and no nominative, because Latin already had the infinitive for that job.

Now look closely at the piece the gerund is made of: the letters -nd-. Latin got a lot of mileage out of that little block. Attach neuter noun endings and you get a gerund. Attach full adjective endings and you get something else entirely.

GERUND · MODULE 4
dēfendendī
"of defending"
A verbal noun. Neuter singular, no nominative. Four case forms in total.
GERUNDIVE · THIS WEEK
dēfendendus, -a, -um
"needing to be defended"
A verbal adjective. Three genders, all five cases, singular and plural — thirty-odd forms.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
If the gerundive is an adjective, it has to agree with some noun. Before you read on: what do you think that noun turns out to be, in a sentence like "the city must be defended"?
📚 PART 2 · THE DEFINITION, AND THE ONE THING TO GET RIGHT
A gerundive is a verbal adjective. It is passive in force, it looks forward in time, and it agrees with its noun in gender, number, and case.

Take those three pieces one at a time.

The gerundive is…Which meansWorked example
passive in forcethe noun it describes receives the actionurbs dēfendenda — "the city to be defended," never "the city that defends"
forward-lookingthe action has not happened yet; it is owed, due, waitingepistula legenda — "the letter still to be read," not "the letter that was read"
an adjectiveit changes ending to match its noun, like bonus, bona, bonumurbs dēfendenda (fem. sg.) · lēgēs scrībendae (fem. pl.) · officium faciendum (neut. sg.)
⚠️ The error to kill on day one. Urbs dēfendenda est means "the city must be defended." It does not mean "the city must defend." Almost every student who loses points on this module loses them right here — they see a nominative noun, assume it is the doer, and flip the voice. The gerundive is passive. The nominative noun is what gets done to.
❌ WRONG
"The city must defend."
✅ RIGHT
"The city must be defended."
💡 A translation crutch that actually works. Whenever you meet a gerundive, say it to yourself first as "needing to be ___ed." Dēfendendus → "needing to be defended." Legenda → "needing to be read." Faciendum → "needing to be done." It is clumsy English, but it locks in the passive force and the forward look at the same time — and you can smooth it out afterwards.
📚 PART 3 · HOW TO BUILD ONE

The recipe is the gerund's recipe with different endings on the end — so you already know two of the three steps.

1. Take the present stem (the infinitive minus -re).
2. Add -nd-.
3. Add the endings of bonus, bona, bonum — a 1st/2nd-declension adjective.
ConjugationInfinitiveGerundive (m. / f. / n.)Meaning
1stlaudārelaudandus, laudanda, laudandumneeding to be praised
2ndhabērehabendus, habenda, habendumneeding to be held, delivered
3rddēfenderedēfendendus, dēfendenda, dēfendendumneeding to be defended
3rd io-stemcaperecapiendus, capienda, capiendumneeding to be seized, captured
4thaudīreaudiendus, audienda, audiendumneeding to be heard
✍️ Two spelling notes worth ten points later. In the 2nd conjugation the long vowel of the infinitive shortens: habēre but habendus; dēlēre but dēlendus. And in the 3rd io-stem and the 4th conjugation an -i- shows up before the -nd-: capiendus, audiendus, faciendus, custōdiendus, pūniendus. These are exactly the same vowel patterns you already learned for the gerund, so if the gerund is solid the gerundive is nearly free.

Deponents have gerundives too — and they are passive

This will feel strange for about ten seconds. A deponent has no passive meaning anywhere else in its paradigm (Module 2), but its gerundive is passive in force like everybody else's:

DeponentGerundiveMeaning
hortor, hortārī — urgehortandus, -a, -umneeding to be urged, encouraged
sequor, sequī — followsequendus, -a, -umneeding to be followed
mīror, mīrārī — admiremīrandus, -a, -umneeding to be admired; admirable
patior, patī — endurepatiendus, -a, -umneeding to be endured

So dux nōbīs sequendus est is perfectly good Latin: "the leader must be followed by us." A deponent's form looks passive and means active; a deponent's gerundive means passive. Latin is consistent about this; it just takes a moment to believe.

🧠 PART 4 · GERUND OR GERUNDIVE?

One test settles every case: is there a noun for this form to agree with? If yes, and the endings match — gerundive. If there is nothing to agree with and the form is sitting there as a neuter noun on its own — gerund.

✅ CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING
Look at the -nd- form. Noun to agree with, or no noun to agree with?
dīcendī causā — "for the sake of speaking"
lēgum scrībendārum causā — "for the sake of writing laws"
Urbs dēfendenda est.
Which of these contains a gerundive?

Then write one Latin sentence in the shape you will learn on Wednesday: a noun, a gerundive that agrees with it, and est. Example: Officium mihi faciendum est. — "The duty must be done by me." Formative and ungraded; the graded ARTHUR project comes at Module 8.

★ OFFICIUM · SERVICE

Officium is built from ob ("toward") and facere ("to do") — something done toward somebody else. For Romans it was not a warm feeling but close to a debt: you owed officia to your family, your allies, your city, the gods, and people kept count. This module's grammar is that idea turned into a sentence pattern. A gerundive of obligation names a thing that has to be done and, in the dative, the person it falls to — what must be done, and by whom. Notice how little room the construction leaves for wanting. It does not say "I would like to defend the republic." It says rēs pūblica mihi dēfendenda est — the defending is already owed, and my name is on it. That is service in the Roman sense, and it is the shape of every hard decision Cicero makes this semester.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A gerund is a verbal noun; a gerundive is a verbal adjective that agrees with something and is passive in force. Read every gerundive as "needing to be ___ed" before you smooth it into English, and you will never flip the voice.
