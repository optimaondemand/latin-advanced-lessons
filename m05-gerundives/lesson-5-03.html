Lesson 5.03
Must Be Done: the Gerundive of Obligation
Grammar Core
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Add est, and a description becomes a demand.
Urbs dēfendenda — "the city needing to be defended" — is just a phrase. Put est after it and you get urbs dēfendenda est: "the city must be defended." That is the single most useful sentence pattern in Roman political speech, and once you can see it you can read the argument of an entire oration.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognize a gerundive of obligation and translate it as "must be ___ed" — passively, every time.
2
I will be able to change the tense of esse to move the obligation into the past or the future.
3
I will be able to translate the impersonal neuter version, pugnandum est, "there must be fighting."
📚 PART 1 · THE PATTERN
nominative noun  +  gerundive agreeing with it  +  a form of esse
= "the noun must be ___ed" / "the noun has to be ___ed"

Grammar books also call this the passive periphrastic. "Periphrastic" just means "said the long way round" — Latin uses two words where English uses "must." Here it is across all six gender-and-number combinations:

LatinGerundive is…English
Urbs dēfendenda est.fem. nom. sg.The city must be defended.
Catilīna pūniendus est.masc. nom. sg.Catiline must be punished.
Officium faciendum est.neut. nom. sg.The duty must be done.
Coniūrātī capiendī sunt.masc. nom. pl.The conspirators must be captured.
Epistulae legendae sunt.fem. nom. pl.The letters must be read.
Multa facienda sunt.neut. nom. pl.Many things must be done.
💡 Notice that esse agrees too. Singular noun, est. Plural noun, sunt. That is nothing new — it is the ordinary rule that a verb agrees with its subject, and here the nominative noun is the subject. So a gerundive of obligation has two things pointing at the same noun: the gerundive's ending and the number of esse. Two clues, one answer. Use both.
⚠️ PART 2 · THE VOICE TRAP, HEAD ON

This is the error that costs more marks than anything else in the module, so we are going to look straight at it. The nominative noun in this construction is not the doer. It is the thing done to.

❌ WHAT THE EYE WANTS TO DO
Urbs dēfendenda est.
"The city must defend."
Nominative noun → must be the subject → must be doing the verb. That chain of reasoning is right for ordinary sentences and wrong here.
✅ WHAT IT MEANS
Urbs dēfendenda est.
"The city must be defended."
The gerundive is passive in force. The city is on the receiving end. Somebody else does the defending.

Run the test on four sentences. In each one, ask yourself: who or what is having something done to it?

Latin❌ The flipped reading✅ The right reading
Catilīna pūniendus est."Catiline must punish.""Catiline must be punished."
Epistula legenda est."The letter must read." — which is not even English"The letter must be read."
Lēgēs scrībendae sunt."The laws must write.""The laws must be written."
Coniūrātī custōdiendī sunt."The conspirators must guard.""The conspirators must be guarded."
✍️ Your safety check. Translate the gerundive as "needing to be ___ed" first, exactly as you practised on Monday. "Catiline, needing to be punished, is." It is ugly, but nobody has ever produced an active mistranslation out of it. Then tidy up to "must be punished." Two steps beats one wrong guess.
📚 PART 3 · THE TENSE LIVES IN ESSE

The gerundive itself never changes tense — it always points forward. If you want to say the obligation belonged to the past, or is coming, you change esse. Nothing else moves.

Form of esseExampleEnglish
est / sunt
present		Ōrātiō habenda est.The speech must be delivered.
erat / erant
imperfect		Ōrātiō habenda erat.The speech had to be delivered.
erit / erunt
future		Ōrātiō habenda erit.The speech will have to be delivered.
⚠️ Do not read erat as "was." Ōrātiō habenda erat is not "the speech was delivered" — that would be ōrātiō habita est, an ordinary perfect passive with the participle habita. The gerundive keeps the obligation alive: the speech had to be delivered, whether or not it ever was. Watch for the -nd-; it is the difference between a fact and a duty.
💡 Latin's other ways to say "ought." The gerundive is not the only option, and knowing the alternatives makes the choice visible. Dēbeō + infinitive: Senātus lēgēs scrībere dēbet — "the Senate ought to write the laws." Impersonal oportet + infinitive: Cōnsulem vigilāre oportet — "the consul ought to keep watch." And necesse est + infinitive: necesse est agere — "it is necessary to act." Each of these keeps the doer in front and the verb active. The gerundive does the opposite: it puts the task in front and leaves the doer for later, or leaves him out altogether. That is a rhetorical choice, and Cicero makes it constantly.
📚 PART 4 · WHEN THERE IS NOTHING TO AGREE WITH

Some verbs never take a direct object. Pugnō ("fight"), vigilō ("stay awake, keep watch"), proficīscor ("set out") — you do not fight a thing, you just fight. So there is no noun for the gerundive to agree with.

Latin's answer is elegant: use the neuter singular as a default, with no subject at all, and let est stand alone.

Pugnandum est.
"There must be fighting." — more naturally, "We must fight."
LatinEnglish
Nōbīs pugnandum est.We must fight. (literally "there must be fighting by us")
Cōnsulī vigilandum erat.The consul had to keep watch.
Mihi nunc agendum est.I must act now.
Nōbīs ex urbe proficīscendum erit.We will have to set out from the city.
✍️ How to spot the impersonal version. A gerundive in -ndum with est or erat, and no nominative noun anywhere. Translate it as "there must be ___ing," then convert to a normal English sentence with a person as subject. And notice proficīscendum in the last example: a deponent, working perfectly well in this construction.
📜 THE MOST FAMOUS EXAMPLE — AND WHO IT BELONGS TO
Carthāgō dēlenda est.
"Carthage must be destroyed."

You may have met this sentence before it ever meant anything to you grammatically. Now you can take it apart: Carthāgō is feminine nominative singular, dēlenda is the gerundive of dēleō agreeing with it, est supplies the present tense. Passive in force, obligation, no agent named. Four words, and no way to argue with it.

Get the attribution right. This is traditionally associated with Cato the Elder — Cato the Censor — a Roman senator of the generation before Cicero's, who ancient writers say ended his speeches by insisting that Carthage be destroyed, whatever the debate had actually been about. Even that much is worth stating carefully: the compact four-word sentence as we quote it is a later formulation rather than a verbatim quotation of Cato's own words.

What matters for this module is that it is not Cicero. Cicero uses the construction constantly, and you will read him using it in Lesson 5.05 — but this particular sentence belongs to a different man and a different century. When you cite Latin, cite it accurately; that is its own kind of officium.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Gerundive + esse = "must be ___ed," and it is passive: the nominative noun is what gets done to, never the doer. The tense lives in esse — est, erat, erit. With no noun to agree with, the neuter goes impersonal: pugnandum est, "we must fight."
