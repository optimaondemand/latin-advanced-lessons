Lesson 5.04
The Dative of Agent
Grammar Core · Vocabulary
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
"The city must be defended" — fine. By whom?
Everything you learned last year says that "by somebody" in a passive sentence is ā/ab + ablative. With the gerundive of obligation, Latin does something else: it uses the dative. This is the second reliable error in the module — and today you make it impossible.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to add a dative of agent to a gerundive of obligation and translate it with "by."
2
I will be able to choose between the dative of agent and ā/ab + ablative by looking at the verb form.
3
I will be able to give the meanings of this module's vocabulary, and explain four English words that are literally Latin gerundives.
📚 PART 1 · WHO HAS TO DO IT

Drop the person into the dative, anywhere in the sentence, and you have named who the duty falls on:

Urbs nōbīs dēfendenda est.
"The city must be defended by us."
or, in plainer English: "We must defend the city."

Both English versions are good translations. The first keeps Latin's shape; the second is what an English speaker would actually say. Notice how differently they land, though. Latin's version puts the city first and the people second — the task exists, and we happen to be the ones it lands on. English puts we first. That difference is not an accident, and it is why Roman public speech reaches for this construction so often.

The literal sense of the dative here is something like "for us" or "as far as we are concerned" — a dative of interest, which is why the case makes sense once you stop expecting ā/ab. These are the forms you will use most:

EnglishDative of agentExample
by memihiOfficium mihi faciendum est. — The duty must be done by me.
by you (sg.)tibiHoc tibi cōnstituendum est. — This must be decided by you.
by usnōbīsRēs pūblica nōbīs dēfendenda est. — The republic must be defended by us.
by you (pl.)vōbīsCōnsilium vōbīs capiendum est. — A decision must be taken by you.
by the consulcōnsulīCatilīna cōnsulī pūniendus erat. — Catiline had to be punished by the consul.
by the SenatesenātuīLēgēs senātuī scrībendae sunt. — The laws must be written by the Senate.
by CiceroCicerōnīŌrātiō Cicerōnī habenda erat. — The speech had to be delivered by Cicero.
by the citizenscīvibusUrbs cīvibus custōdienda est. — The city must be guarded by the citizens.
💡 The agent is optional. Urbs dēfendenda est is a complete sentence — "the city must be defended," with nobody named. Leaving the agent out is a rhetorical move: it makes the obligation sound like a fact of nature rather than somebody's job. Putting it in is the opposite move: it assigns responsibility, out loud, in front of witnesses. Watch which one Cicero chooses in tomorrow's reading.
⚠️ PART 2 · DATIVE, OR Ā/AB + ABLATIVE?

Latin has two ways of saying "by somebody," and they are not interchangeable. The verb form decides which one you get.

ORDINARY PASSIVE VERB
Catilīna ā Cicerōne damnātus est.
"Catiline was condemned by Cicero."
Agent = ā/ab + ablative. This is what you learned in 0706010, and it is still right — for real passive verbs.
GERUNDIVE OF OBLIGATION
Catilīna Cicerōnī pūniendus est.
"Catiline must be punished by Cicero."
Agent = bare dative. No preposition. This is the new rule, and it applies only here.

Matched pairs, so you can see the switch happening:

Passive verb → ā/ab + ablativeGerundive of obligation → dative
Lēgēs ā senātū scrīptae sunt.
The laws were written by the Senate.		Lēgēs senātuī scrībendae sunt.
The laws must be written by the Senate.
Epistula ā Lentulō missa est.
The letter was sent by Lentulus.		Epistula mihi legenda est.
The letter must be read by me.
Urbs ā cīvibus dēfēnsa est.
The city was defended by the citizens.		Urbs cīvibus dēfendenda est.
The city must be defended by the citizens.
⛔ Writing ā nōbīs dēfendenda est is the error. It is not a small stylistic wobble; it is the wrong construction, and it is the single thing graders look for first on this module's translation. Your tell is the -nd-. If the verb form has -nd- in it, the agent has no preposition and sits in the dative. If the verb form is a participle in -tus/-sus with esse, the agent takes ā/ab. Gloss: damnātus est — "was condemned" (perfect passive of damnō).

📜 DERIVATIVE MINUTE · THE ENGLISH WORDS THAT ARE GERUNDIVES

English borrowed a handful of Latin gerundives whole, without translating them — which means you have been reading gerundives your entire life without knowing it. Every one carries the same force: a thing that must be done.

English wordLatin gerundiveLiterally
agendaagenda, from agōneuter plural — "things that must be done." A meeting agenda is a list of duties, not topics.
addendumaddendum, from addōneuter singular — "a thing that must be added." Plural addenda.
memorandummemorandum, from memorōneuter singular — "a thing that must be remembered." Plural memoranda.
referendumreferendum, from referōneuter singular — "a thing that must be referred back," i.e. sent back to the people to decide.
corrigendacorrigenda, from corrigōneuter plural — "things that must be corrected." The list of errors printed in the back of a book.

Why "one addendum, two addenda." The -um/-a pair is just the neuter singular and neuter plural of the paradigm you learned in 5.02. English kept the Latin endings for these words and nothing else, which is why the plurals look so strange in an English sentence.

And two names. Amanda is the feminine gerundive of amō — "she who must be loved." Miranda, the heroine of Shakespeare's The Tempest, is the feminine gerundive of the deponent mīror — "she who must be wondered at." Shakespeare knew exactly what he was doing when he named her. English propaganda comes from the same pattern by way of a seventeenth-century Church body whose Latin name described the faith "that must be spread."

💡 Referō is a compound of ferō, an irregular verb you meet properly in Module 15. Its gerundive, referendus, is built regularly off the stem refer-, so it gives you no trouble at all here.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

With a gerundive of obligation the person who must act goes in the bare dative — mihi, tibi, nōbīs, cōnsulī, senātuī — and never in ā/ab + ablative. Your tell is the -nd-: gerundive means dative agent; a -tus/-sus participle with esse means ā/ab + ablative.
