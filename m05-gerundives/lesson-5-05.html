📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Ten gerundives in one short speech — and a consul naming, out loud, whose job each one is.
Cicero is standing in front of the Senate with proof in his hand and no legal power to act alone. Watch what the grammar does for him: every claim comes out as an obligation rather than a proposal, and the datives distribute the blame. This is what you learned this week, being used to win an argument.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to parse and translate every gerundive in an adapted passage of Cicero.
2
I will be able to sort any -nd- form into gerund, gerundive-for-gerund-plus-object, or gerundive of obligation.
3
I will be able to explain why an orator with no power to act alone would choose obligation language.
📜 PART 1 · THE READING

Adapted from Cicero, In Catilīnam I and III. A simplified Latin retelling drawing on both speeches — not Cicero's own sentences. Every gerundive is marked in red. Read it once straight through for sense before you parse anything.

Cicerō cōnsul erat. Catilīna in urbe manēbat et bellum parābat. Multa cōnsulī agenda erant, sed sine testibus nihil facere poterat.

Tandem epistulae inventae sunt. Coniūrātī enim litterās ad Gallōs mīserant, et illae litterae in manūs cōnsulis vēnērunt. Nunc Cicerō documentum habēbat.

Cicerō in senātum vēnit et locūtus est: "Patrēs cōnscrīptī, rēs pūblica nōbīs dēfendenda est. Diū patientēs fuimus; nunc patientia dēpōnenda est. Hae epistulae legendae sunt, quia senātōrēs vēritātem audīre dēbent."

Litterīs lēctīs, senātōrēs tacuērunt. Lentulus, quī signum suum vīderat, nihil dīcere potuit.

"Vidētis," inquit Cicerō. "Coniūrātī capiendī et custōdiendī sunt. Catilīna ex urbe expellendus est. Hodiē agendum est, nōn crās. Mihi haec omnia suscipienda sunt, quia cōnsul sum; vōbīs cōnsilium capiendum est, quia senātōrēs estis."

Senātus cōnsēnsit. Sīc Cicerō officium suum fēcit.

📖 VOCABULARY GLOSS · every word not on your running list
  • sine (+ abl.) — without
  • testibus — abl. pl. of testis, testis m., "witness"
  • tandem (adv.) — at last, finally
  • inventae sunt — "were found" (perfect passive of inveniō)
  • enim — for, in fact (always the second word in its clause)
  • litterae, litterārum f. pl. — a letter, a dispatch (plural in form, singular in sense)
  • Gallōs — acc. pl., "the Gauls" — here the Allobroges, a Gallic people the conspirators tried to recruit
  • manūs — acc. pl. of manus, manūs f., "hand"; in manūs — "into the hands"
  • documentum, documentī n. — proof, evidence
  • illae litterae — "those letters" (illae, fem. nom. pl. of ille)
  • patrēs cōnscrīptī — "Enrolled Fathers," the formal way of addressing the Roman Senate
  • hae — "these" (fem. nom. pl. of hic) · haec omnia — "all these things" (neut. nom. pl.)
  • inquit — "he says / he said," inserted inside a quotation
  • diū (adv.) — for a long time
  • patiēns, patientis — patient, enduring; patientēs fuimus — "we have been patient"
  • patientia, patientiae f. — patience, forbearance
  • dēpōnō, dēpōnere, dēposuī, dēpositum — to lay aside, put down — the verb behind the word deponent
  • quia — because
  • vēritās, vēritātis f. — truth
  • tacuērunt — "fell silent" (perfect of taceō)
  • signum, signī n. — sign; here the seal pressed into the wax of a letter
  • Lentulus, Lentulī m. — Publius Cornelius Lentulus Sura, a senator and one of the chief conspirators
  • hodiē (adv.) — today · crās (adv.) — tomorrow
  • suscipiō, suscipere, suscēpī, susceptum — to take on, undertake
  • cōnsilium capere — idiom, "to take a decision, form a plan"
  • cōnsēnsit — "agreed" (perfect of cōnsentiō)
  • sīc (adv.) — thus, in this way
✍️ An honest note about the adaptation. Cicero's actual sentences here are two or three times longer than these, and they are packed with a verb mood you have not met yet — the subjunctive, which arrives in Module 6. This retelling keeps his gerundives, his datives and his argument, and rebuilds everything else out of grammar you already own. What you are reading is genuinely Ciceronian in structure; it is not Ciceronian in wording. By Module 16 you will be able to handle a good deal more of the real thing.

🔍 Two contrasts the passage sets up on purpose. First: epistulae inventae sunt ("the letters were found") is a real perfect passive with a participle, three lines before epistulae legendae sunt ("the letters must be read") with a gerundive. Same noun, two different constructions, and the -nd- is the only visible difference. Second: Cicero says senātōrēs vēritātem audīre dēbent — "the senators ought to hear the truth" — using dēbeō with the senators as an active subject. One sentence later he switches to vōbīs cōnsilium capiendum est. Same people, same obligation, and a completely different feel: the second one has the duty landing on them rather than the two of them agreeing about it.

✅ The three-second sort. You meet an -nd- form. Ask:
1. Is there a form of esse? Yes → gerundive of obligation. Now find the nominative noun; if there is none, it is impersonal.
2. No esse. Is there a noun beside it whose ending matches? Yes → gerundive replacing gerund + object.
3. Neither? Gerund. Translate it as "___ing" and let the case do the rest.
✍️ PART 4 · TRANSLATE, THEN CHECK

Write your English out before you flip. These four are the best possible warm-up for tonight's translation assignment.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Multa cōnsulī agenda erant, sed sine testibus nihil facere poterat.
click to compare
"Many things had to be done by the consul, but without witnesses he could do nothing." Agenda is neuter nominative plural agreeing with multa; erant makes the obligation past; cōnsulī is the dative of agent. A smoother English version: "The consul had many things to do."
Rēs pūblica nōbīs dēfendenda est.
click to compare
"The republic must be defended by us" — or, more naturally, "we must defend the republic." Dēfendenda is feminine nominative singular agreeing with rēs pūblica; nōbīs is dative, never ā nōbīs. Note that Cicero says this before he says anything about himself: the duty is everyone's first, his second.
Coniūrātī capiendī et custōdiendī sunt; Catilīna ex urbe expellendus est.
click to compare
"The conspirators must be captured and guarded; Catiline must be driven out of the city." Two masculine nominative plurals agreeing with coniūrātī, then a masculine nominative singular agreeing with Catilīna. No agent is named in either clause — the tasks are stated as simple facts, which is a choice.
Mihi haec omnia suscipienda sunt, quia cōnsul sum.
click to compare
"All these things must be undertaken by me, because I am consul." Suscipienda is neuter nominative plural agreeing with haec omnia; mihi is the dative of agent. The quia clause is the whole argument in miniature: the office creates the obligation, and the obligation is not optional.
🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · A CONSUL WHO COULD NOT ACT ALONE

Cicero was consul, the highest office Rome had. He was also, in the crisis of 63 BC, close to powerless on his own. Executing Roman citizens without trial was not something a consul could simply order; he needed the Senate behind him, in public, on the record. Everything he says in these speeches is shaped by that one fact.

Which is why the gerundive is his instrument of choice. Look at what the construction does for a speaker in his position. Catilīna expellendus est does not say "I want Catiline gone" — a personal wish, which anyone can refuse. It says the expulsion is owed, that it exists as a duty independent of who happens to be speaking. And when he needs to assign it, the dative does the work quietly: mihi haec suscipienda sunt, these are mine to take on; vōbīs cōnsilium capiendum est, the decision is yours to make. He has just told several hundred senators what their job is, without once giving them an order.

The evidence mattered as much as the grammar. The conspirators had tried to recruit a Gallic people, the Allobroges, who reported the approach instead; letters were intercepted and brought before the Senate, and the men who had sealed them were confronted with their own seals in front of witnesses. Cicero prized this episode for the rest of his life, and later wrote about the duties of public office at length — his treatise Dē Officiīs, "On Duties," is one of the most widely read books of the ancient world. The word in its title is this module's virtue.

Worth keeping in view. Roman officium was closer to a debt than a feeling — something owed and accounted for, to family, allies, city, and gods. The gerundive of obligation is what that idea sounds like in a sentence. It is also worth asking, as you read: is Cicero describing duties that genuinely existed, or manufacturing them out of grammar? Romans argued about that at the time, and the argument outlived them all. Sallust, whom you have been reading since Module 0, tells the same story with a noticeably cooler eye.

🧠 MODULE 5 IN FIVE LINES
LessonThe one thing to keep
5.01A gerundive is a verbal adjective — passive in force, forward-looking, and it agrees. Read it first as "needing to be ___ed."
5.02It declines like bonus, bona, bonum. When a gerund would have taken an object, Latin promotes the object and lets the gerundive agree with it.
5.03Gerundive + esse = "must be ___ed." The tense lives in esse. With nothing to agree with, the neuter goes impersonal: pugnandum est.
5.04The one who must act goes in the bare dative — mihi, nōbīs, cōnsulī, senātuī — never ā/ab + ablative.
5.05In real oratory the gerundive turns a wish into a duty and hands it to somebody by name. Grammar is how obligation gets assigned.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

The gerundive of obligation is the grammar of duty. It states a task as something owed rather than something wanted, and the dative names whose it is. When Cicero says mihi haec suscipienda sunt, he is not volunteering — he is reading out a debt with his own name against it. That is officium, and this week you learned to write it.
