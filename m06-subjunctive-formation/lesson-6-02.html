📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Same vowel. Different endings. Nothing new to learn except where to put what you already have.
Yesterday's rule — ē for the 1st conjugation, ā for the rest — does not change today. What changes is what comes after it. Swap the active endings for the passive endings you have known since 0706010 and you have the entire present passive subjunctive. Then the deponents walk in and get paid back for Module 2.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to form the present passive subjunctive of all four conjugations, all six persons.
2
I will be able to form the present subjunctive of a deponent verb and translate it actively.
3
I will be able to recite the present subjunctive of sum, possum, and eō from memory.
📚 PART 1 · THE ENDINGS YOU ALREADY OWN

Here are the two sets of personal endings side by side. You learned both of these in 0706010. Neither one is new; only the vowel in front of them belongs to this module.

Present subjunctive = present stem + the mood vowel (ē for 1st conj., ā for the rest) + active or passive endings.

First conjugation, both voices, so you can see the vowel is the same on both sides:

amō, amāreActivePassivePassive means
1st sg.amemamerI may be loved
2nd sg.amēsamērisyou may be loved

✍️ The short vowels behave exactly as before. Amer and amentur have a short e, for the same reason amem and ament do — long vowels shorten before final -r and before -nt-. Everywhere else the ē stays long.

📚 PART 3 · THE DEPONENTS COLLECT WHAT THEY ARE OWED

You know the deal from Module 2: a deponent has passive forms and active meaning. That deal holds in the subjunctive with no adjustment whatsoever. Build the passive subjunctive; translate it actively. That is all.

loquor, loquī, locūtus sumPresent subjunctiveMeans
1st sg.loquarI may speak
2nd sg.loquārisyou may speak
3rd sg.loquāturhe/she may speak
1st pl.loquāmurwe may speak
2nd pl.loquāminīyou (pl.) may speak
3rd pl.loquanturthey may speak

⚠️ The three-way pile-up you must be able to untangle. For loquor, second person singular, there are now three forms that look nearly identical, and the difference is one vowel:
FormWhat it isMeans
loquerispresent indicative"you are speaking" — short e, and it is a fact
loquērisfuture indicative"you will speak" — long ē, still a fact, just later
loquārispresent subjunctive"you may speak" — ā, and not a fact at all
You met the first two in 2.02. The third is today's. If you can hold these apart, you can hold apart most of what this semester throws at you.
📚 PART 4 · THREE VERBS WORTH LEARNING AS A LIST

Almost everything in Latin obeys the vowel rule. Two verbs do not, and they are the two you will meet most often, so learn them as chants rather than deriving them.

SUM, ESSE — TO BE
sim · sīs · sit
sīmus · sītis · sint
"I may be, you may be, he may be…" The indicative is sum, es, est — nothing about the subjunctive resembles it. Just learn the six.
POSSUM, POSSE — TO BE ABLE
possim · possīs · possit
possīmus · possītis · possint
Which is a free gift: possum is pot- + sum, so its subjunctive is pos- + the sim chant you just learned.

And one that looks irregular but is not. Eō, īre ("to go") is an irregular verb — you meet its full system in Module 15 — but its present subjunctive is built on the stem e- plus the ordinary -ā-, exactly like audiam:

eō, īre — to goSingularPlural
Present subjunctiveeam · eās · eateāmus · eātis · eant
💡 Its compounds work the same way, and one of them is about to matter a great deal. Exeō, exīre means "to go out, leave," and its present subjunctive is exeam, exeās, exeat, exeāmus, exeātis, exeant. Hold on to exeat — "let him leave." A room full of Roman senators is going to shout it at Catiline on Friday.

Lesson 6.03 — The Imperfect Subjunctive, and the Easiest Rule in Latin
One rule, no exceptions, not even for the irregular verbs: take the present infinitive and add the endings. Amāre → amārem. Esse → essem. You will also build forms yourself in the Subjunctive Tense Builder.
💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

The passive subjunctive is the active subjunctive with the passive endings swapped in — amem becomes amer, regat becomes regātur. Deponents use those passive forms with active meaning, exactly as they always have: loquātur = "he may speak." And three chants earn their keep: sim, sīs, sit · possim, possīs, possit · eam, eās, eat.
