📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Building a form is one skill. Recognising one at reading speed is a different skill, and it is the one that gets tested.
When you meet regat in a sentence, nobody tells you it is subjunctive. You have to see it. Today you build the reflex — three reliable markers, the traps that catch everyone, and the one place where Latin genuinely will not tell you and you have to read the context instead.

📚 PART 1 · THREE MARKERS THAT DO MOST OF THE WORK
1. -bā- or -bō- in the middle → indicative, always. Amābat, monēbātur, audiēbam. The subjunctive never uses these markers.
2. -re- or -sse- in the middle, with a personal ending after it → imperfect subjunctive. Amāret, regerētur, esset.
3. A vowel that does not belong to the verb's own conjugation → present subjunctive. An ē in a 1st-conjugation verb (amēs); an ā anywhere else (moneat, regat, capiat, audiat).

⚠️ PART 2 · THE ONE PLACE LATIN WILL NOT TELL YOU

Here is an honest fact about Latin that textbooks sometimes hide. In the 3rd, 3rd io and 4th conjugations, the future indicative first person singular and the present subjunctive first person singular are the same word. Not similar. Identical, macrons and all.

READING 1 — FUTURE INDICATIVE
regam
"I shall rule." A prediction. A fact about later.
READING 2 — PRESENT SUBJUNCTIVE
regam
"let me rule," "may I rule," "am I to rule?" Not a fact at all.

The same collision hits every verb of these three conjugations in the first person singular, active and passive:

FormAs future indicativeAs present subjunctive
dīcamI shall saylet me say / may I say
capiamI shall seizelet me seize
audiamI shall hearlet me hear
regarI shall be ruledlet me be ruled
loquarI shall speaklet me speak
💡 What settles it: the sentence, not the word. A subjunctive almost never stands alone. If the form sits after ut, nē, cum, cūr or another introducing word, it is subjunctive. If it stands as the plain main verb of a statement, it is future indicative. Modules 8 to 12 exist to give you those signposts. Until then, when you meet a bare dīcam, the correct answer is "either — and here is how you would tell", not a guess.
⚠️ Do not over-apply it. The collision is first person singular only. Second person onward, the two tenses separate cleanly: future regēs, reget, regēmus against subjunctive regās, regat, regāmus. And it never happens in the 1st or 2nd conjugation at all, because their future is amābō and monēbō — the -bō- marker keeps them apart.
📚 PART 3 · A FIRST LOOK: WHY TWO TENSES?

A fair question at this point: if the subjunctive is not really about time, why does it have a present and an imperfect? The short answer is that the subjunctive's tense is usually decided not by when the action happens, but by the tense of the main verb it hangs under. That relationship has a name: sequence of tenses.

If the main verb is…the sequence is called…the subjunctive under it is…
present or future indicativeprimarypresent subjunctive
any past indicative tensesecondaryimperfect subjunctive

You can see it in a single pair. The clause does the same job both times; only the main verb has moved:

Cicerō dīcit ut senātōrēs intellegant.
"Cicero speaks so that the senators may understand." Present main verb → present subjunctive.

Cicerō dīxit ut senātōrēs intellegerent.
"Cicero spoke so that the senators might understand." Past main verb → imperfect subjunctive.
✍️ That is as far as this module goes, deliberately. Module 7 teaches sequence of tenses properly — including what happens when the two actions are not simultaneous. And the ut construction in those two sentences is a purpose clause, which belongs to Module 8. It is printed here only so the two tenses have a visible reason to exist. You will not be graded on sequence of tenses in this module's quiz or translation — this module grades the forms.

📜 DERIVATIVE MINUTE · DOUBT, FLIGHT, AND ONE GRAMMATICAL TERM
LatinEnglish descendants
dubium, dubitōdoubt, dubious, indubitable, redoubtable — in-dubit-able is literally "not able to be doubted."
fugiōfugitive, refuge, refugee, centrifugal — and a musical fugue, where a theme flees from voice to voice.
intellegōintelligent, intellect, intelligence, intelligible — all from the idea of picking one thing out from among others.
relinquōrelinquish, relic, derelict, dereliction — a relic is what has been left behind.
comprehendōcomprehend, comprehensive, apprehend, apprehension — the same word does police work and mental work in both languages.
mōs, mōrēsmoral, morale, morality, and the borrowed plural mores — "the mores of a society" is Latin still wearing its own ending.
sevērussevere, severity, asseverate
cōnfiteorconfess, confession; and from its relative profiteor, profess, professor, profession

And the name of the mood itself. English subjunctive is a straight borrowing of subiūnctīvus — sub ("under") + iūnctus ("joined"), from iungō. The same root gives you junction, conjunction, juncture, adjunct and yoke. Every one of those words is about two things fastened together, which is precisely what a subjunctive clause is: a verb fastened underneath another verb.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Three markers settle almost every case: -bā- means indicative; -re- or -sse- plus an ending means imperfect subjunctive; a vowel that does not belong to the verb's conjugation means present subjunctive. And when the form genuinely will not tell you — dīcam, capiam, audiam — that is not your failure. Latin left it to the sentence, and so should you.
