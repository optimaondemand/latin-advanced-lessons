Lesson 6.03
The Imperfect Subjunctive
Lesson · with the Subjunctive Tense Builder
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
There is one rule in Latin with no exceptions at all. This is it.
Take the present infinitive. Add the personal endings. You now have the imperfect subjunctive. It works for all four conjugations, both voices, every deponent, and — astonishingly — for the irregular verbs too. Esse gives essem. Posse gives possem. Īre gives īrem. After yesterday's two-vowel split, today is a holiday.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state the imperfect subjunctive rule in one sentence and apply it to any verb I am given.
2
I will be able to form the imperfect subjunctive active and passive, including deponents, sum, possum, and eō.
3
I will be able to assemble a subjunctive form from its parts, one decision at a time, and place the macrons correctly.
📚 PART 1 · ONE RULE, NO EXCEPTIONS
Imperfect subjunctive = the present infinitive + -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt.

The second principal part is the whole stem. No vowel to choose, no conjugation to identify first. Watch what happens across all four conjugations:

ConjugationInfinitiveImperfect subjunctive, active
1st — amōamāreamārem, amārēs, amāret, amārēmus, amārētis, amārent
2nd — moneōmonēremonērem, monērēs, monēret, monērēmus, monērētis, monērent
3rd — regōregereregerem, regerēs, regeret, regerēmus, regerētis, regerent
3rd io — capiōcaperecaperem, caperēs, caperet, caperēmus, caperētis, caperent
4th — audiōaudīreaudīrem, audīrēs, audīret, audīrēmus, audīrētis, audīrent
✍️ Where the macron goes. The e of the infinitive is long before -s, -mus, -tis and short before -m, -t, -nt. So: amārem but amārēs; amāret but amārētis. This is the identical shortening pattern you saw in amet versus amēs in 6.01. Learn it once and it covers both tenses.
💡 Note that 3rd io behaves like plain 3rd here. The infinitive of capiō is capere — no -i- — so the imperfect subjunctive is caperem, not capierem. The -i- that shows up all over the present system disappears the moment you work from the infinitive. Same for patior, fugiō and every other io-stem verb: paterer, fugerem.

amārem · monērem · regerem · caperem · audīrem
Say it beside yesterday's chant — amem, moneam, regam, capiam, audiam — and you have the first person singular of both subjunctive tenses this course teaches, in every conjugation. Ten forms; the rest is endings.
📚 PART 3 · DEPONENTS, AND THE VERBS THAT PROVE THE RULE

A deponent has no active infinitive — loquor's second principal part is loquī, which is passive in shape. So the rule needs one small adjustment: use the active infinitive the verb would have had, then add the passive endings.

sequor, sequī — followsequeresequerersequerētur

💡 A shortcut if reconstructing an infinitive feels strange. Take the deponent's present indicative 2nd singular, drop the final -is, and you have the same stem. Loqueris → loquer- → loquerer. Hortāris → hortār- → hortārer. Pateris → pater- → paterer. Both routes arrive at the same place; use whichever you can do faster.

The irregular verbs keep the rule

This is the part worth being pleased about. Sum, possum and eō have wildly irregular present subjunctives — you learned sim, possim and eam as chants yesterday because there was nothing to derive them from. In the imperfect they simply obey:

VerbInfinitiveImperfect subjunctive
sum — to beesseessem, essēs, esset, essēmus, essētis, essent
possum — to be ablepossepossem, possēs, posset, possēmus, possētis, possent
eō — to goīreīrem, īrēs, īret, īrēmus, īrētis, īrent
exeō — to go outexīreexīrem, exīrēs, exīret, exīrēmus, exīrētis, exīrent
✍️ Say the pair out loud: sim … essem. Those two forms have no letters in common, which is exactly why students mix them up. The present is the odd one; the imperfect is regular. If you can only hold one fact, hold this: anything ending in -rem, -rēs, -ret (or -ssem, -ssēs, -sset from esse) is imperfect subjunctive.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Present infinitive + personal endings = imperfect subjunctive. Every conjugation, both voices, deponents (with the would-be active infinitive), and the irregular verbs too: essem, possem, īrem. If a verb form has -re- or -sse- in the middle and a personal ending on the end, you are almost certainly looking at one.
