📜 PART 1 · FIRST, THE REAL THING

Two sentences of genuine, unadapted Cicero — the first words of the speech, and a line from a page later. You met the first of them in Lesson 0.05, when you had to take most of it on trust. Read them again now.

Quō ūsque tandem abūtēre, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā?
Cicero, In Catilīnam I.1 (unadapted)
Ō tempora, ō mōrēs!
Cicero, In Catilīnam I.2 (unadapted)
WordWhat it is doing
quō ūsque tandem"how long, in the end?" — a set phrase; tandem adds the impatience
abūtēre2nd sg. future of the deponent abūtor, "will you go on abusing?" — an alternative spelling of abūtēris. Passive in form, active in meaning: Module 2.
patientiā nostrāablative — abūtor governs the ablative, not the accusative. And patientia is now on your vocabulary list.
ō tempora, ō mōrēstwo accusatives of exclamation: "what an age! what a state of morals!" Mōrēs is the plural of mōs — also new this module.
💡 Notice what is not in the famous line: a subjunctive. Cicero opens in the indicative, with a question about a fact. The subjunctives arrive a few sentences later, when he starts explaining what he could do, what he fears might be said, and what he wishes Catiline would do. The mood tracks the argument — and that is why he needed it.
📜 PART 2 · THE READING

Adapted from Cicero, In Catilīnam I. A simplified Latin retelling of the scene in the temple on 8 November 63 BC — not Cicero's own sentences, apart from the three words Ō tempora! Ō mōrēs!, which are his (In Cat. I.2). Every subjunctive is marked in red. Read it once straight through for sense.

Cōnsul senātum in templum Iovis Statōris convocāverat. Catilīna ipse vēnit et sēdit; nēmō tamen prope eum sedēbat, et sellae circum eum vacuae erant. Cicerō surrēxit et paulum tacuit, ut omnēs audīrent.

"Quō ūsque tandem, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā abūtēris? Cōnsilia tua iam omnibus nōta sunt. Nox illa, in quā tū cum coniūrātīs tuīs locūtus es, nōbīs nōn est occulta.

"Rogās fortasse cūr tē nōn statim comprehendam. Nōn tē comprehendō, nē quis dīcat cōnsulem sevērum esse. Ō tempora! Ō mōrēs! Senātus haec intellegit, cōnsul videt; tū tamen in urbe manēs. Tibi, Catilīna, ex urbe exeundum est."

Multī senātōrēs timēbant nē rēs pūblica ā coniūrātīs dēlērētur. Nēmō dubitābat quis auctor huius cōnsiliī esset; aliī rogābant quid cōnsul facere posset. Ōlim cōnsulēs, ut rem pūblicam dēfenderent, cīvēs malōs statim necābant; nunc autem cīvis Rōmānus sine iūdiciō necārī nōn dēbet.

Catilīna surrēxit ut loquerētur, sed nihil dīxit. Senātōrēs, cum haec intellegerent, tacēbant. Tandem ūnus rogāvit num Catilīna ex urbe exīret. Tum multī clāmāvērunt: "Exeat! Ex urbe discēdat! Urbs servētur!"

Catilīna tandem ex templō discessit. Nocte proximā ex urbe profectus est, ut in Etrūriā exercitum parāret.

📖 VOCABULARY GLOSS · every word not on your running list
  • Iuppiter Stator, Iovis Statōris — "Jupiter the Stayer," the god who makes a line hold; Cicero delivered this speech in his temple
  • convocō, convocāre, convocāvī, convocātum — to call together, summon
  • sella, sellae f. — seat, bench
  • circum (+ acc.) — around
  • vacuus, -a, -um — empty
  • paulum (adv.) — for a moment, a little
  • quō ūsque tandem — "how long, in the end?" — the set phrase from Modules 0 and 4
  • abūtēris — "will you go on abusing?" (2nd sg. future of the deponent abūtor, which governs the ablative: patientiā nostrā)
  • nōta sunt — "are known" (from nōtus, -a, -um, known; the person to whom goes in the dative: omnibus)
  • nox illa, in quā — "that night, on which"
  • occultus, -a, -um — hidden, secret
  • statim (adv.) — at once, immediately
  • quis (here, after nē) — "anyone"
  • auctor, auctōris m. — originator, the one behind something
  • huius — "of this" (gen. sg. of hic, haec, hoc)
  • ōlim (adv.) — once, in the old days
  • iūdicium, iūdiciī n. — trial, judgment, court proceeding
  • num — "whether" (introduces an indirect question)
  • servō, servāre, servāvī, servātum — to save, protect, keep safe
  • nocte proximā — "on the following night" (ablative of time)
  • Etrūria, Etrūriae f. — Etruria, the region north of Rome where Catiline's army was waiting
  • From this module's list: patientia, mōrēs, fortasse, sevērus, intellegō, comprehendō, necō, dubitō, exeō. From Module 5: dēleō, and the gerundive of obligation exeundum est.
✍️ About every red form in that passage. Each one sits in a construction you have not studied yet — purpose, fear, indirect question, command. Those constructions are the next eight modules, and the table below tells you which module owns each one. You are not expected to parse the constructions today, and you will not be graded on them. What you can do, and what this module grades, is name the verb, the tense, the person, the number and the voice of every single one. Try it before you look.

🔍 Count the tenses and something jumps out. Nine of the fourteen are imperfect, and every one of those nine hangs under a past main verb: tacuit, timēbant, dubitābat, rogābant, necābant, surrēxit, tacēbant, rogāvit, profectus est. The five present subjunctives sit under present-tense speech, or stand alone as shouts in the moment. That is sequence of tenses operating in front of you — and it is Module 7's whole subject.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Cicerō surrēxit et paulum tacuit, ut omnēs audīrent. (ut + subjunctive = "so that…might" — Module 8)
click to compare
"Cicero stood up and was silent for a moment, so that everyone might hear." Audīrent is imperfect subjunctive, 3rd plural, active — built from audīre + -nt. It is imperfect because the main verb tacuit is past.
Multī senātōrēs timēbant nē rēs pūblica ā coniūrātīs dēlērētur. (timeō nē = "fear that" — Module 12)
click to compare
"Many senators were afraid that the republic would be destroyed by the conspirators." Dēlērētur is imperfect subjunctive, 3rd singular, passive: dēlēre + -tur. Note ā coniūrātīs — ablative of agent with a genuine passive, which is why it is not a dative here.
Catilīna surrēxit ut loquerētur, sed nihil dīxit. (ut + subjunctive = purpose — Module 8)
click to compare
"Catiline stood up in order to speak, but said nothing." Loquerētur is the imperfect subjunctive of the deponent loquor — passive endings, active meaning, exactly as in Module 2. "Might be spoken" is the misreading to avoid.
Tum multī clāmāvērunt: "Exeat! Ex urbe discēdat! Urbs servētur!" (independent subjunctive = a command — Module 14)
click to compare
"Then many shouted: 'Let him leave! Let him depart from the city! Let the city be saved!'" Three present subjunctives: exeat and discēdat active, servētur passive. A crowd using the subjunctive to demand something is the mood doing exactly what it exists for.
🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · WHAT CICERO COULD NOT DO

A reasonable question about that scene: Cicero was consul. Catiline was sitting ten feet away, and Cicero had already survived one attempt on his life. Why give a speech instead of an order?

Because Roman law was jealous about the rights of citizens. A statute of 123 BC, the lēx Semprōnia, established that a Roman citizen could not be put to death without the judgment of the people. A consul who ignored it did not become a strong leader; he became the story. Catiline had real supporters and real grievances behind him, and Cicero knew that the moment he acted outside the law, the argument would stop being about the conspiracy and start being about him.

He was right to worry. A month later, with the plot proven, the Senate passed the emergency decree that modern historians call the senātūs cōnsultum ultimum, and the arrested conspirators were executed without trial. Caesar argued against it. Five years after that, a tribune named Clodius carried a law penalising anyone who had executed a Roman citizen without trial, and Cicero — who by then had no doubt at all whom it was aimed at — left Italy into exile. The most successful act of his career became the charge against him.

Which is why the grammar of this speech is what it is. Cicero is not simply reporting facts; he is managing what might be said, what could be feared, what a reasonable man would do. That is subjunctive territory, and he lives in it for the length of the speech. Nē quis dīcat cōnsulem sevērum esse — "so that nobody may say the consul is harsh." A man in full control of the city, worrying out loud about his reputation, in the mood of things-that-are-not-facts.

🧠 MODULE 6 IN FIVE LINES
LessonThe one thing to keep
6.01Mood is not tense. Present subjunctive: 1st conjugation swaps ā for ē; everything else adds ā. Amem, moneam, regam, capiam, audiam.
6.02Passive = the same vowel with the passive endings: amer, regātur. Deponents use those forms actively: loquātur = "he may speak." Chant sim · possim · eam.
6.03Imperfect subjunctive = present infinitive + endings. No exceptions: amārem, regerem, essem, possem, īrem. Deponents use the would-be active infinitive: loquerer.
6.04-bā- means indicative. -re-/-sse- plus an ending means imperfect subjunctive. And dīcam, capiam, audiam are genuinely ambiguous — the sentence decides, not the form.
6.05In real oratory the subjunctive carries the argument: what might be said, what was feared, what should be done. Cicero needed a mood for hedging because he was a lawyer in a room full of enemies.

You have just read a page of Latin in which fourteen verbs refused to state a fact — and you could name every one of them. That is the whole point of the module.
