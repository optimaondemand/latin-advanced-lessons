📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
There is one measurement that matters at the end of a semester, and it is not your grade.
It is this: put a sentence in front of you that you could not read in September, and read it. That is what this week is for. There is no new grammar in Module 17 — not one form, not one construction. What there is instead is a tour of everything you built, a lesson on how to sit an exam that covers a whole semester, and one long piece of Latin about the Catilinarian conspiracy that you could not have touched sixteen modules ago and can now read end to end.

📚 PART 1 · TWO SENTENCES, SIXTEEN MODULES APART

Here is a sentence you could have read on the first day of this course. Nothing in it is beyond the Latin you brought with you from Latin Intermediate.

Cicerō, coniūrātiōne cognitā, senātōrēs convocāvit et omnia nūntiāvit.
“With the conspiracy discovered, Cicero called the senators together and reported everything.”
An ablative absolute, two perfect indicatives, one conjunction. Solid Latin. Also: nothing but statement.

And here is a sentence about the same afternoon in which, in September, you could not have parsed a single verb.

Cum Cicerō cognōvisset quid coniūrātī parāvissent, verēbātur nē rēs pūblica, quae sibi dēfendenda erat, ūnā nocte dēlērētur.
“When Cicero had found out what the conspirators had prepared, he was afraid that the republic — which he himself had to defend — might be destroyed in a single night.”

Count what is in it. Five constructions, from five different modules, in twenty words:

The LatinWhat it isWhere you got it
Cum…cognōvissetcum + pluperfect subjunctiveModule 10 gave you the clause; Module 13 gave you the tense.
quid…parāvissentindirect question, pluperfect subjunctiveModule 11, again with Module 13's “already finished” tense.
verēbāturdeponent verb, imperfect indicativeModule 2 — passive in form, active in meaning.
nē…dēlērēturfear clause — “that it might be…”Module 12, and the nē that reverses.
quae sibi dēfendenda eratgerundive of obligation + dative of agent, inside a relative clauseModule 5, tucked inside a construction you already owned.
💡 Notice what the second sentence can do that the first cannot. The first one reports events. The second one reports a mind — what Cicero knew, what he did not know, what he feared, and what he believed he was obliged to do. Nearly every construction you added this semester does that same kind of work: it lets Latin say something about the relationship between a fact and a person. That is not decoration. It is the difference between reading a chronicle and reading a historian.

📚 PART 2 · THE FIVE THINGS THIS SEMESTER ADDED

Seventeen modules is a lot of weeks and not very many ideas. Here is the whole semester as five additions:

#What was addedModulesWhat it let you do
1Deponent and semi-deponent verbsm02, m03Read a whole class of extremely common verbs — loquor, sequor, patior, morior, cōnor, proficīscor, arbitror, ūtor, vereor, audeō, soleō, gaudeō, fīdō — that had been invisible to you.
2Gerunds and gerundivesm04, m05Turn an action into a noun (ars dīcendī, “the art of speaking”) and say that something must be done, and by whom (rēs pūblica nōbīs dēfendenda est).
3The subjunctive mood — all four tenses, active and passivem06, m13Read the mood Latin uses for anything that is not a plain assertion of fact: present, imperfect, perfect, pluperfect.
4Six subordinate constructions that use it, plus the rule that picks the tensem07–m12Purpose · indirect command · result · cum (temporal, causal, concessive) · indirect question · fear clause. All governed by sequence of tenses.
5The subjunctive outside a subordinate clause, conditions, and the irregular verbsm14, m15Jussive, hortatory, optative and deliberative subjunctives · conditions that are not facts · ferō, eō, volō, nōlō, mālō, fīō.
✍️ Three of those five are about one mood. Deponents and gerundives are real additions, but the spine of this semester is the subjunctive: its forms (Lesson 17.02), the constructions that use it (17.03), and the discipline of telling them apart when nobody labels them for you (17.04, and the whole of Module 16). If you revise nothing else this week, revise the mood.

★ ADMĪRĀTIŌ · WONDER

Admīrātiō, admīrātiōnis f. is wonder — the state of being struck by something. It is built on mīror, mīrārī, mīrātus sum, “to marvel at,” which is a deponent verb you learned in Module 2, with ad- in front of it: wonder directed at something. The same root gives English admire, miracle, and mirror — a thing you look into and are surprised by.

This course opened with admīrātiō in Module 0 and closes with it here, and that is deliberate. In September the wonder was ahead of you: a language you had two years of, about to open into something harder. The wonder available to you now is different and better, because it is retrospective. You can look at verēbātur nē rēs pūblica dēlērētur and see, without effort, a man's fear about a state — and four months ago that string of letters would have told you nothing at all.

Wonder is not the same as feeling pleased with yourself. Being pleased looks at you; wonder looks at the thing. What is actually remarkable here is not that you worked hard, though you did. It is that a language spoken by people who have been dead for two thousand years still carries their thinking accurately enough that a twelve-year-old in Florida can pick up a sentence and find a Roman consul's mind still in it. That is the thing to marvel at this week. Mīrāre — look at it, and be surprised.
