How to Sit a Cumulative Exam
Lesson · Procedure
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A long Latin sentence is not a harder sentence. It is several ordinary sentences stacked inside each other.
Two things today, and the second is the real one. First, five habits for sitting an exam that covers a whole semester — the sort of advice that sounds obvious and that almost nobody actually does. Then a thirty-five-word Latin sentence with seven constructions in it, taken apart clause by clause in front of you, using a procedure you can run on anything. Then you do it again yourself on a second sentence.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to find the main verb of a long Latin sentence before I try to translate any of it.
2
I will be able to mark the boundaries of every subordinate clause and name each one by its opening word and mood.
3
I will be able to use two exam attempts deliberately instead of accidentally.
📚 PART 1 · FIVE HABITS WORTH MORE THAN ANOTHER HOUR OF REVISION
#The habitWhy it earns points
1On a form-production question, say the sequence out loud before you write anything.Every short-answer form item on the Final gives you the main verb on purpose. “Rogāvit is perfect → secondary → imperfect subjunctive” takes four seconds and prevents the single commonest error on the exam, which is a correctly built form in the wrong tense.
2On a construction question, find the main verb before you look at ut or nē.Yesterday's tables all turned on this. A verb of fearing makes nē mean “that”; a verb of commanding makes the ut clause the content of an order; anything else makes it purpose. The little word is the last thing to look at, not the first.
3On a best-translation question, read all four options to the end before choosing.These items are built so that two options are fluent English and only one is a correct reading of the Latin. The wrong-but-fluent option is usually second in the list, and if you stop reading when something sounds right you will take it.
4Write down the number of every item you were unsure of, even the ones you got right.Because you have two attempts. Between them, that list is your entire revision plan — targeted, short, and generated by the exam itself rather than by guessing what might be on it.
5Do not offer a form you cannot parse.If you cannot say what tense, voice, mood, person and number a form is, you have not produced it — you have remembered a shape. On a 50-item exam a shape you cannot parse will let you down about half the time. Build it from the rule instead.
⚠️ One honest warning about macrons. The short-answer items on the Final accept both the fully macronned spelling and the plain-ASCII spelling — loquerētur and loqueretur are both marked correct, because not every keyboard can produce a macron. What is not accepted is the wrong vowel or the wrong ending. So type the form however your keyboard lets you, and spend your attention on getting the ending right.
📚 PART 2 · THE PROCEDURE FOR A LONG SENTENCE — SIX STEPS

Notice that steps 1 to 5 involve no translation at all. That is deliberate. The mistake almost everyone makes with a long sentence is to start translating at the first word, which means guessing at the structure from inside it.

StepDo thisNotes
1Read the whole sentence to the end. Do not translate.You are looking for shape, not meaning. Where are the commas? Where are the little words?
2Mark every finite verb.Finite means it has a person and number: -t, -nt, -mus, -tis, -m, -s or the passive equivalents. Participles and infinitives are not finite and can never be the main verb. Each finite verb is one clause.
3Find the main verb: the finite verb whose clause has no subordinating word in front of it.Subordinating words: ut, nē, cum, sī, nisi, quamquam, quia, quod, a relative pronoun, a question word. Cross out every verb that has one of these in front of its clause. What is left is your main verb.
4Draw the clause boundaries.A subordinating word opens a clause; its own finite verb usually closes it. Clauses nest — one can sit entirely inside another. Latin does this far more happily than English.
5Name each clause: opening word + mood.This is Lesson 17.03's master table, applied. Write the label next to the clause. Do not skip this and hope.
6Now translate, starting from the main clause and working outward.Translate the main clause first even if it sits in the middle of the sentence, then hang each subordinate clause off it. Rearrange freely into English word order — that is not cheating, it is translating.
📜 PART 3 · THE WORKED EXAMPLE — SEVEN CONSTRUCTIONS, THIRTY-FIVE WORDS
This sentence is the adaptation's own Latin, written for this lesson out of the events of 5 December 63 BC. It is not a sentence from Sallust or from Cicero, and it should not be quoted as one. The historical frame is sound — on that day Cicero, as consul, convened the Senate in the aedēs Concordiae, the Temple of Concord, and the question before the house was what should be done with the arrested conspirators — but the words are ours, built to hold seven of this semester's constructions at once.

 Cum Cicerō cognōvisset quid coniūrātī nocte ēgissent, veritus nē rēs pūblica, cuius salūs sibi cūranda erat, ūnā nocte dēlērētur, senātōrēs in aedem Concordiae convocāvit ut ipsī statuerent utrum coniūrātī pūniendī essent an in custōdiam trādendī.

📖 GLOSS — EVERY WORD NOT ON YOUR RUNNING LIST, IN THE FORM IT APPEARS
  • ēgissent — pluperfect subjunctive of agō, agere, ēgī: to do, drive, carry on. Here “had done, had been up to”
  • veritus — perfect participle of the deponent vereor, verērī, veritus sum, “to fear.” Deponent, so active in meaning: “fearing, having feared”
  • cuius — genitive singular of the relative pronoun: “whose”
  • salūs, salūtis f. — safety, welfare, survival
  • cūranda — gerundive of cūrō, cūrāre, “to look after, take care of,” feminine nominative singular
  • sibi — dative of the reflexive: “by him(self),” here the dative of agent that the gerundive of obligation requires
  • ūnā nocte — ablative: “in a single night”
  • aedem — accusative singular of aedēs, aedis f.: a temple, a building · Concordiae — genitive: “of Concord”
  • ipsī — nominative plural of ipse: “they themselves”
  • statuerent — imperfect subjunctive of statuō, statuere: to decide, determine
  • pūniendī — gerundive of pūniō, pūnīre, “to punish,” masculine nominative plural
  • custōdia, custōdiae f. — custody, confinement · in custōdiam — into custody
  • trādendī — gerundive of trādō, trādere, “to hand over,” masculine nominative plural. The essent is not repeated after it — Latin leaves it to be understood from the first half

Step 2 — Mark every finite verb

There are six: cognōvisset, ēgissent, erat, dēlērētur, convocāvit, statuerent — and a seventh, essent, near the end. Seven finite verbs, seven clauses.

💡 And notice what is not on that list. Veritus looks like a verb and behaves like one, but it is a participle — no person, no number, no tense of its own. It cannot be the main verb of anything. Students who choose veritus at step 3 lose the whole sentence, which is exactly why it is sitting there.

Seven clauses, from six different modules, and three of them are nested inside others. That nesting is why the sentence looks impossible and is not.

Step 6 — Now translate, from the middle outward

First the main clause, alone:
“He called the senators together into the Temple of Concord.”
Then hang each clause off it, in the order the Latin gives them:
“When Cicero had found out what the conspirators had done in the night, fearing that the republic — whose safety he himself had to look after — might be destroyed in a single night, he called the senators together into the Temple of Concord so that they themselves might decide whether the conspirators had to be punished or handed over into custody.”
✍️ Read that English again and notice that it is a perfectly ordinary sentence. Long, but ordinary: a man finds something out, is afraid of a consequence, and calls a meeting to get a decision made. There was never anything difficult in the content. The difficulty was entirely in the packing — and packing is a thing you can unpack mechanically, with six steps and no cleverness at all.
🧠 PART 4 · NOW YOU DO ONE
Also the adaptation's own Latin — and the counterfactual in it is the adaptation's own judgement, not Sallust's. What is historically sound is the choice it describes: Caesar had proposed that the conspirators be kept under guard in the towns of Italy, and Cato spoke after Cicero and carried the vote the other way.

 Nisi Catō, cum omnēs tacērent, surrēxisset ut sententiam dīceret, senātus fortasse cōnstituisset coniūrātōs in oppida Ītaliae esse trādendōs.

📖 GLOSS
  • Catō, Catōnis m. — Cato, the senator who spoke last in the debate of 5 December
  • surrēxisset — pluperfect subjunctive of surgō, surgere, surrēxī: to rise, stand up
  • sententia, sententiae f. — opinion, formal proposal, vote
  • fortasse — perhaps
  • cōnstituisset — pluperfect subjunctive of cōnstituō, cōnstituere: to decide
  • oppidum, oppidī n. — town · in oppida Ītaliae — into the towns of Italy
  • trādendōs — gerundive of trādō, trādere, masculine accusative plural, agreeing with coniūrātōs

Run the six steps. Five questions, one per step — do not skip ahead.

✅ CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING · ONE STEP AT A TIME
Work the procedure on the sentence above.
Step 2 — how many finite verbs does the sentence contain?

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Find the main verb first. Not the first verb, not the nearest verb — the one with nothing in front of its clause. Everything else in a Latin sentence is hanging off that word, and once you have it, the sentence has a spine and you are reading instead of guessing.
