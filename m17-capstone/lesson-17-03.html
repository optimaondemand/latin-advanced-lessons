📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A subjunctive form tells you the tense. It does not tell you the meaning. The opening word does.
Yesterday you could parse dīceret. That is genuinely worth something — and by itself it does not let you translate a single sentence, because dīceret means “in order that he might speak,” or “that he should speak,” or “so that he spoke,” or “when he was speaking,” or “since he was speaking,” or “although he was speaking,” depending entirely on the word in front of it. Today: the seven subordinate constructions, the four main-clause uses, the four jobs of nē, the three jobs of ut, and the two tables that sort all of it.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name all seven subordinate subjunctive constructions and the word that opens each one.
2
I will be able to say all four jobs of nē and explain which one is the odd one out.
3
I will be able to pick the right tense of the subjunctive from the main verb plus one further question.
📚 PART 1 · THE MASTER TABLE: SEVEN SUBORDINATE CONSTRUCTIONS

Read it down the opening word column. That is the column your eye uses when you read Latin.

ConstructionModuleOpens withEnglishThe test
Purposem08ut · negative nē“in order that…may”
“so that…not”		Answers why the main action happened. No signpost word in the main clause.
Indirect commandm08ut · negative nē, after imperō, rogō, moneō, persuādeō, cēnseō“that he should…”
“ordered/asked him to…”		The main verb is a verb of commanding or asking. The clause is what was ordered, not why.
Resultm09ut · negative ut nōn“so that…(and it happened)”A signpost in the main clause: tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō, ita. And the negative is ut nōn, never nē.
Cum clausem10cum + subjunctive“when / after”
“since / because”
“although”		Three meanings, one spelling. Tamen in the main clause is your best single clue for “although.” Otherwise decide from sense.
Indirect questionm11a question word: quis, quid, cūr, ubi, quandō, quōmodo, quantus, quot, uter · or num, -ne, utrum…an, necne“he asked what…”
“did not know whether…”		The main verb asks, knows, does not know, perceives or finds out. The question mark is gone.
Fear clausem12nē = “that” · nē nōn (or rarely ut) = “that…not”“I am afraid that it will happen”The main verb is timeō, vereor, metuō — or the noun metus est. A verb of fearing reverses nē.
Conditionm15sī · nisi = “unless”“should…would”
“were…would be”
“had…would have”		Both halves match in tense. See Part 4.
💡 And the constructions that look like these and are not. A subordinate clause with an indicative verb is never one of the seven above. Cum + indicative is plain “when.” Quamquam + indicative is “although.” Quia and quod + indicative are “because.” Quī with an antecedent and an indicative is a relative clause. And an indirect statement reports with an accusative subject and an infinitive and no subjunctive at all — which is why, after a semester of subjunctives, it is the construction students most often mis-name.
📚 PART 2 · THE FOUR JOBS OF NĒ AND THE THREE JOBS OF UT

These two words carry more of this semester than any other two words in the language. Memorise both tables.

NĒ is doing…ModuleExampleWhat decides it
1. negative purposem08Cicerō tacuit nē Catilīna suspicārētur.
“so that Catiline would not suspect”		Main verb is an ordinary action; the clause answers why.
2. negative indirect commandm08Cicerō cīvēs monuit nē timērent.
“warned them not to be afraid”		Main verb commands, warns, asks, persuades.
3. FEAR CLAUSE — and here nē is positivem12Cicerō timēbat nē coniūrātī effugerent.
“was afraid that they would escape”		Main verb is a verb of fearing. This is the odd one out, and the single most common error in the semester.
4. negative jussive, hortatory or optativem14Nē dubitēmus. “Let us not hesitate.”
Nē veniat. “Let him not come.”
Utinam nē vēnisset! “Would that he had not come!”		There is no main clause other than this one. The subjunctive is the main verb — and it is a willed one: a command, an exhortation, a wish.
✍️ One main-clause subjunctive does not take nē: the deliberative question. An ego nōn venīrem? — “Was I not to come?” The negative there is nōn, not nē, and the reason is worth holding onto: nē negates something willed — an order, an exhortation, a wish. A deliberative question wills nothing. It is genuinely asking, so it takes the ordinary negative that any other question would take. Three of the four main-clause uses take nē; the one that is a question takes nōn.
UT is doing…ModuleExampleWhat decides it
1. purposem08Cōnsul mīlitēs mīsit ut urbem dēfenderent.Answers why. No signpost.
2. indirect commandm08Cicerō imperāvit ut signa agnōscerent.Main verb commands or asks.
3. resultm09Catō tam ācriter dīxit ut omnēs audīrent.A signpost word in the main clause. Negative is ut nōn.
(4. the rare fear-clause ut)m12Timeō ut veniat. — “I am afraid he will not come.”Only after a verb of fearing, and rare. Most authors write nē nōn instead.
⚠️ Look at what these two tables have in common. In every single row, the thing that decides the construction is the main verb, not ut or nē themselves. That is why the procedure on the Final and in Lesson 17.04 always starts in the same place: find the main verb first, and ask what kind of verb it is. Verbs of fearing, verbs of commanding and everything else are three different worlds, and the little word means something different in each.

✍️ Two things this grid gets right that students get wrong. First, future is primary. Cognōscet quot maneant — a future main verb takes the present subjunctive, because there are only two rows and future lives in the top one. Second, purpose and result clauses use only the left column. You cannot have a purpose that was already finished before the action it was the purpose of; so in practice purpose, result, indirect command and fear clauses live in the left-hand column, and it is cum clauses and indirect questions that make real use of the right.
📚 PART 4 · CONDITIONS, AND THE SUBJUNCTIVE AS A MAIN VERB  m14 + m15

A condition has two halves: the protasis (the sī clause) and the apodosis (the main clause). In Latin the two halves match, and the pair of tenses is the whole diagnosis.

TypeProtasis + apodosisLatinEnglish
Simple / factual
known since Latin Intermediate		indicative + indicativeSī Catilīna in urbe est, perīculum est.“If Catiline is in the city, there is danger.” No comment on whether he is.
Future less vividpresent subj. + present subj.Sī Catilīna veniat, cīvēs timeant.“Should Catiline come, the citizens would be afraid.” Possible, not expected.
Contrary to fact, presentimperfect subj. + imperfect subj.Sī Catilīna in urbe esset, cīvēs timērent.“If Catiline were in the city, the citizens would be afraid.” He is not.
Contrary to fact, pastpluperfect subj. + pluperfect subj.Nisi Cicerō vigilāvisset, rēs pūblica dēlēta esset.“If Cicero had not been watchful, the republic would have been destroyed.” He was; it was not.

And the four places the subjunctive stands as the main verb of its own sentence, with no ut, no cum, no question word and nothing above it:

UsePersonLatinEnglish · and the negative
Jussive3rdVeniat. · Nē veniat.“Let him come.” · “Let him not come.” Negative nē.
Hortatory1st pluralDīcāmus. · Nē dubitēmus.“Let us speak.” · “Let us not hesitate.” Negative nē.
OptativeanyUtinam adsit! · Utinam adesset! · Utinam vēnisset!present = an open wish · imperfect = a wish for what is not so now · pluperfect = a wish about the past. Negative utinam nē.
Deliberative1st, usuallyQuid faciam? · Quid facerem? · An ego nōn venīrem?“What am I to do?” · “What was I to do?” · “Was I not to come?” A real question with no good answer. Negative nōn — not nē. This is the one main-clause use that does not take nē, because it wills nothing; see the note in Part 2.
💡 The single most useful sort in the semester. Before you ask which construction a subjunctive belongs to, ask whether it belongs to one at all. If there is a subordinating word in front of it — ut, nē, cum, sī, nisi, a question word — it is subordinate, and the master table applies. If there is nothing in front of it and no other verb in the sentence for it to depend on, it is a main-clause subjunctive, and you are in Module 14. That one question sorts more sentences than any other.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

The subjunctive never tells you what a sentence means. The main verb and the opening word tell you what it means; the subjunctive only tells you when. Every table on this page is a version of that one sentence.
