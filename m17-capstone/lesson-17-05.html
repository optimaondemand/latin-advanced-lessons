Lesson 17.05
Dē Rē Pūblicā Dēfendendā — On Defending the Republic
Capstone Reading · Whole-Semester Review
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have read this story eleven times in pieces. Today you read it whole.
Since Module 0 this course has been circling one year: 63 BC, one consul, one conspiracy, and the question of what a republic is allowed to do to save itself. Every module gave you a fragment — a character sketch, a meeting in a private house, a speech, an arrest at a bridge, a night in a prison, a battle in a valley in Etruria. Today those fragments are one passage of fourteen lines, from Catiline's character to his death, and you can read all of it. Not because the Latin has been made easy. Because you have the grammar it is made of.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to read a fourteen-line adapted narrative of the whole conspiracy and name the construction in every line.
2
I will be able to tell the story of 63 BC in order, from Catiline's failed elections to Pistoria.
3
I will be able to state the whole semester in one table and start my Review Activity and Final without re-reading seventeen modules.
📚 PART 1 · BEFORE YOU READ
1. The title is itself a piece of this semester's grammar. Dē rē pūblicā dēfendendā — dē takes the ablative, rē pūblicā is ablative, and dēfendendā is a gerundive agreeing with it: “concerning the republic-to-be-defended,” which English turns into “on defending the republic.” In September that title would have been three words of noise. Read it again now.
2. Two voices, one story. The passage is stitched from both of the writers this course has used. Sallust (Bellum Catilīnae) is the historian, writing about twenty years after the events, interested in why Rome had come to this. Cicero (In Catilīnam I–IV) is the consul who was actually in the room, publishing his own speeches afterwards. Where line 7 quotes Cicero, you are hearing a man defending his record as well as his city.
3. What is in these fourteen lines. Six deponent forms — two participles, two perfects, an infinitive and a future; one gerund; two gerundives of obligation; a negative indirect command; two purpose clauses; four cum clauses, two with pluperfect subjunctives and two with imperfects; two indirect questions, one of them double; a hortatory subjunctive with nē; the irregular ferō; four relative clauses; and a contrary-to-fact past condition in the last line. That is the entire semester in one paragraph — which is why it is the last thing you read.
💡 Read it once straight through before you touch the gloss. You will not understand all of it that way, and that is fine — you are looking for the shape of the story, not the meaning of every word. Then read it again with the gloss, running the six steps from Lesson 17.04 on the long lines (3, 10, 11 and 14). The order matters: whole first, pieces second.
📜 PART 2 · THE READING
Adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae, and Cicero, In Catilīnam I. Lines 1 and 2 stay close to Sallust's own words; line 7 is Cicero's famous opening, reordered and with one ending modernised (see the note below). Everything else has been shortened, simplified and stitched together for this lesson. This is an adaptation, not the Latin of either author.

1Lūcius Catilīna, nōbilī genere nātus, magnā vī et animī et corporis fuit, sed ingeniō malō prāvōque.

2Huic ab adulēscentiā bella intestīna, caedēs, rapīnae, discordia cīvīlis grāta fuērunt.

3Cum autem cōnsulātum bis petīvisset neque adipīscī potuisset, coniūrātiōnem contrā rem pūblicam fēcit.

4Nam in urbe multī erant quī, magnīs dēbitīs pressī, novās rēs cupiēbant; hīs Catilīna spem rapiendī dabat.

5Hī nocte in aedibus M. Porciī Laecae convēnērunt ut ducem suum audīrent; ille eōs hortātus est nē tantum perīculum timērent.

6Sed Cicerō omnia iam cognōverat: Fulvia enim, mulier nōbilis, ad eum tulerat quae ex Q. Curiō audīverat.

7Itaque cōnsul in senātū: “Quō ūsque tandem,” inquit, “Catilīna, abūtēris patientiā nostrā?”

8Quae cum ita essent, Catilīna eā ipsā nocte ex urbe profectus est et ad Manlium in Etrūriam contendit.

9Deinde lēgātī Allobrogum, quibus coniūrātī litterās dederant, ad pontem Mulvium comprehēnsī sunt.

10Cum litterae in senātū recitātae essent, nēmō iam dubitāre poterat quantum scelus parātum esset.

11Tum senātus, cum quīnque coniūrātī in custōdiā tenērentur, cōnsultāvit utrum eī pūniendī essent an in oppida Ītaliae trādendī.

12Catō, ut sententiam dīceret, surrēxit: “Nē diūtius dubitēmus; dē salūte omnium nostrum agitur.”

13Itaque eā nocte coniūrātī in carcerem dēductī et necātī sunt; Cicerō autem, ē carcere ēgressus, ūnum verbum dīxit: “Vīxērunt.”

14Postrēmō Catilīna in agrō Pistōriēnsī fortiter pugnāns cecidit; et nisi Cicerō tam vigilāns fuisset, rēs pūblica illā hieme dēlēta esset.

  • 1 · nōbilī genere — of noble family (ablative) · nātus — born (perfect participle of the deponent nāscor, nāscī, nātus sum) · magnā vī — with great force, energy (ablative of vīs) · animī et corporis — of mind and of body · ingeniō malō prāvōque — but with a bad and crooked nature (ablative of description; ingenium — natural character; prāvus, -a, -um — crooked, perverse)

  • 3 · cōnsulātum — the consulship (accusative) · bis — twice · adipīscī — to obtain (present infinitive of the deponent adipīscor) · contrā (+ acc.) — against
  • 4 · dēbitīs — by debts (ablative plural of dēbitum, dēbitī n.) · pressī — crushed, weighed down (perfect passive participle of premō) · novās rēs — literally “new things,” i.e. revolution · hīs — to these men (dative) · spem rapiendī — the hope of plundering (rapiendī is a genitive gerund of rapiō)
  • 5 · in aedibus — in the house of (ablative plural of aedēs, aedis f.) · M. Porciī Laecae — of Marcus Porcius Laeca (genitive) · ducem suum — their own leader · hortātus est — he urged (perfect of the deponent hortor) · tantum perīculum — so great a danger
  • 6 · enim — for, in fact (never stands first) · mulier, mulieris f. — woman · tulerat — had brought (pluperfect of the irregular ferō, ferre, tulī, lātum) · quae — the things which (neuter accusative plural) · ex Q. Curiō — from Quintus Curius
  • 7 · Quō ūsque tandem — “How far, in the end…?” i.e. “How long is this going to go on?” · abūtēris — will you abuse, will you go on exploiting (2nd singular future of the deponent abūtor, + ablative) · patientiā nostrā — our patience (ablative, governed by abūtor)
  • 8 · Quae cum ita essent — a fixed Ciceronian phrase: “since these things were so.” A causal cum clause (m10) with the imperfect subjunctive essent · eā ipsā nocte — that very night · profectus est — he set out (perfect of the deponent proficīscor) · ad Manlium — to Manlius, who was commanding Catiline's forces in Etruria · contendit — he hurried, marched
  • 9 · Deinde — then, next · lēgātī — envoys · Allobrogum — of the Allobroges, a Gallic people (genitive plural) · quibus — to whom (dative plural) · ad pontem Mulvium — at the Mulvian Bridge, on the northern road out of Rome · comprehēnsī sunt — they were arrested
  • 10 · recitātae essent — had been read aloud (pluperfect subjunctive passive of recitō) · nēmō — no one · dubitāre — to doubt · quantum scelus — how great a crime (scelus, sceleris n.) · parātum esset — had been prepared (pluperfect subjunctive passive of parō)
  • 11 · quīnque — five · in custōdiā — in custody (custōdia, custōdiae f.) · tenērentur — were being held (imperfect subjunctive passive of teneō) · cōnsultāvit — deliberated, took counsel · eī — they, those men (nominative plural of is) · pūniendī — to be punished (gerundive of pūniō) · oppida — towns (accusative plural of oppidum) · trādendī — to be handed over (gerundive of trādō; the essent is not repeated and must be understood)
  • 12 · sententiam — his formal opinion, his vote (sententia, sententiae f.) · surrēxit — stood up (perfect of surgō) · diūtius — any longer · dubitēmus — let us hesitate (present subjunctive, 1st plural — hortatory, negated with nē) · salūs, salūtis f. — safety, survival · omnium nostrum — of all of us · agitur — it is at stake, the matter concerns (impersonal passive of agō)
  • 13 · in carcerem — into the prison (carcer, carceris m.) · dēductī…sunt — were led down · necātī sunt — were put to death · autem — however (never stands first) · ēgressus — having come out (perfect participle of the deponent ēgredior) · Vīxērunt — “They have lived” — a Roman way of saying they are dead
  • 14 · Postrēmō — finally, in the end · in agrō Pistōriēnsī — in the territory of Pistoria, in Etruria · fortiter — bravely · pugnāns — fighting (present active participle) · cecidit — he fell (perfect of cadō) · nisi — if…not, unless · vigilāns — watchful, wakeful · illā hieme — that winter (ablative of hiems, hiemis f.) · dēlēta esset — would have been destroyed (pluperfect subjunctive passive of dēleō)
⚠️ Five honest notes about this passage. (1) Lines 1 and 2 are close to Sallust's own sentences about Catiline's character, lightly regularised — Sallust's fuēre has been written out as fuērunt so that you are not asked to parse a form nobody taught you. (2) Line 7 is Cicero's real opening of the First Catilinarian, but he wrote abūtēre, using an older alternative ending for the second person singular; the adaptation gives you abūtēris, the form you were actually taught, and moves the words around to fit inquit. His order was Quō ūsque tandem abūtēre, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā? (3) The two quoted speeches in lines 5 and 12 are the adaptation's own Latin. Catiline did address the conspirators and Cato did speak last in the debate of 5 December — Sallust gives a version of Cato's speech at length — but these particular sentences are ours, written to put an indirect command and a hortatory subjunctive where you can see them. (4) The single word Vīxērunt is reported by Plutarch, writing much later, who places it on Cicero's walk home rather than at the prison door. Sallust does not mention it at all. Treat it as a good story that may be true, not as a quotation from an eyewitness. (5) The counterfactual in line 14 — that the republic would have been destroyed — is the adaptation's judgement, not Sallust's. Sallust does not say it, and as the cultural note below explains, plenty of Romans did not think it either.

🏛️ THE ROMAN WORLD · WHAT DEFENDING THE REPUBLIC COST

The last line of the reading says the republic would have been destroyed. Cicero certainly thought so, and he said so for the rest of his life. Not everyone agreed — and the disagreement was not academic. Five years after that December, in 58 BC, the tribune Publius Clodius Pulcher carried a law penalising anyone who had put a Roman citizen to death without a trial. It named nobody. It did not need to. Cicero left Italy, and the house he had bought on the Palatine was pulled down. He was recalled the following year, but the charge never really let go of him: the man who saved the state, or the consul who executed citizens without a court.

Sallust, writing about twenty years later, refuses to settle it for you. He will not praise Catiline and he will not excuse him — and his real argument is bleaker than either: that Roman mōrēs, the whole shared character of the city, had been rotting for generations, and that Catiline was a symptom rather than a cause. Which leaves the question this semester has been asking since Module 0 exactly where the Romans left it. Rēs pūblica means “the public thing,” the business that belongs to everybody. What is a person allowed to do to save a thing that belongs to everybody? Cicero answered one way, Clodius another, and Rome spent the next thirty years finding out that neither answer held.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Go back and read line 7 one more time. Quō ūsque tandem, Catilīna, abūtēris patientiā nostrā? Cicero said something extremely close to that out loud, in a temple, to a man who was sitting in the room, on a day in November more than two thousand years ago. He published it because he wanted to be remembered saying it. And it worked: you just read it.

That is admīrātiō — not being pleased with yourself, but being struck by the thing in front of you. A dead language kept a living man's voice intact well enough to reach a twelve-year-old on the other side of the world. Whatever your score on the Final turns out to be, that already happened, and nobody can take it back off you.
