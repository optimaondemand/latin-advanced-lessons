📚 PART 1 · DEPONENTS AND SEMI-DEPONENTS  m02, m03

A deponent verb has laid down its active forms (Latin dēpōnere, “to lay down”) and uses passive endings with active meaning. It has three principal parts, not four, and the third one is a participle plus sum.

A semi-deponent is only half of that. It is active in the present system (present, imperfect, future) and deponent in the perfect system (perfect, pluperfect, future perfect). There are four to know:

Principal partsPresent systemPerfect systemMeaning
audeō, audēre, ausus sumaudet — he daresausus est — he daredto dare
soleō, solēre, solitus sumsolēbat — he was accustomedsolitus est — he was accustomedto be accustomed
gaudeō, gaudēre, gāvīsus sumgaudet — he rejoicesgāvīsus est — he rejoicedto rejoice

⚠️ The one thing that gets marked wrong on exams. Because the perfect of a deponent or semi-deponent is a participle plus sum, the participle must agree with its subject in gender and number. Fulvia ausa est, not Fulvia ausus est. Coniūrātī profectī sunt, not profectus sunt. On the Final, when a form-production item names a masculine or feminine subject, that is what it is testing.

And the one construction Module 5 exists for — the gerundive of obligation, gerundive + a form of esse, with the person who must act in the dative:

LatinEnglish
Rēs pūblica nōbīs dēfendenda est.“The republic must be defended by us.”
Catilīna cōnsulī pūniendus erat.“Catiline had to be punished by the consul.”
Nōbīs prō patriā pugnandum est.“We must fight for our country.” (impersonal — no object, so the gerundive stays neuter singular)
💡 Why the dative and not ā + ablative? Because the ablative of agent slot is already taken. Rēs pūblica ā Cicerōne dēfenditur means “the republic is being defended by Cicero” — a plain passive. Latin needed a different way to say “is the one who has to do it,” and it reached for the dative, the case of the person concerned. English keeps them both as “by,” which is why this is the item students lose points on: they read nōbīs as an indirect object and reverse the sentence.
📚 PART 3 · THE SUBJUNCTIVE, ACTIVE: PRESENT AND IMPERFECT  m06

📚 PART 4 · THE SUBJUNCTIVE, ACTIVE: PERFECT AND PLUPERFECT  m13

These two are built off the perfect stem — the third principal part with its ending cut off — and they are identical for every conjugation, because the perfect stem does all the work. amāv-, monu-, rēx-, cēp-, audīv-, fu-.

PERFECT subj. — stem + -eri-PLUPERFECT subj. — stem + -isse-What each one means in a clause
1 sg.amāverimamāvissemPERFECT = “already finished” in primary sequence (after a present or future main verb).

PLUPERFECT = “already finished” in secondary sequence (after a past main verb).

Neither one means “perfect” or “pluperfect” the way an indicative does. In a subordinate clause they mean earlier than the main verb.

⚠️ The honest collision, first named in Lesson 13.02. The perfect subjunctive and the future perfect indicative overlap. Fēcerit can be “he will have done” (future perfect indicative) or a perfect subjunctive; only fēcerō and fēcerimus versus fēcerim and fēcerīmus differ. This is not a flaw in your memory. It is a real ambiguity in Latin, and it is settled the same way everything else is: mood is decided by the clause, not by the word. If the form sits after cum, after a question word, after ut or nē, it is subjunctive. If it stands as the main verb of its own sentence, it is indicative.
📚 PART 5 · PASSIVE AND DEPONENT SUBJUNCTIVES  m06, m13

✍️ Deponents look exactly like passives here too. Loquātur and regātur are built identically; the difference is entirely in the dictionary entry. If the verb's principal parts end in -or, -ī, -us sum, the meaning is active however passive the ending looks. This is the Module 2 rule, still true in Module 17, and it never stops mattering.

💡 Three of these are the same verb wearing different clothes. Volō (“I want”), nōlō (from nē + volō, “I want…not”) and mālō (from magis + volō, “I want more”) share one set of endings and one irregularity. Learn volō and you have paid for the other two. And fīō supplies the passive that faciō lacks in the present system: faciō “I make,” fīō “I am made / I become.”
🧠 PART 7 · FORM CARDS — SAY IT BEFORE YOU FLIP IT

Name the form out loud — tense, voice, mood, person, number — and then click to check. Saying it out loud is the whole exercise; reading the back of the card teaches you nothing.

📌 FLIP CARDS · PARSE THE FORM
fēcerit
perfect stem + what?
Perfect subjunctive active, 3rd singular, of faciō. Also, honestly, a possible future perfect indicative — the clause decides.
regerētur
infinitive + endings
Imperfect subjunctive passive, 3rd singular, of regō: “he/it might be ruled.” regere + -tur.
locūtus esset
two words
Pluperfect subjunctive of the deponent loquor, 3rd singular: “he had spoken.” Active in meaning.
capiāmus
-a- or -e-?
Present subjunctive active, 1st plural, of capiō. Third conjugation -iō, so -a-. On its own it could be a hortatory “let us seize.”
vēnissent
-isse-
Pluperfect subjunctive active, 3rd plural, of veniō. Perfect stem vēn- + -issent.
vellet
an irregular
Imperfect subjunctive, 3rd singular, of volō: “he would want.” From the infinitive velle + -t — so it is regular after all, once you know the infinitive.
ausa est
who is the subject?
Perfect of the semi-deponent audeō, 3rd singular, with a feminine subject: “she dared.” Active in meaning.
dēfendenda
noun or adjective?
Gerundive, feminine nominative singular. It needs a feminine singular noun to agree with — e.g. rēs pūblica dēfendenda est.
dīcendī
is there a noun for it?
Genitive gerund — “of speaking” — if nothing agrees with it, as in ars dīcendī. It could also be a gerundive if a masculine genitive singular or nominative plural noun is standing next to it. Look at the neighbours.
fīat
an irregular, and a famous one
Present subjunctive, 3rd singular, of fīō: “let it be made / let it happen.” As a main-verb jussive this is the fīat lūx of the Latin Bible.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Every subjunctive you have ever met comes from one of three places: the present stem with a changed vowel, the present infinitive with endings stuck on, or the perfect stem with -eri- or -isse-. Three sources, four tenses. If you can say which of the three a form came from, you have parsed it.
