The Debate in the Senate

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Five men had been arrested. The Senate had to decide what to do with them.
5 December 63 BC. Caesar argues that Roman citizens should not be executed without trial; Cato argues that these men have stopped being citizens. Sallust gives both speeches at length, and the contrast between them is the heart of his book. Your job today is to read the debate — and to sort five result clauses from one purpose clause and one indirect command, with nothing labelled for you.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to find and parse every result clause in an adapted passage of Sallust, and name its signpost.
2
I will be able to distinguish the purpose clause and the indirect command in the same passage from the result clauses around them.
3
I will be able to explain what Caesar and Cato each argued, and why Sallust reports both at length.
📜 PART 1 · THE READING

Adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 50–53. A simplified Latin retelling of the Senate debate — not Sallust's own sentences. Result clauses are marked in red. The purpose clause and the indirect command are underlined in blue. Read it once straight through for sense before you start parsing.

Nōnīs Decembribus senātus convēnit ut dē coniūrātīs comprehēnsīs cōnsuleret. Tanta erat dissēnsiō ut senātōrēs saepe clāmārent. Prīmus Sīlānus, cōnsul dēsignātus, dīxit coniūrātōs necārī dēbēre.

Deinde Caesar surrēxit. Tam callidē dīxit ut multī eī crēderent. Nōn negāvit coniūrātōs scelus fēcisse; sed poenam novam posterīs perīculōsam futūram esse dīxit. Itaque cēnsuit coniūrātōs in vinculīs per Italiae oppida tenendōs esse et bona eōrum pūblicanda esse. Hoc cēnsuit nē posterī tālī exemplō abūterentur.

Tum Catō surrēxit. Adeō ācriter locūtus est ut omnēs audīrent. Dīxit hōs hominēs nōn cīvēs sed hostēs esse. Tantum erat perīculum ut nōn diūtius cūnctandum esset. Tālis erat ōrātiō Catōnis ut senātōrēs sententiam mūtārent.

Itaque senātus dēcrēvit ut coniūrātī necārentur. Eōdem vespere Lentulus et cēterī in carcerem ductī et necātī sunt. Sallustius autem ōrātiōnem utrīusque virī nārrat et iūdicium lēctōrī relinquit.

📖 VOCABULARY GLOSS · every word not on your running list
  • Nōnīs Decembribus — “on the Nones of December” = 5 December (ablative of time when)
  • convēnit — “met, assembled” (perfect of conveniō)
  • comprehēnsīs — “arrested” (PPP of comprehendō, ablative plural, with coniūrātīs)
  • cōnsuleret — “might deliberate” (imperfect subjunctive of cōnsulō; cōnsulere dē + abl. = “to deliberate about”)
  • dissēnsiō, dissēnsiōnis f. — disagreement
  • saepe (adv.) — often
  • Sīlānus, Sīlānī m. — Silanus (Decimus Iunius Silanus, consul-elect for the coming year)
  • cōnsul dēsignātus — consul-elect, “consul designate”
  • necārī — “to be put to death” (present passive infinitive of necō); necārī dēbēre — “that they ought to be put to death”
  • callidē (adv.) — cleverly, shrewdly (and often with a hint of slyness)
  • negāvit — “he denied” (perfect of negō)
  • scelus, sceleris n. — crime, wickedness
  • fēcisse — “to have committed” (perfect infinitive of faciō, in indirect statement)
  • poena, poenae f. — penalty, punishment
  • posterī, posterōrum m. pl. — those who come after, later generations
  • futūram esse — “would be” (future infinitive of sum, agreeing with poenam)
  • cēnsuit — “he proposed, he moved” (perfect of cēnseō — the formal word for the motion a senator puts forward)
  • vincula, vinculōrum n. pl. — chains; in vinculīs — in custody
  • oppidum, oppidī n. — town; per Italiae oppida — throughout the towns of Italy
  • tenendōs esse — “must be held” (gerundive of obligation, Module 5, inside an indirect statement)
  • bona, bonōrum n. pl. — goods, property
  • pūblicanda esse — “must be confiscated” (gerundive of pūblicō, “to make into public property”)
  • exemplum, exemplī n. — example, precedent
  • abūterentur — “might misuse” (imperfect subjunctive of the deponent abūtor, which takes the ablative)
  • ācriter (adv.) — fiercely, sharply
  • cīvis, cīvis m./f. — citizen
  • hostis, hostis m. — enemy of the state (not inimīcus, a personal enemy — the difference is Cato's whole argument)
  • diūtius (adv.) — any longer
  • cūnctandum esset — “there must be no delaying” (gerundive of obligation from the deponent cūnctor, “to delay,” with the subjunctive of esse — Module 5 and Module 6 working together)
  • ōrātiō, ōrātiōnis f. — speech
  • mūtārent — “changed” (imperfect subjunctive of mūtō)
  • dēcrēvit — “decreed, voted” (perfect of dēcernō)
  • eōdem vespere — on the same evening
  • Lentulus, Lentulī m. — Lentulus (Publius Cornelius Lentulus Sura, the most senior of the arrested men)
  • cēterī, cēterōrum m. pl. — the rest, the others
  • carcer, carceris m. — prison
  • ductī sunt — “they were led” (perfect passive of dūcō)
  • Sallustius, Sallustiī m. — Sallust
  • utrīusque virī — “of each of the two men” (genitive of uterque)
  • nārrat — tells, relates
  • iūdicium, iūdiciī n. — judgment, verdict
  • lēctor, lēctōris m. — reader; lēctōrī — to the reader (dative)
  • relinquit — leaves, leaves behind
✍️ One note about the adaptation. Sallust's own Latin here is much harder than this — long speeches in reported form, packed with constructions you meet in Modules 11 to 15. This retelling keeps the argument and the sequence of events while staying inside the grammar you now have. When you read the real chapters in high-school Latin, you will recognize the shape of what you learned here.
📑 PART 2 · CLAUSE INVENTORY

Work through the passage and classify each ut or nē clause yourself first, using the three-question test. Then check against this table — it is also your revision sheet for the quiz.

🔍 Two things worth noticing. First: every subjunctive in the passage is imperfect, because every main verb is a past tense. One rule, eight clauses, no exceptions — that is secondary sequence doing its job. Second: look at nē posterī tālī exemplō abūterentur. There is a signpost word in it, tālī — but it sits inside the subordinate clause and simply means “such a.” A signpost counts only when it is in the main clause, pointing forward at an ut. Here the nē settles the matter before the signpost can mislead you.
⚠️ Read one sentence twice: Tālis erat ōrātiō Catōnis ut senātōrēs sententiam mūtārent. This is the most important result clause in the passage, and it makes a claim of fact: the speech did change votes. A purpose clause would have said something quite different — that Cato spoke in order to change votes, with no promise that it worked. Sallust is not telling us Cato's intention. He is telling us the outcome, and the outcome is why five men died that evening.
✍️ PART 3 · TRANSLATE, THEN CHECK

Write your English out before flipping. These four are good practice for the graded translation.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Tanta erat dissēnsiō ut senātōrēs saepe clāmārent.
click to compare
“The disagreement was so great that the senators often shouted.” Tanta is feminine nominative singular, agreeing with dissēnsiō. Erat is imperfect, so the subjunctive is imperfect: clāmārent. Also fine: “There was such great disagreement that…”
Tam callidē dīxit ut multī eī crēderent.
click to compare
“He spoke so cleverly that many believed him.” Signpost tam with the adverb callidē. Watch the dative: crēdō takes the dative, so eī is “him,” not “to him.” And note what Sallust's word choice does — callidē is admiring and suspicious at the same time.
Hoc cēnsuit nē posterī tālī exemplō abūterentur.
click to compare
“He proposed this so that later generations would not misuse such a precedent.” Nē, so purpose, not result: this is Caesar's reason. Abūtor is deponent and takes the ablative, hence tālī exemplō. This one sentence is the whole of Caesar's argument in miniature.
Tantum erat perīculum ut nōn diūtius cūnctandum esset.
click to compare
“The danger was so great that there could be no further delay.” (Also good: “…that they must not delay any longer.”) Tantum is neuter, agreeing with perīculum; the negative is ut nōn, so this is a negative result — Cato is claiming a fact about the situation, not stating an aim.
🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · CAESAR, CATO, AND THE HISTORIAN'S VĒRITĀS

Five men were in custody. Everyone in the room agreed they had plotted to burn Rome. The question was what a Roman senate was allowed to do about it. Silanus, the consul-elect, was asked first and moved the death penalty. Then Julius Caesar — still a young politician on the rise, not yet the Caesar of the civil wars — argued against it: confiscate their property, hold them in custody in the towns of Italy for life, but do not kill Roman citizens without trial. His reason was the one in your reading: a precedent set today will be used by somebody else tomorrow.

Then Cato spoke. Marcus Porcius Cato, tribune-elect, great-grandson of the famous censor, and for Romans a byword for rigid integrity, made the case that Caesar's argument rested on a false premise. These men were no longer cīvēs, citizens with a citizen's protections; by taking up arms against Rome they had made themselves hostēs, public enemies, and you do not put an enemy on trial. The Senate voted for execution. That evening the men were taken down into the prison and killed.

Now the honest part, and you already know it from Module 2. The two great speeches in Sallust's account are Sallust's compositions, not transcripts. Nobody in that room was taking shorthand. Ancient historians openly composed speeches to fit what they judged the speaker's character and argument to have been, and Sallust does it here at unusual length — the two speeches are among the most admired pieces of prose in Latin. What we can say with confidence is the shape of the debate: Caesar argued against execution, Cato argued for it, and the executions happened. What Sallust gives us on top of that is his reading of two extraordinary men.

This is vēritās as a historian practises it. Sallust admired Caesar — he had served under him — and he still gives Cato the argument that won. He reports the minority case at full strength instead of quietly letting it lose. Writing about Cato later in the book, Sallust puts it in five words:

SALLUST, BELLUM CATILĪNAE 54 — UNADAPTED, ONE LINE
Esse quam vidērī bonus mālēbat.
“He preferred to be good rather than to seem good.”
Gloss: esse — to be; quam — than; vidērī — to seem (present passive infinitive of videō); bonus — good; mālēbat — “he preferred” (imperfect of the irregular verb mālō — not yet studied; gloss provided, and you meet it in Module 15).

One consequence, five years later. Caesar's warning about precedent turned out to be sound. In 58 BC Cicero, who had presided over the executions as consul, was driven out of Rome by political enemies who used exactly this charge against him: that he had put citizens to death without trial. That is a result clause the length of half a decade — and it is not in Sallust, who says nothing about it. It is what later history did with the evening of 5 December.

🧠 MODULE 9 IN FIVE LINES
LessonThe one thing to keep
9.01A result clause is ut + subjunctive meaning “with the result that.” Same machinery as purpose; opposite job.
9.02Signposts announce it: tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō, sīc/ita. Tantus and tālis agree; the rest never change.
9.03Purpose negates with nē; result negates with ut nōn. And nēmō / nihil / numquam carry their own negative — do not add nōn.
9.04Three questions in order: negative? main verb? signpost? Two of them need no interpretation at all.
9.05Real prose mixes all three constructions in one paragraph — and a result clause is a claim about what actually happened.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A result clause is a claim about the world: this actually followed from that. Purpose only tells you what somebody hoped. Which is why Tālis erat ōrātiō Catōnis ut senātōrēs sententiam mūtārent is a sentence about history and not about Cato's feelings — the votes changed, and five men died that evening.
