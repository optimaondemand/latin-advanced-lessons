Lesson 9.02
The Signposts
Vocabulary & Recognition
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Latin tells you a result clause is coming. You only have to notice.
A good reader of Latin does not wait until the ut to start guessing. Six little words — tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō, sīc/ita — sit in the main clause and lean forward, promising a consequence. Learn to see them and half of this module's work is already done. Learn which ones decline and you will stop making the mistake that costs almost everyone marks.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name the six signpost words and say what each one attaches to.
2
I will be able to make tantus and tālis agree with their nouns, and leave tam, tot, and adeō alone.
3
I will be able to use this module's new vocabulary, and explain why a signpost is a warning rather than a guarantee.
📚 PART 1 · THE SIX SIGNPOSTS

All six mean roughly “so” or “such.” What separates them is what they attach to in the main clause.

SignpostMeansAttaches toExample in a main clause
tamsoan adjective or adverbtam callidus — so clever
tam ācriter — so fiercely
tantus, tanta, tantumso great, so mucha noun — and it agreestantus timor — so great a fear
tanta dissēnsiō — so great a disagreement
tālis, tālesuch, of such a kinda noun — and it agreestālis vir — such a man
tāle scelus — such a crime
totso manya plural noun — never changestot coniūrātī — so many conspirators
tot verba — so many words
adeōto such a degree, so muchusually a verbadeō timēbant — they were afraid to such a degree
sīc · itaso, in such a waya verbsīc dīxit — he spoke in such a way
ita rem gessit — he handled the matter in such a way
💡 Two of them are near-twins. Sīc and ita both mean “so, in this way,” and for reading purposes you can treat them as one item. Adeō is close to them too; the difference is that sīc and ita describe the manner of the action (“in such a way”) while adeō describes its degree (“to such an extent”). You will also meet adeō attached to an adjective or adverb from time to time; when that happens it is doing tam's job, and nothing about the result clause changes.
📚 PART 2 · THE TRAP: WHO DECLINES AND WHO DOES NOT
ADJECTIVES — THEY AGREE
tantus, -a, -um · tālis, tāle
They describe a noun, so they take that noun's gender, number, and case — like any adjective you have ever met.
ADVERBS AND ONE ODDITY — NEVER CHANGE
tam · adeō · sīc · ita · tot
Four adverbs, plus tot, which is an indeclinable word. There is no such form as tamus, tama, tamum, or totī meaning “so many.”

Tantus agreeing, four times over

Tantus declines like magnus, -a, -um — a perfectly ordinary 1st/2nd-declension adjective. Read this table across:

NounGender, case, numberCorrect formMeans

⚠️ The mistake almost everyone makes once. Students who have just learned that tantus agrees start making tam agree too, and write things like tama fortis or tamus timor. Those are not Latin words. Tam is an adverb; adverbs do not decline, ever. The same goes for adeō, sīc, ita, and (although it looks like an adjective) tot. Say it once and keep it: tantus and tālis dress to match. The rest wear the same clothes every day.
🧠 PART 3 · PICK THE RIGHT SIGNPOST

Five rows. Ask two questions each time: what does the signpost attach to? and does that word need agreement?

Complete each main clause with the correct signpost.

Caesar ____ callidus erat ut multī eī crēderent. (“Caesar was so clever that…”)
____ erat dissēnsiō ut senātōrēs saepe clāmārent. (“The disagreement was so great that…”)

____ timēbant ut nēmō loquerētur. (“They were afraid to such a degree that no one spoke.”)
⚠️ PART 4 · A WARNING, NOT A GUARANTEE

Signposts are the fastest clue you have, and they are right the great majority of the time. But they are clues, not laws. All six of these words are perfectly ordinary Latin in their own right, and they can appear with no result clause anywhere in sight:

Ita Cicerō dīxit.
“Cicero spoke in this way.” Full stop. No ut, no result clause — just an adverb doing its plain job.
Caesar tālī exemplō nōn crēdēbat.
“Caesar did not trust such a precedent.” Tālī is a signpost word, correctly agreeing with exemplō (ablative singular) — but nothing follows it. Gloss: exemplum, exemplī n. — example, precedent.
🔍 So what is the rule? A signpost tells you to look ahead for ut plus a subjunctive. If you find one, you have a result clause and you can translate with confidence. If you do not, the signpost is simply an intensifier and you translate it as “so,” “such,” or “in this way.” Two words together make the construction: the signpost and the ut clause.
📖 MODULE 9 VOCABULARY

The six signposts, plus the words the Senate-debate reading will need. Add all of these to your running list.

  • tam (adv.) — so (with an adjective or adverb)
  • tantus, tanta, tantum — so great, so much (agrees with its noun)
  • tālis, tāle — such, of such a kind (agrees with its noun)
  • tot (indeclinable) — so many
  • adeō (adv.) — to such a degree, so much, so far
  • sīc, ita (adv.) — so, thus, in such a way
  • crēdō, crēdere, crēdidī, crēditum (+ dative) — to believe, trust (crēdō Cicerōnī — “I believe Cicero”)
  • sententia, sententiae f. — opinion, vote, motion (in the Senate, the formal opinion a senator gives when asked)
  • dissēnsiō, dissēnsiōnis f. — disagreement, dissent
  • supplicium, suppliciī n. — punishment; the death penalty
  • carcer, carceris m. — prison
  • scelus, sceleris n. — crime, wickedness
  • exemplum, exemplī n. — example, precedent
  • ācriter (adv.) — fiercely, sharply, keenly (from ācer, ācris, ācre)
  • callidē (adv.) — cleverly, shrewdly (from callidus, -a, -um — clever, and often slightly sly)
  • diū (adv.) — for a long time; diūtius — longer, any longer
  • postrēmō (adv.) — finally, at last
  • pateō, patēre, patuī — to lie open, be evident; patet — “it is obvious”
📜 DERIVATIVE MINUTE · CLICK EACH CARD
crēdō
what English words?
credible, incredible, credit, credence, creed, credential. Every one of them is about trust — a creed is what you believe, a credential is what makes you believable.
tālis
what English word?
retaliate. Through Latin tāliō, punishment “of such a kind” as the offence — the old law of like for like. To retaliate is to give back the same sort of thing.
pateō
what English words?
patent, patently. Something patent lies open to view. A patent for an invention is a document that makes a claim public and open.
dissēnsiō
what English words?
dissent, dissension. From dis- (“apart”) + sentiō (“to feel, think”) — people whose thinking has gone in different directions. Which is exactly what December 5th was.
ācer, ācriter
what English words?
acrid, acrimony, acerbic. The root idea is sharp. Cato speaking ācriter is Cato speaking with an edge on him.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō, sīc/ita — when one of these is in the main clause, look ahead for ut plus a subjunctive. And remember which ones move: tantus and tālis agree with their noun; tam, tot, adeō, sīc, and ita never change at all.
