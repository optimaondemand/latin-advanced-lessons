Lesson 9.03
Nē or Ut Nōn?
Grammar in Use
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The twins are identical — until you make them say “not.”
In the positive, purpose and result are the same three letters: ut. In the negative they part company completely. Purpose says nē. Result says ut nōn. This is the single most useful fact in Module 9, because it is the one thing you can check mechanically — no judgment call, no feel for the sentence. Look at the negative word and you know which construction you are in.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state the negative asymmetry: purpose nē, result ut nōn.
2
I will be able to read a negative ut clause and say instantly whether it reports an intention or an outcome.
3
I will be able to handle the “no one / nothing / never” versions: nē quis for purpose, ut nēmō for result.
📚 PART 1 · THE WHOLE RULE IN ONE TABLE
ConstructionPositiveNegativeEnglish for the negative
Purpose
Module 8		ut + subjunctivenē + subjunctive“so that…not” · “in order not to” · “to prevent…”
Result
this module		ut + subjunctiveut nōn + subjunctive“so that…did not” · “with the result that…not”

Read the second column and the third column side by side. In the positive column the two constructions are indistinguishable — that is why Lessons 9.01 and 9.02 gave you signposts and a meaning test. In the negative column they are impossible to confuse, and you do not need any test at all. Just read the word.

SEE NĒ → SOMEBODY WAS TRYING TO PREVENT SOMETHING
Catilīna ex urbe fūgit nē comprehenderētur.
“Catiline fled from the city so that he would not be arrested.”
His plan. Whether it worked is not stated.
SEE UT NŌN → SOMETHING DID NOT HAPPEN
Catilīna tam callidus erat ut nōn statim comprehenderētur.
“Catiline was so clever that he was not arrested at once.”
The outcome. He really was not arrested at once.
💡 Look at the verb in both boxes. It is the same word: comprehenderētur, imperfect subjunctive passive of comprehendō, “he was being arrested / would be arrested.” The verb form carries no information about which construction you are in. Only nē versus ut nōn tells you, and that is exactly why it is worth memorizing. Gloss: comprehendō, comprehendere, comprehendī, comprehēnsum — to seize, arrest; statim (adv.) — at once, immediately.
📚 PART 2 · ONE MORE MINIMAL PAIR

Here is the same trick with a different verb. Everything in the two ut clauses is identical except the negative word — and that one word changes what the sentence is claiming about the world.

Coniūrātī nocte convēnērunt nē vidērentur.
“The conspirators met at night so that they would not be seen.”
Purpose. This is why they chose night-time. The sentence says nothing about whether anybody spotted them.
Nox tam obscūra erat ut nōn vidērentur.
“The night was so dark that they were not seen.”
Result. Signpost tam, negative ut nōn. This sentence does make a claim: nobody saw them. Gloss: obscūrus, -a, -um — dark, dim.
✍️ A production warning. When you write Latin this week, the commonest error is not a wrong ending — it is reaching for nē out of habit because you have just spent a whole module on purpose clauses. Before you write a negative ut clause, ask yourself one question: am I saying what somebody wanted, or what happened? Wanted → nē. Happened → ut nōn.
🎵 MEMORY WORK · SAY IT OUT LOUD THREE TIMES
ut … nē — that is purpose.
ut … ut nōn — that is result.
Nē is the plan. Ut nōn is the outcome.

Say it now, before you look at the drill. Six seconds of chanting will save you several marks on Friday's translation, and it is faster than reasoning the rule out from scratch every time you meet a negative clause.

📚 PART 3 · “SO THAT NO ONE…”

Often the negative idea is not carried by nōn at all, but by a word that is already negative: “no one,” “nothing,” “never.” When that happens, Latin does not add a second negative on top. And the two constructions still use different words:

EnglishPurpose saysResult saysWatch out
…that no onenē quisut nēmōnever ut nōn nēmō
…that nothingnē quidut nihilnever ut nōn nihil
…that nevernē umquamut numquamnever ut nōn umquam
Cicerō custōdēs posuit nē quis effugeret.
“Cicero posted guards so that no one would escape.” — his intention. Gloss: custōs, custōdis m. — guard; quis (after nē) — anyone; effugiō — to escape.
Adeō timēbant ut nēmō loquerētur.
“They were afraid to such a degree that no one spoke.” — the outcome. Signpost adeō; the negative lives inside nēmō, so no nōn is needed.
⚠️ Do not double up. English tolerates “didn't see nobody” in casual speech; classical Latin does not. One negative per clause. If you have written nēmō, nihil, or numquam, the clause is already negative — leave ut on its own.
🧠 PART 4 · THE MECHANICAL CHECK

Five rows. For the first three you do not need to understand the sentence at all — read the negative word and answer. For the last two you have to choose the negative yourself.

✅ CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING
Purpose or result — and which negative?
Cicerō litterās recitāvit nē cīvēs dubitārent. (recitō — to read aloud; dubitō — to doubt)
Nox tam obscūra erat ut nōn vidērentur.
Adeō timēbant ut nēmō loquerētur.
Complete for purpose: Coniūrātī nocte convēnērunt ____ vidērentur. (“…so that they would not be seen”)
Complete for result: Catilīna tam callidus erat ____ statim comprehenderētur. (“…that he was not arrested at once”)

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Purpose negates with nē. Result negates with ut nōn. Everything else about the two constructions can look identical; this cannot. When the clause is negative, stop reasoning and just read the word — and if the negative is already inside nēmō, nihil, or numquam, do not add another one.
