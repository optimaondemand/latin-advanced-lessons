📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
This is the skill the course has been building toward all semester.
Since Module 6 you have been assembling pieces: the subjunctive, sequence, purpose, indirect command, and now result. Today they all arrive in the same pile of sentences, unlabelled, and you sort them. Three questions, asked in the same order every time, will get you through almost any ut clause in Latin prose.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to run the three-question test on any ut or nē clause and name the construction.
2
I will be able to tell a result clause apart from purpose and from indirect command, the third look-alike.
3
I will be able to write result clauses in Latin, positive and negative, with the correct signpost and sequence.
📚 PART 1 · THE THREE-QUESTION TEST

Ask these in order. Most sentences are settled by question 1 or question 3 without your having to think about the meaning at all.

1. Is the clause negative? If yes, you are already finished.
ut nōn (or ut nēmō, ut nihil, ut numquam) → result.  ·  nē → not result — it is purpose or indirect command, and question 2 will tell you which.
2. What is the main verb?
A verb of ordering, asking, urging, or persuading — imperō, rogō, hortor, moneō, persuādeō — means the ut clause is an indirect command: the content of what was ordered. Any other verb: keep going.
3. Is there a signpost in the main clause?
tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō, sīc, ita → result. No signpost, and the clause answers “why did he do it?” → purpose. Still unsure? Try both English versions out loud: “in order to…” against “with the result that…” One of them will be obviously false.
💡 Why question 1 comes first. It is the only one of the three that requires no interpretation. You are not deciding what the author meant — you are reading a word off the page. Habits of that kind are what make you fast, and being fast is what lets you read whole paragraphs instead of decoding single sentences.
📚 PART 2 · THREE JOBS FOR ONE LITTLE WORD

By now ut + subjunctive has three jobs in your Latin. Here they are together for the first time. Notice that two of the three negate with nē — which is why the negative test rules out result rather than pinning down purpose.

🔍 An honest complication, worth knowing about. A few verbs sit on the border. Persuādeō (“persuade,” taking the dative) can introduce an indirect command — Catō senātuī persuāsit ut coniūrātōs pūnīret, “Cato persuaded the Senate to punish the conspirators” — but if the author puts a signpost like ita in front, the same shape tips toward result: “Cato persuaded them in such a way that…” Do not lose sleep over these. Name the ones you can name, translate sensibly, and note the doubt.
🧠 PART 3 · THE PURPOSE / RESULT IDENTIFIER

Six sentences, unlabelled, in no helpful order. Run the three questions on each one. Not all six contain a subordinate clause at all — one of them is there to see whether you are reading or pattern-matching.

✍️ PART 4 · NOW WRITE SOME

Reading them is half the job. Write each of these out in Latin before you flip the card — on paper, not in your head. Vocabulary you need is given in the prompt; the work is the signpost, the negative, and the sequence.

🔍 WRITE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
“Caesar spoke so cleverly that many believed him.” (callidē — cleverly; dīxit; multī; crēdō + dative)
click to compare
Caesar tam callidē dīxit ut multī eī crēderent.
Signpost tam with the adverb callidē. Dīxit is perfect → secondary sequence → imperfect subjunctive crēderent. And crēdō takes the dative, so “him” is eī, not eum. Also correct: adeō callidē.
“The disagreement was so great that the senators shouted.” (dissēnsiō f.; senātōrēs; clāmō)
click to compare
Tanta erat dissēnsiō ut senātōrēs clāmārent.
Tanta, feminine nominative singular, agreeing with dissēnsiō — not tantus, not tam. Erat is imperfect → secondary → clāmārent.
“The crime was so great that no one dared to speak.” (scelus, sceleris n.; nēmō; audeō; dīcere)
click to compare
Tantum erat scelus ut nēmō dīcere audēret.
Tantum — neuter, because scelus is neuter. The negative is carried by nēmō, so there is no nōn. Audēret is the imperfect subjunctive of the semi-deponent audeō (Module 3), built on the infinitive audēre.
“Cicero posted guards so that no one would escape.” (custōdēs; pōnō; effugiō)
click to compare
Cicerō custōdēs posuit nē quis effugeret.
A trap: this one is purpose, not result. There is no signpost, and the clause gives Cicero's reason — so the negative is nē, and “no one” is nē quis. Ut nēmō effugeret would claim that nobody in fact got away, which is a different sentence.
📄 Getting ready for the translation. Those four are the same four skills Friday's graded translation asks for: agreement on the signpost, the right negative, correct sequence, and a case governed by the verb. If any of them felt shaky, redo it on paper now while the answer is fresh.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Three questions, in this order: Is it negative? What is the main verb? Is there a signpost? Two of the three can be answered by reading words off the page rather than interpreting them — which is why an experienced reader identifies an ut clause almost without slowing down.
