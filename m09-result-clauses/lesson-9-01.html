📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two different sentences. Identical grammar.
Catō surrēxit ut dīceret means “Cato stood up in order to speak.” Tam ācriter dīxit ut omnēs audīrent means “He spoke so fiercely that everyone listened.” Same ut. Same imperfect subjunctive. Two completely different claims about the world — one about somebody's intention, the other about the outcome. Today you learn the second one: the result clause.

📚 PART 1 · THE SHAPE IS ALREADY FAMILIAR

Here is the entire construction, in one line:

ut + subjunctive
“that…” · “so that…” · “with the result that…”

That is exactly what a purpose clause looks like. Latin has no separate word for “result” — it re-uses ut and expects the reader to work out which job the clause is doing. That sounds unfair. It is actually a gift, because it means you already know how to build one. Compare:

PURPOSE · MODULE 8
Catō surrēxit ut dīceret.
“Cato stood up in order to speak.”
What he was trying to do. Looks forward, out of his mind.
RESULT · THIS MODULE
Tam ācriter dīxit ut omnēs audīrent.
“He spoke so fiercely that everyone listened.”
What actually followed. Looks back, at the world.
💡 Why the subjunctive at all? In Module 6 you learned that the subjunctive is the mood of what is not simply asserted as bare fact. A result clause is one step away from bare fact as well: it does not merely report an event, it reports an event as the consequence of something else. Latin marks that dependence with the subjunctive — even though in English we translate it with an ordinary indicative: “everyone listened.”
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Latin uses one word, ut, for two very different jobs. Before you read on: what do you expect will help you tell them apart?
📚 PART 2 · WHAT A RESULT CLAUSE CLAIMS
A result clause states the consequence of the main clause — what followed, whether or not anyone meant it to.

English does this with “so … that” and “such … that.” You use it constantly:

  • The fear was so great that the senators fled.
  • Catiline was such a man that even his friends were afraid of him.
  • There were so many conspirators that nobody could count them.

Notice what those three have in common: a small intensifying word (so, such, so many) sits in the main clause and leans forward, promising a consequence. Latin does the same, and its intensifiers are tomorrow's whole lesson. Here are the three you meet most often:

LatinMeansExample
tamso (with an adjective or adverb)Catilīna tam callidus erat ut omnēs eum timērent.
“Catiline was so clever that everyone feared him.”
tantus, -a, -umso great, so much (with a noun)Tantus erat timor ut multī ex urbe fugerent.
“The fear was so great that many fled from the city.”
adeōto such a degree, so much (with a verb)Adeō timēbant ut nēmō loquerētur.
“They were afraid to such a degree that no one spoke.”
✍️ How to put it into English. Any of these will do, and you should choose whichever sounds least awkward: “so … that” (tam ācriter dīxit ut omnēs audīrent → “he spoke so fiercely that everyone listened”), “with the result that” (“he spoke fiercely, with the result that everyone listened”), or a plain “that” after an English intensifier. What you must never write is “in order that” — that is purpose, and it is a different claim about the world.
📚 PART 3 · SEQUENCE: NOTHING NEW

The tense of the subjunctive is chosen exactly as in Module 7. Look at the main verb and use the two rows:

Main verb is…SequenceSubjunctive in the ut clause
present or futureprimarypresent subjunctive
any past tense (imperfect, perfect, pluperfect)secondaryimperfect subjunctive

Watch one sentence move from present into the past. Only the endings change; the construction does not.

Cicerō tam clārē dīcit ut omnēs cīvēs audiant.
“Cicero speaks so clearly that all the citizens hear him.”
Dīcit is present → primary sequence → audiant, present subjunctive of audiō.
Cicerō tam clārē dīxit ut omnēs cīvēs audīrent.
“Cicero spoke so clearly that all the citizens heard him.”
Dīxit is perfect → secondary sequence → audīrent, imperfect subjunctive: present infinitive audīre plus the personal endings.
Tantus erat timor ut multī senātōrēs ex urbe fugerent.
“The fear was so great that many senators fled from the city.”
Erat is imperfect → secondary → fugerent, from the infinitive fugere. Note tantus agreeing with timor — masculine nominative singular.
⚠️ One honest footnote, then put it away. Because a result genuinely happened, Latin authors sometimes bend the sequence rule in a result clause to stress that the outcome was completed — using a subjunctive tense you meet in Module 13. You will not see it in this module, and nothing you are asked to write needs it. Every result clause in Module 9 follows the two rows above.

★ VĒRITĀS · HONESTY

Vēritās, vēritātis f. is truth — and for Romans it named truthfulness as a habit, not accuracy on one occasion. This module's grammar rests on it. A purpose clause reports what someone wanted. A result clause reports what followed, and it does not care whether anybody wanted it. When Sallust writes ita … ut, he is making a claim of fact: this really did come out of that.

The distinction is worth having in your own speech. “I was trying to help” is a purpose clause. “It came out so badly that nobody spoke to me for a week” is a result clause. Both can be true at once, and an honest account gives you both. Blurring them is how people talk their way out of consequences — and it is also, this week, how students lose marks.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A result clause is ut + subjunctive meaning “with the result that” — the same machinery as a purpose clause, doing the opposite job. Purpose looks forward out of somebody's mind; result looks back at what the world actually did.
