📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Four modules ago you learned that nē means "not." Today it stops.
Timeō nē veniat does not mean "I am afraid so that he may not come." It means "I am afraid that he will come." After a verb of fearing, nē stops carrying a negative into your English altogether — it marks the outcome you do not want, and you translate it with a plain "that." This is the most counter-intuitive construction in the semester. We are going to meet it head-on rather than hope you absorb it.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name the three parts of a fear clause and identify one in a Latin sentence.
2
I will be able to translate timeō nē… as "I am afraid that…" and explain why nē behaves this way after a verb of fearing.
3
I will be able to recognize the four ways Latin says "fear" — timeō, metuō, the deponent vereor, and the noun-based timor est.
📚 PART 1 · THE SENTENCE THAT LOOKS WRONG

Here is a three-word Latin sentence. Everything you learned in Module 8 tells you one thing about it. Latin means the other thing.

Timeō nē veniat.
WHAT MODULE 8 TRAINED YOU TO SAY
"I am afraid so that he may not come."
Which is not English, and not what Latin means.
WHAT IT ACTUALLY MEANS
"I am afraid that he will come."
The nē disappears from the English entirely.

You have spent Modules 8, 9, 10 and 11 building an instinct: see nē, write "not." That instinct was correct for purpose clauses and it is about to cost you marks. Today's job is to install a second setting next to it, and a switch that tells you which one to use.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT A FEAR CLAUSE IS
A fear clause names the thing somebody is afraid of. It has three parts: a verb of fearing, then nē, then a verb in the subjunctive.

Nothing about the shape is new to you. You already build present and imperfect subjunctives (Module 6) and you already let the main verb pick the tense (Module 7). The only new thing in this module is what the marker word means when you turn it into English.

LatinEnglish
Cicerō timet nē Catilīna reveniat.Cicero is afraid that Catiline will come back.
Senātōrēs timēbant nē urbs dēlērētur.The senators were afraid that the city would be destroyed.
Coniūrātī metuēbant nē cōnsul omnia cognōsceret.The conspirators dreaded that the consul would learn everything.
The rule, as short as it goes: after a verb of fearing, nē marks what you are afraid will happen. Do not put the word "not" anywhere in your English.

Notice what the English does with the tense, too. Latin has a present subjunctive (reveniat) or an imperfect subjunctive (dēlērētur), but English reaches for "will" and "would". That is because a fear looks forward — you are afraid of something not settled yet. Keep "will / would" in your pocket for this whole module; they are almost always the natural English.

🧠 PART 3 · WHY LATIN DOES THIS

A rule you understand is a rule you keep. Here is where the construction came from.

Long before Cicero, this was not one sentence. It was two:

SENTENCE ONE — THE FEELING
Timeō.
"I am afraid."
SENTENCE TWO — THE WISH
Nē veniat!
"May he not come!"

Read those two together and you can hear the modern sentence forming: I'm afraid. Don't let him come! Over time the wish stopped standing on its own and got swallowed into the sentence in front of it — but nē kept doing the job it had always done: pushing away an outcome the speaker does not want.

So the negative in nē was never negating the fear. It was negating a wish that has since vanished from the sentence. When you translate, that vanished wish is why "not" has nothing left to attach to — and why nē comes out in English as a plain, flat "that."

💡 Your mental handle for the whole module. Say it once and keep it: after a verb of fearing, nē marks what you are afraid will happen. The unwanted thing. The outcome you would wish away if wishing worked.
✍️ English used to do this too. "I fear lest he come." That is exactly the old nē feeling — and notice that even in that archaic English, lest does not add a "not" to the fear. Nobody talks that way now, which is precisely why the Latin looks so strange to you.
📚 PART 4 · THE FOUR WAYS LATIN SAYS "FEAR"

All four take nē and a subjunctive. They differ in flavour, not in grammar.

EntryKindWhat it feels like
timeō, timēre, timuī2nd conj., ordinaryThe everyday word. Plain fear, from a real danger. Sallust's soldiers timent.
metuō, metuere, metuī3rd conj., ordinaryHeavier and colder — dread. The fear that sits in the stomach and waits.
vereor, verērī, veritus sum2nd conj., deponentFear with respect mixed into it. You vereor a god, a father, the Senate — or a genuinely formidable enemy.
timor est… / magnus metus erat…a noun, not a verb"There is a fear that…" Latin can hang a fear clause off the noun: Timor erat nē urbs caperētur — "There was a fear that the city would be captured."

★ VIRTŪS · COURAGE

Virtūs comes from vir, "man," and for Romans it meant courage first and excellence generally. You met it in Module 2 as the nerve to hold two readings of a form at once, and in Module 8 as deliberate intention. Here it is something plainer: saying out loud what you are afraid of.

That is literally what this construction does. A fear clause takes a vague dread and forces it into a sentence with a subject and a verb — timeō nē exercitus diffugiat, "I am afraid the army will scatter." Named, it can be answered. Unnamed, it just sits there and grows. Romans understood metus as one of the great engines of politics; Sallust says the conspiracy ran on it. By the end of this week you will read about a man who was, on Sallust's own telling, afraid of the wrong thing — and who went into the line anyway.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

After a verb of fearing, nē marks what you are afraid will happen, and it comes into English as a plain "that" — no "not" anywhere. Timeō nē veniat = "I'm afraid he will come." The little word did not change meaning on its own; the verb in front of it changed its job.
