📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday: "I'm afraid he will come." Today: "I'm afraid he won't."
Both are real fears and Latin can say both. But the marker words swap: nē gave you the first, and the second needs either nē nōn or, more rarely, ut on its own. Ut is the one every textbook shows and almost nobody writes. Today you learn which is which, and why the pattern is not arbitrary.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to say "I am afraid that X will not happen" in Latin, using nē nōn or ut.
2
I will be able to explain why ut after a verb of fearing comes into English as "that…not."
3
I will be able to pick the right marker word from an English fear, and say why nē nōn is legal here even though Module 8 banned it.
📚 PART 1 · THE FEAR THAT SOMETHING WON'T HAPPEN

Fears come in two flavours, and you feel the difference instantly in English:

I DON'T WANT IT TO HAPPEN
Timeō nē veniat.
"I am afraid that he will come."
I WANT IT TO HAPPEN
Timeō ut veniat.
"I am afraid that he will not come."

Identical verbs. Identical subjunctive. The only difference is the marker word — and the English polarity flips completely. That looks like a cruel joke until you go back to yesterday's explanation, which handles both cases at once.

Remember the wish hidden inside the construction. With nē, the wish was negative — nē veniat!, "may he not come!" So what is feared is the coming. Now make the wish positive:

THE FEELING
Timeō.
"I am afraid."
THE WISH — NOW POSITIVE
Ut veniat!
"If only he would come!"
💡 So the logic is consistent after all. The marker word tells you what the speaker wants, and the fear is always that the wish fails. Wish it away with nē → you fear it will happen. Wish for it with ut → you fear it will not. The "not" in your English never comes from the Latin word; it comes from the wish being disappointed.
✍️ PART 2 · THE ONE YOU WILL ACTUALLY WRITE: NĒ NŌN

Timeō ut veniat is correct Latin. It is also rare. Open Cicero or Sallust and the ordinary way to say "I'm afraid he won't come" is not ut at all — it is nē plus an honest nōn:

Timeō nē nōn veniat.
"I am afraid that he will not come."

Read it the way the construction is built and it makes plain sense. Nē marks the unwanted outcome. The unwanted outcome here is his not coming. So you write the negative into the clause where it belongs: nē + nōn veniat. Nothing clever is happening.

LatinEnglishHow common
Timeō nē veniat.I'm afraid he will come.The standard positive-polarity fear. Everywhere.
Timeō nē nōn veniat.I'm afraid he will not come.The normal way to say this. Write this one.
Timeō ut veniat.I'm afraid he will not come.Correct, and genuinely used — but uncommon. You must be able to read it.
⚠️ Module 8 told you never to write nē nōn. That was true — about purpose clauses. In a purpose clause, nē already carries the "not" into your English, so adding nōn double-negates and the sentence collapses. In a fear clause, nē carries no "not" into your English at all — you translate it "that." So the nōn has real work to do, and Latin lets it do it. Same two words, opposite verdict, because the governing verb is different. Two constructions, two rules; you now hold both.
Constructionnē nōn?Why
Purpose (Module 8)✗ neverNē = "so that…not." The negative is already spent.
Fear (this module)✓ yes, normallyNē = "that." The negative is still available, and nōn uses it.
🔏 One shape that is never right after a verb of fearing: ut nōn. You met ut nōn in Module 9 as the negative of a result clause, and that is the only place it lives. After timeō, ut by itself already delivers the "not," so timeō ut nōn veniat would negate twice and mean nothing useful. Do not write it, and do not choose it on Friday's quiz.
🎵 MEMORY WORK · SAY IT OUT LOUD THREE TIMES
After fearing: nē is "that."
After fearing: ut is "that not."
And if I want the "not" said plainly — nē nōn.

Chant it before you touch the drill. Nine seconds of this is worth more than reasoning the rule out from first principles in the middle of a translation, and it is the single highest-value thing you will memorize this quarter.

🔄 PART 3 · THE NĒ / UT FLIP

Six rows. Each one gives you an English fear; you pick the Latin that actually says it. Read the English twice before you choose — the whole question is which way the fear points.

🔄 THE FLIP · ENGLISH FEAR → LATIN MARKER
Which Latin sentence says the English?
"I am afraid that Catiline will come back." (reveniō, revenīre — to come back)
"I am afraid that help will not come." (auxilium, auxiliī n. — help; veniō, venīre)
"The soldiers were afraid that the army would not be there." — written the rarer way, with ut. (adsum, adesse — to be present; imperfect subj. adesset)
Which sequence of words is not used after a verb of fearing?
"We are afraid that the city will be captured." (capiō, capere — present subj. passive capiātur; imperfect subj. passive caperētur)
"I am afraid that we do not have enough grain." (frūmentum, frūmentī n. — grain; satis frūmentī — enough grain)
✍️ PART 4 · TRANSLATE, THEN CHECK

Write your English out before you flip. Two of these four have a negative in the English and two do not — deciding which is the whole exercise.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Vereor nē hostēs iam in agrīs sint.
click to compare
"I am afraid that the enemy are already in the fields." Vereor is the deponent — "I fear," never "I am feared." Nē → plain "that." Sint is present subjunctive of esse after a present main verb.
Cicerō timēbat nē cōnsilia coniūrātōrum nōn cognōscerentur.
click to compare
"Cicero was afraid that the plans of the conspirators would not be discovered." Here nē nōn gives the negative fear, and cognōscerentur is imperfect subjunctive passive — the plans are not doing the discovering.
Metuēbant nē nōn satis cibī esset.
click to compare
"They dreaded that there would not be enough food." Metuēbant is imperfect → secondary sequence → esset. Satis cibī — "enough of food," partitive genitive. Gloss: cibus, cibī m. — food.
Timeō ut cōnsul in tempore veniat.
click to compare
"I am afraid that the consul will not come in time." This is the rare ut pattern — the speaker wants the consul to come, and fears he will not. If you wrote "that the consul will come," you read ut as nē. Gloss: in tempore — in time, on time.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

The marker word tells you what the speaker wanted, and the fear is always that the wish fails. Nē → "that…will." Ut → "that…will not." And when you want the "not" said out loud, write nē nōn — which Module 8 forbade in a purpose clause and this module welcomes, because the governing verb changed the rules.
