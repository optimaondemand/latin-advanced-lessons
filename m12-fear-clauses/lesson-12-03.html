📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The strangest construction of the semester runs on a rule you have had since Module 7. No new machinery today.
Lessons 12.01 and 12.02 settled which marker word to use — nē, nē nōn, or the rare ut. They left one question open: which tense of the subjunctive goes inside the clause? The answer is the two-row sequence table you already own, applied without a single change. Once that is nailed down we do the full paradigm of the deponent vereor, this module's word list, and the metus trap — the one that catches everybody exactly once.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to choose the present or the imperfect subjunctive for a fear clause by reading the main verb first.
2
I will be able to write out the present and imperfect subjunctive of the deponent vereor from memory.
3
I will be able to spot a fear clause hanging off a noun — metus est nē…, timor erat nē… — where English uses a verb.
📚 PART 1 · THE TWO-ROW RULE, UNCHANGED

Here is the table from Module 7. Read it again knowing that it governs fear clauses too, exactly as it governs purpose, result, cum and indirect questions. Look at the main verb. It picks the tense. That is the whole rule.

✍️ Two of the four boxes are still shut. That grey right-hand column has been grey since Module 7, and it stays grey for one more week. Every subjunctive you write in this module is present or imperfect — no exceptions, in the lessons, the drills, the quiz or the translation. Module 13 opens the column, and it opens it on purpose right after this one, because a fear is the natural place to want to say “I am afraid something has already happened.” You cannot say that yet. You can say everything else.
💡 Why fear clauses barely miss the shut boxes. A fear points forward. You are afraid of something that has not settled yet, which is why your English keeps reaching for “will” and “would.” Forward-looking fear lives in the left-hand column: present subjunctive after a present main verb, imperfect after a past one. The module works cleanly without Module 13 because that is where fear naturally sits.
📚 PART 2 · FOUR MAIN VERBS, FOUR DECISIONS

Same construction each time. Only the main verb moves, and the subjunctive follows it. Cover the Latin, read the English, and say which tense you would write before you look.

Vereor nē hiems viās claudat.
“I am afraid that winter will close the roads.”
Main verb vereor, present → primary → present subjunctive claudat. Gloss: hiems, hiemis f. — winter; claudō, claudere, clausī — to close, block.
Verēbātur nē mīlitēs spem salūtis āmitterent.
“He was afraid that the soldiers would lose hope of safety.”
Main verb verēbātur, imperfect → secondary → imperfect subjunctive āmitterent. Gloss: spēs, speī f. — hope; salūs, salūtis f. — safety; āmittō, āmittere, āmīsī — to lose.
Metuent nē dux ipse in aciē cadat.
“They will dread that the leader himself will fall in the battle line.”
Main verb metuent, future → and future is primary → present subjunctive cadat. This is the row students get wrong, and they got it wrong in Module 11 too: a future main verb does not want a future-looking subjunctive. There is no such thing. It sits in the top row with the present. Gloss: dux, ducis m. — leader; aciēs, aciēī f. — battle line; cadō, cadere, cecidī — to fall.
Timor erat nē nūntius ad castra nōn pervenīret.
“There was a fear that the messenger would not reach the camp.”
Main verb erat, imperfect → secondary → imperfect subjunctive pervenīret. Two other things are happening here and both come back later on this page: the fear is carried by a noun, timor, not a verb; and the nōn of nē nōn does not have to sit glued to nē. It can stand next to the verb it negates. Gloss: nūntius, nūntiī m. — messenger; perveniō, pervenīre, pervēnī — to arrive, reach.
💡 The English will not tell you. Notice that three of the four sentences came out in English with “will” or “would,” which are not tenses Latin has here at all. If you choose your subjunctive by translating first and then guessing, you will guess. Read the Latin main verb, name its tense, name the sequence, write the form. Four seconds, no guessing.
📚 PART 3 · VEREOR IN FULL

Lesson 12.01 introduced vereor, verērī, veritus sum and promised the paradigm. Here it is. Nothing in it is new: it is a 2nd-conjugation deponent, so it takes passive endings and means something active, exactly as hortor and sequor have since Module 2. Three principal parts, because the perfect is built from a participle.

Both subjunctives are built the way every 2nd-conjugation passive is built, and you can check them without memorising anything. The present subjunctive swaps the stem vowel for -eā- (moneātur → vereātur). The imperfect subjunctive takes the whole present active infinitive and adds passive endings — and since a deponent has no active infinitive on show, use the shape: verē- + -rer, -rēris, -rētur (monērētur → verērētur).

THE OTHER PARTS
Present infinitiveverērī — to fear
Perfect indicativeveritus sum, veritus es, veritus est… — I feared, you feared, he feared
Participlesverēns, verentis — fearing · veritus, -a, -um — having feared
Imperativeverēre (one person) · verēminī (more than one) — “be afraid!”
⚠️ The one error to refuse. Veritus sum means “I feared,” never “I was feared.” Verēbātur means “he was afraid,” never “he was being feared.” That is Module 2's rule and it has not moved. A deponent looks passive and is not; the only verb whose voice you have to think about in a fear clause is the one inside the clause.

⚠️ PART 5 · THE METUS TRAP

Every fear clause you have met so far hung off a verb: timeō, metuēbant, vereor. Latin does not require one. It will happily hang the clause off a noun:

Metus erat nē cōpiae in montibus dēficerent.
“There was a fear that the forces would give out in the mountains.”
Gloss: cōpiae, cōpiārum f. pl. — forces, troops; dēficiō, dēficere, dēfēcī — to fail, give out.

Here is why it catches people. The student runs the habit from 12.01 — look at the verb in front of nē — and the verb in front of nē is erat. “Was” is not a verb of fearing. So the sentence looks like it has no fear in it, the student falls back on the Module 8 reflex, and out comes “there was a fear so that the forces would not give out” — which is nonsense, and which they can usually hear is nonsense without being able to say why.

✍️ The repair is one word wider. Do not look for a verb of fearing. Look for a fear word — verb or noun. If timeō, metuō, vereor, timor or metus is anywhere in the main clause, you are in a fear clause and nē means “that.”
LatinEnglish
Metus est nē hostēs veniant.There is a fear that the enemy will come.
Magnus timor erat nē urbs incenderētur.There was great fear that the city would be set on fire.
Metus nōn erat nē nōn satis frūmentī habērent.There was no fear that they would not have enough grain.

Two smaller things about metus while you have it in front of you. It is 4th declension, so the ablative is metū — magnō metū, “in great dread” — and never metō. And the verb metuō has a spelling collision worth knowing: metuit is both present (“he dreads”) and perfect (“he dreaded”). Only the sentence around it tells you which, and in a fear clause the tense of the subjunctive will usually settle it for you: metuit nē veniat is present, metuit nē venīret is perfect.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A fear clause adds no new machinery. Marker word from 12.01 and 12.02; tense from the Module 7 table; forms from the conjugations you have had since 0706010. The only genuinely new things this module asks you to hold are a translation habit — nē is “that” — and a searching habit: look for a fear word in the main clause, verb or noun.
