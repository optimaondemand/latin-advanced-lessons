📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Module 10 left Catiline alive in Etruria with an army. This is how that ends.
Two months after the executions in Rome, in January of 62 BC, Catiline was cornered in the hills near Pistoria with an army he could no longer move and no road out. What Sallust wrote about that valley is the coldest and most careful passage in his book — and it is built out of fear. You will read it adapted: eleven lines carrying all three fear patterns you have learned, plus a purpose clause, an indirect command and a cum clause, so yesterday's five steps get their first real test. Then the module review, and your graded work.

📚 PART 1 · BEFORE YOU READ

Four things the passage assumes and does not explain.

1. Catiline had already left Rome. He walked out of the city in November of 63 BC, after Cicero's first speech against him, and went north to the forces gathering in Etruria. The Senate then declared him a public enemy — a hostis. From that point there was no legal way back for him; the only questions left were where he would be caught and whether he would fight.
2. By January he was boxed in. Roman forces were closing from more than one direction and the route north into Gaul was blocked. Sallust says Catiline saw himself shut in by the mountains and by the enemy's troops. So he turned and drew up his line in the territory of Pistoria — the ager Pistōriēnsis, in the Apennines north-west of Florence, near the modern town of Pistoia.
3. The Roman commander was not the man in charge on paper. Gaius Antonius had been Cicero's fellow consul in 63 and held the command against Catiline the following year. He was not present at the battle: Sallust says he was ill — pedibus aeger, ill in the feet, which is usually taken to mean gout. His deputy, Marcus Petreius, fought the battle in his place.
4. Neither side expected prisoners. This was a Roman army against Romans who had been declared enemies of the state. Nobody in that valley had a surrender to offer or to accept, and both commanders knew it. That is the fact which makes the fears in this passage the kind of fears they are.
💡 Watch the grammar doing the work. There are three fear clauses in the passage and they do not all point the same way: one plain nē, one nē nōn, and one of the rare uts — in adjacent lines, which puts the whole module in one place. Then a purpose clause and an indirect command arrive using the same two marker words. Run the five steps. They will hold.
📜 PART 2 · THE READING
Adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 57–61. This is an adaptation, not Sallust's own Latin: the sentences are shortened and rebuilt using only grammar you have been taught. From Sallust come the events and the names, the removal of the horses, Petreius calling on his men one by one, Catiline fighting on foot in the front line, the dead lying on the ground each had held, and the finding of Catiline himself. What is not from Sallust is listed in the red note below the gloss.

1Catilīna, cum montibus et cōpiīs hostium claudērētur, spem fugae iam nōn habēbat.

2Itaque in agrō Pistōriēnsī, mēnse Iānuāriō, aciem in valle angustā īnstrūxit.

3Verēbātur enim nē mīlitēs suī sine proeliō dīlāberentur.

4Multī in castrīs metuēbant nē nōn satis armōrum esset; aliī timēbant ut dux viam salūtis invenīret.

5Catilīna equōs omnium remōvit, ut, perīculō aequātō, mīlitum animus maior esset.

6Antōnius ipse aberat, quod pedibus aeger erat; itaque Petrēius, lēgātus eius, exercituī praeerat.

7Petrēius singulōs mīlitēs nōmine appellābat et rogābat ut fortiter prō patriā pugnārent.

8Catilīna, quod equum nōn habēbat, pedibus in prīmā aciē pugnābat, et mīlitis strēnuī et bonī imperātōris officia simul praestābat.

9Nam ferē quem quisque locum vīvus pugnandō cēperat, eum, animā āmissā, corpore tegēbat.

10Catilīna ipse longē ā suīs inter hostium cadāvera repertus est, paulum etiam spīrāns.

11Et in vultū ferōciam animī retinēbat quam vīvus habuerat.

  • 1 · claudērētur — was being shut in (imperfect subjunctive passive of claudō, claudere, clausī, clausum) · cōpiīs — by the forces (ablative plural of cōpiae, cōpiārum f. pl. — troops, forces) · spēs, speī f. — hope · fugae — of escape (genitive of fuga, fugae f.)
  • 2 · ager Pistōriēnsis — the territory of Pistoria, in the Apennines north-west of Florence · mēnse Iānuāriō — in the month of January (ablative of time when) · aciēs, aciēī f. — battle line · vallis, vallis f. — valley · angustus, -a, -um — narrow · īnstrūxit — drew up (īnstruō, īnstruere, īnstrūxī)
  • 3 · verēbātur — he was afraid (imperfect indicative of the deponent vereor, verērī, veritus sum) · enim — for, because (never first in its clause) · proelium, proeliī n. — battle · dīlāberentur — imperfect subjunctive of the deponent dīlābor, dīlābī, dīlāpsus sum: to slip away, melt away
  • 4 · satis armōrum — enough weapons (partitive genitive of arma, armōrum n. pl.) · aliī — others · dux, ducis m. — leader · via salūtis — a way to safety (salūs, salūtis f.) · invenīret — imperfect subjunctive of inveniō, invenīre, invēnī: to find
  • 5 · equōs omnium — everyone's horses · remōvit — sent away, had removed (removeō, removēre, remōvī) · perīculō aequātō — the danger having been made equal (ablative absolute; aequō, aequāre, aequāvī, aequātum — to make equal) · maior — greater (comparative of magnus)
  • 6 · Antōnius — Gaius Antonius, Cicero's colleague as consul in 63 BC, who held the command against Catiline the next year · aberat — was absent (absum, abesse, āfuī) · pedibus aeger — ill in the feet (Sallust's own phrase; usually taken as gout) · lēgātus, lēgātī m. — a commander's deputy · praeerat (+ dative) — was in command of (praesum, praeesse, praefuī)
  • 7 · singulōs — one by one, each man in turn · nōmine — by name (ablative of nōmen, nōminis n.) · appellābat — was calling on, addressing (appellō, appellāre) · fortiter (adv.) — bravely
  • 8 · pedibus — on foot · prīma aciēs — the front line · strēnuus, -a, -um — energetic, hard-driving · imperātor, imperātōris m. — commander · officium, officiī n. — duty, the job proper to someone · simul (adv.) — at the same time · praestābat — was performing, was carrying out (praestō, praestāre, praestitī) · et…et — both…and
  • 9 · ferē (adv.) — nearly, more or less · quisque — each man · vīvus, -a, -um — alive · pugnandō — by fighting (ablative of the gerund, Module 4) · animā āmissā — his life having been lost (ablative absolute; anima, animae f. — breath, life; āmittō, āmittere, āmīsī, āmissum — to lose) · tegēbat — was covering (tegō, tegere, tēxī, tectum) · word order: the relative clause comes first and eum picks it up afterwards — “the place which each man had taken…, that he was covering.” This is close to Sallust's own arrangement; do not straighten it out until you have understood it.
  • 10 · longē ā suīs — far from his own men · cadāver, cadāveris n. — corpse · repertus est — was found (reperiō, reperīre, repperī, repertum) · paulum (adv.) — a little · spīrāns — breathing (present participle of spīrō, spīrāre)
  • 11 · vultus, vultūs m. — face, expression · ferōcia, ferōciae f. — fierceness, defiance · retinēbat — was keeping, holding on to (retineō, retinēre, retinuī) · habuerat — pluperfect indicative: he had had
⚠️ What is adapted, said plainly. Sallust's own sentences here use tenses of the subjunctive you will not meet until Module 13, so no line above is his Latin. Three things in particular are the adapter's and not his: the month in line 2 comes from the ancient chronology of the war rather than from Sallust's text; the three fears in lines 3 and 4 are written to put this week's grammar into the narrative — Sallust does report that most of Catiline's men lacked proper military weapons and that holding an army together without a battle was Catiline's problem, but these particular sentences of fear are not in him; and line 11 is split off from line 10 for readability. Everything else follows what he wrote. In Module 13 you will be able to read his sentences as he built them, and the last one is worth waiting for.

🏛️ THE ROMAN WORLD · DYING IN PLACE, AND TELLING THE TRUTH ABOUT IT

A Roman battle line was a piece of ground held by men standing in ranks, and the discipline it asked for was mostly the discipline of not moving. That is why Catiline had the horses taken away, his own included: a man on foot cannot leave, and a commander who cannot leave has made a promise to the men beside him. It is also why Sallust's flattest sentence about the aftermath lands hardest. When it was over, he says, you could see where each man had stood, because each lay dead on more or less the ground he had taken while he was alive. No speech says what that says. Sallust also reports that Catiline took his stand beside a legionary eagle which was said to have been carried in the army of Marius forty years before — a claim he passes on rather than vouches for, and a reminder that the men in that valley thought of themselves as Romans doing a Roman thing.

Then Sallust does something a lesser writer would not. He has spent a whole book showing Catiline as a wrecker, and at the end he neither praises him nor makes excuses for him. He records that the man was found far in front of his own lines, among the enemy dead, still breathing a little, with the fierceness he had had while alive still on his face — and he does not tell you what to feel about it. This is where the vēritās thread from Module 9 reaches its hardest case: telling the truth about someone you find contemptible means reporting the parts that do him credit as well. Sallust closes the work not with a verdict but with the mood of the winning army, men finding relatives and friends among the enemy corpses, joy and grief moving through the camp together. Courage without any of the other virtues is still courage. It is also still a catastrophe. Both are true at once, and he refuses to simplify either.

The grey column from Module 7 finally opens. 💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Line 5 of the reading and the last sentence of the self-check describe the same act — Catiline sending the horses away — and both are purpose clauses with the same two words available. Line 3 sits three lines above them with an identical nē and means something opposite, because the verb in front of it is a verb of fearing. That is the whole module: the marker word is evidence about nothing until you have read the main clause.
