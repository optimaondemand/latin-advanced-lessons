📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two tiny words, six jobs between them — and the evidence that decides is never in the words themselves.
Nē now does three different jobs: negative purpose, negative indirect command, and this module's fear clause. Ut does three of its own: purpose, result, and the rare fear-clause ut that comes out in English as “that…not.” Six constructions, two marker words, and identical shapes on the page. Today you get the two tables side by side, a five-step procedure that reads the main clause first, an unlabelled pile mixing Modules 8 through 12, and an honest account of the two places where the categories overlap.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name nē's three jobs and ut's three jobs and give a Latin example of each.
2
I will be able to run the five-step procedure in order on a sentence nobody has labelled for me.
3
I will be able to explain why the main clause, not the marker word, decides which construction I am reading.
📚 PART 1 · NĒ'S THREE JOBS

All three of these are nē + subjunctive. On the page they are indistinguishable. What separates them sits in the main clause, every time.

JobWhat the main clause hasEnglish for nēExample
Negative purpose
Module 8		an ordinary verb of doing — and no signpost“so that…not”
“in order not to”		Cōnsul portās clausit nē coniūrātī effugerent.
“The consul shut the gates so that the conspirators would not escape.”
Negative indirect command
Module 8		a verb of ordering, asking, urging, warning, persuading — imperō, rogō, moneō, hortor, persuādeō, cēnseō“…not to…”Petrēius mīlitēs monuit nē ōrdinēs relinquerent.
“Petreius warned the soldiers not to leave the ranks.”
Fear clause
Module 12 — this week		a fear word: the verbs timeō, metuō, vereor or the nouns timor, metusa flat “that”
no “not” anywhere		Catilīna verēbātur nē mīlitēs dīlāberentur.
“Catiline was afraid that his soldiers would slip away.”
⚠️ Look at the middle column and then at the right-hand one. In two of the three jobs, nē puts a “not” into your English. In the third it puts nothing. So the honest description of nē is not “nē means not.” It is: nē pushes an outcome away, and whether your English needs the word “not” depends on what the main clause is doing. A purpose clause says somebody acted to keep the outcome off; a fear clause says somebody is afraid the outcome is coming anyway.
✍️ And the negatives divide the same way. A purpose clause is already negative when it uses nē, so nē nōn is forbidden there. A fear clause has spent nothing, so nē nōn is the ordinary way to write the negative fear. Same two words, opposite verdicts — and Lesson 12.02 explained why.
📚 PART 2 · UT'S THREE JOBS
JobWhat the main clause hasEnglish for utExample
Purpose
Module 8		an ordinary verb; no signpost“so that”
“in order to”		Catilīna aciem īnstrūxit ut in valle pugnāret.
“Catiline drew up his line in order to fight in the valley.”
Result
Module 9		a signpost: tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō, ita, sīc“that”
negative: ut nōn		Tanta erat vallis angustia ut nēmō ōrdinēs mūtāre posset.
“So great was the narrowness of the valley that nobody could shift the ranks.”
Fear clause
Module 12 — rare		a fear word“that…not”Mīlitēs timēbant ut dux viam salūtis invenīret.
“The soldiers were afraid that the leader would not find a way to safety.”
✍️ “What about rogāvit ut…?” Good question, and the answer is that the positive indirect command — Petrēius mīlitēs rogāvit ut fortiter pugnārent, “Petreius asked the soldiers to fight bravely” — is the purpose ut wearing a different hat. Same word, same subjunctive, and the English “to” covers both. We file it with purpose and count three jobs rather than four, because for the purpose of reading Latin the pair behave identically. The one place the difference matters is when you are choosing a negative, and both take nē. So nothing is lost.

Now put the negatives of all six in one place. This small table is the single most useful thing on the page, and it is worth copying out by hand.

🧠 PART 3 · THE FIVE-STEP PROCEDURE

Run these in order, and notice what they have in common: not one of them asks what the sentence means. Every step is a look-up — read a word, check a short list. Meaning enters only after the construction is already named, which is the reverse of what most students do and the reason most students guess.

1
Read the first word of the subordinate clause.
Cum → Module 10, and you already have a four-step procedure for that one. A question word (quis, quid, cūr, ubi, quandō, quot, quantus, num, utrum) → Module 11, indirect question. Quī, quae, quod with an antecedent in front of it → relative clause, from 0706010.
Ut or nē → you are in the m08/m09/m12 family. Go to step 2.
2
Is there a fear word in the main clause? The list is five items long: timeō, metuō, vereor, timor, metus.
Yes → FEAR CLAUSE. Nē = “that.” Nē nōn or ut = “that…not.” Stop.
No → step 3.
3
Is the main verb a verb of ordering, asking, urging, warning or persuading? Imperō, rogō, moneō, hortor, persuādeō, cēnseō, ōrō.
Yes → INDIRECT COMMAND. Ut = “to”; nē = “not to.” Stop.
No → step 4.
4
Is there a signpost word in the main clause? Tam, tantus, tālis, tot, adeō, ita, sīc.
Yes → RESULT. Ut = “that”; ut nōn = “that…not.” Stop.
No → step 5.
5
Nothing fired.
→ PURPOSE. Ut = “so that / in order to”; nē = “so that…not.” Purpose is the default because it is the plainest of the six: somebody did something in order that something else should happen.
✍️ Why fear goes at step 2 and not step 5. Because it is the construction that reverses your instinct, and instincts fire fast. If you leave the fear check until late, you will already have translated nē as “not” in your head and you will have to unpick it. Ask the cheap question early — five words to check, and the answer is either yes or no.
🧠 PART 4 · THE UNLABELLED PILE

Six sentences drawn from Modules 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12, in no helpful order and with nothing marked. Run the five steps on each one before you look at the options. All the vocabulary you need is glossed beside the sentence.

✅ CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING
Which construction is this — and what does the marker word mean here?
Verēbantur nē hostēs subitō impetum facerent. (subitō — suddenly; impetum facere — to make an attack)
Cōnsul portās clausit nē coniūrātī effugerent. (porta, portae f. — gate; claudō, claudere, clausī — to shut)

Petrēius mīlitēs monuit nē ōrdinēs relinquerent. (moneō, monēre, monuī — to warn; relinquō, relinquere, relīquī — to leave, abandon)

📜 PART 5 · SPEED SORT · CLICK EACH CARD

No full sentences here — just the fragment that decides. Name the construction and the English before you flip. The goal is to get each one down to about a second.

🃏 FLIP CARDS · NAME THE JOB BEFORE YOU FLIP
timeō nē…
which job?
Fear clause. “I am afraid that…will…” No “not” in the English.
fūgit nē…
which job?
Negative purpose. “He fled so that…not…” Fugiō is a verb of fleeing, not of fearing — and that one letter of difference is the whole module.
imperāvit nē…
which job?
Negative indirect command. “He ordered them not to…” Step 3.
timeō ut…
which job?
Fear clause, the rare pattern. “I am afraid that…not…” The speaker wants the thing and fears it will fail.
tam… ut…
which job?
Result. “So … that…” The signpost in the main clause is doing the announcing. Step 4.
metus erat nē…
careful
Fear clause off a noun. “There was a fear that…” The verb in front of nē is only erat; the fear word is metus. This is the trap from 12.03.
tantus… ut nōn…
which job?
Negative result. “So great … that…not…” Ut nōn lives here and nowhere else.
timeō ut nōn…
think
Does not exist. After a verb of fearing, ut already delivers the “not,” so ut nōn would negate twice. Do not write it and do not choose it. The negative fear is nē nōn.
📚 PART 6 · TWO PLACES WHERE THE BOXES OVERLAP

The five steps are mechanical, and mostly that is the truth about Latin. But two of the boundaries are drawn by grammarians rather than by the language, and you should know which two, because being told a boundary is sharp when it is not is how students learn to distrust the whole system.

1. Purpose and indirect command are the same shape.
Petrēius mīlitēs rogāvit ut fortiter pugnārent. Call it an indirect command and you get “he asked the soldiers to fight bravely.” Call it purpose and you get “he asked the soldiers so that they would fight bravely.” The first is better English; both are defensible readings of the Latin, and Roman writers did not experience them as two things. Step 3 exists to get you to the better English, not to police a real border.
2. Some verbs have fear inside them.
Caveō means “I am on my guard” — a word with both a warning and a fear in it. Cavē nē cadās comes out as “take care you don't fall,” which is a command; but you can hear the fear underneath, and grammars file caveō nē in more than one place. The same goes for videō nē, “see that…not.” The practical point is that with these verbs both readings produce nearly the same English, so you have not made an error by choosing either. Save your worry for the cases where the two readings say different things.
⚠️ What this does not license. Two blurred boundaries out of six is not a general permission to guess. Steps 1, 2 and 4 are hard evidence: an opening cum is an opening cum, timeō is a verb of fearing, and tanta is a signpost. Reading Verēbantur nē hostēs venīrent as a purpose clause is not a judgment call — it is simply wrong, and it makes the sentence say the opposite of what it says. The overlaps live in two named places. Everywhere else, the procedure decides.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

The marker word is the last thing you should look at. Nē has three jobs and ut has three, and in every single case the deciding evidence is somewhere else in the sentence: a fear word, a verb of ordering, a signpost, or none of the three. Read the main clause. Then read the marker.
