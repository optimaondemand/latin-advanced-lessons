📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You can already read Latin. So what is this year for?
Two years ago Latin was puella and servus. Now you handle five declensions, four conjugations, participles, ablative absolutes, and indirect statement. This year adds the one thing Latin has been holding back — the subjunctive mood — and puts you inside the most famous political crisis of the Roman Republic, told by two men who were there.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name the three big grammar additions this course makes to what I already know.
2
I will be able to describe the historical crisis of 63 BC and the two Roman authors who wrote about it.
3
I will be able to explain how a week in this course works and what my work will be graded on.

📚 PART 2 · THE THREE THINGS THIS YEAR ADDS

Semester 1 is built around three additions. Click each card to see what it does.

🔍 CLICK EACH CARD TO REVEAL
Deponent verbs
Modules 2–3 · click
Verbs with passive forms but active meaning. Loquitur looks like "he is spoken" and means "he speaks." Sallust's narration is full of them.
Gerunds & gerundives
Modules 4–5 · click
How Latin turns an action into a noun ("the act of speaking") and into an obligation ("this must be done"). Cicero argues with these constantly.
The subjunctive mood
Modules 6–16 · click
The mood of purpose, result, doubt, fear, wish, and what-if. Two-thirds of this semester. It is the key to complex Latin, and it is why this course exists.
💡 Mood, not tense. You already know one mood — the indicative, the mood of plain facts. The subjunctive is a second mood, with its own endings, for things that are wished, feared, intended, or merely possible. Latin marks the difference in the verb itself. English mostly does not, which is why the subjunctive takes a semester to learn well.
🏛️ PART 3 · THE STORY YOU WILL READ

Rome, 63 BC

There is no emperor yet — Augustus is still thirty years away. Rome is governed by its Senate (senātus), by elected magistrates, and by the Roman people (populus Rōmānus). Romans are fiercely proud of that arrangement, and they call it the rēs pūblica — literally "the public thing."

It is also under enormous strain. A century of civil war has worn out the old traditions, and ambitious men compete for power by any means available. In 63 BC a Roman senator named Lūcius Sergius Catilīna — Catiline — gathered ruined aristocrats, indebted men, and discontented soldiers, and planned to seize the state by force. The consul who stopped him was Marcus Tullius Cicerō.

Sallust · the historian
Wrote the Bellum Catilīnae about twenty years later. He is looking back, judging, using the story to argue that Rome had lost its virtūs. His Latin is compressed and severe.
Cicero · the orator
Delivered four speeches In Catilīnam in 63 BC, while the conspiracy was still live. He is speaking in the moment, under pressure, to persuade. His Latin is long, rhythmic, and built to be heard.

You already know Cicero — but you know him as a letter-writer and a friend, from M/J Latin Intermediate. This year you meet his public voice: the same man, standing in the Senate, in the worst week of his career. Same events, two authors, two purposes. Where they disagree is where the reading gets interesting.

📜 About the Latin you will read. Every passage this year is real Sallust and real Cicero, adapted — simplified so that it never asks you for a form you have not been taught, with every unfamiliar word glossed. Each passage says clearly at the top that it is adapted. Reading these authors completely unadapted, at full speed, is what high-school Latin is for; your job this year is to read them well at the level you are at.

🌐 YOUR ARTHUR SESSION THIS MODULE

Where: the late-Republican Forum Rōmānum or the Senate House (Cūria) — the ground Cicero and Catiline actually stood on. [ARTHUR: link and entry steps]

Do this: label six things you can see, using Latin you already own — columna (column), iānua (door), via (road), templum (temple), senātor (senator), rōstra (the speaker's platform).

Then write one Latin sentence using an ablative absolute about something in the scene. For example: Populō spectante, ōrātor ad rōstra ambulat. — "With the people watching, the speaker walks to the platform." Stay inside vocabulary you know; this is for confidence, not for a grade.

★ ADMĪRĀTIŌ · WONDER

Cicero used the word admīrātiō for the state a person is in when something strikes them as worth understanding. It is not the same as being impressed; it is closer to being caught. You are three years into a language that most people never begin, and this year you will read Romans arguing about whether their republic deserved to survive — in their own words, in their own grammar. Admīrātiō is the right way to start: not "how hard will this be," but "what am I about to get to see?"
