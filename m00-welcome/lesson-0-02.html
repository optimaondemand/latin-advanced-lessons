📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two questions, asked in order, unlock any Latin noun.
There are only two things you ever need from a noun: which family does it belong to (so you know the endings), and what job is it doing in this sentence (so you know the meaning). You already have the tools for both. Today is one fast pass to make them automatic again.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name any noun's declension from its genitive singular ending.
2
I will be able to state the core job of each of the five cases and the question it answers.
3
I will be able to identify the case, number, and function of nouns in sentences about the Catilinarian crisis.
📚 PART 1 · THE GENITIVE IS THE ID CARD

A dictionary entry gives you the nominative and then the genitive singular. The nominative can lie — manus and servus look alike and behave completely differently. The genitive never lies.

DeclensionGenitive sg.Example entry
1st-aepuella, puellae f. — girl · patria, patriae f. — homeland
2nd-īservus, servī m. — slave · ager, agrī m. — field · bellum, bellī n. — war
3rd-isrēx, rēgis m. — king · cōnsul, cōnsulis m. — consul · nōmen, nōminis n. — name
4th-ūsmanus, manūs f. — hand · senātus, senātūs m. — Senate · cornū, cornūs n. — horn
5th-eī / -ēīrēs, reī f. — thing, affair · diēs, diēī m. — day
💡 Two words you will meet constantly this year live in the 4th and 5th declensions: senātus, senātūs m. (the Senate) and rēs pūblica, reī pūblicae f. (the republic — literally "the public thing," two words that decline separately: rēs 5th declension, pūblica a 1st/2nd-declension adjective agreeing with it).

📚 PART 2 · FIVE CASES, FIVE JOBS

Knowing the family gets you the ending. The case tells you what the word is doing. Each case answers a question — ask the question and the translation follows.

CaseCore jobAsk yourself
NominativeSubject; predicate nounWho or what is doing this?
GenitivePossession; description; "of"Whose? Of what?
DativeIndirect object; person concernedTo whom? For whom?
AccusativeDirect object; object of ad, in, per, trāns…Whom or what — receiving the action?
AblativeMeans, manner, agent, separation, place; object of ā/ab, cum, dē, ē/ex, in, sineBy, with, from, or in what?
✍️ Two ablatives worth separating again, because they look identical and mean different things: ā/ab + ablative marks a person who does something (agent) — ā Cicerōne, "by Cicero." A bare ablative with no preposition marks a thing used to do it (means) — virtūte, "by courage," verbīs, "by words."

The genitive of description

One genitive use is worth naming now, because Sallust uses it to introduce Catiline. Latin can describe a person by putting the quality in the genitive:

Catilīna erat vir magnae audāciae sed malī animī.
"Catiline was a man of great boldness but of an evil mind."
magnae audāciae and malī animī are both genitive singular — the genitive of description. Not possession: the boldness does not own the man, it characterizes him.
🧠 PART 3 · PUT IT TO WORK
✅ CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING
Each sentence is about the crisis of 63 BC. Name the case and function of the highlighted word.
Cicerō senātuī nūntiāvit perīculum magnum esse. — "Cicero announced to the Senate that the danger was great."
Coniūrātōrum cōnsilium ā Cicerōne cognitum est. — "The plan of the conspirators was discovered by Cicero."
Virtūte Rōmānōrum hostēs superātī sunt. — "By the courage of the Romans the enemy were overcome."
Senātōrēs in Cūriā convēnērunt. — "The senators gathered in the Senate House."
Which of these is a 4th-declension noun?
📜 DERIVATIVE MINUTE

Senātus comes from senex, "old man" — the Senate was literally the council of elders. English keeps it in senate, senator, and senile. And rēs pūblica, "the public thing," went through French into English as republic. When Romans argued about the rēs pūblica, they were arguing about whose thing Rome was.
