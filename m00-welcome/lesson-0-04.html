The Big Three, and Your First Sallust

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Three constructions do most of the heavy lifting in Roman prose.
Ablative absolute, indirect statement, relative clause. You already know all three. Today you refresh them side by side — and then meet them in the wild, in your first passage of adapted Sallust.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell an ablative absolute apart from a participle that simply agrees with the subject.
2
I will be able to spot an indirect statement by its accusative subject and infinitive.
3
I will be able to read an adapted passage of Sallust and name the constructions in it.
📚 PART 1 · THE BIG THREE, SIDE BY SIDE

1 · Ablative absolute

A noun and a participle (or a second noun), both in the ablative, sitting outside the grammar of the main sentence. It sets the circumstances: when, because, although, or after.

Cicerōne cōnsule, Catilīna coniūrātiōnem fēcit.
"With Cicero as consul — when Cicero was consul — Catiline formed a conspiracy."
Two ablative nouns, no participle needed: Latin has no present participle of esse, so Cicerōne cōnsule does the job on its own.
⚠️ The test that never fails. An ablative absolute is absolute — "loosened," standing free. Its noun is not the subject of the main verb and has no other job in the sentence. If the participle instead agrees with the subject in the nominative, it is not an ablative absolute at all: senātōrēs perterritī audiēbant = "the senators, frightened, listened." Same participle idea, different structure. You will be asked to tell these apart today.

2 · Indirect statement

After a verb of saying, thinking, knowing, or perceiving, Latin reports the content as accusative + infinitive. The infinitive's tense shows time relative to the main verb.

Cicerō crēdidit Catilīnam esse perīculōsum.
"Cicero believed that Catiline was dangerous."
Catilīnam = accusative subject · esse = present infinitive, so the danger is going on at the same time as the believing. English needs "that"; Latin needs no word for it at all.

3 · Relative clause

Quī, quae, quod takes its gender and number from its antecedent but its case from its own job inside the relative clause. That split is the whole skill.

Senātor quem Cicerō laudāvit fortis erat.
"The senator whom Cicero praised was brave."
Quem is masculine singular because senātor is — but accusative because it is the object of laudāvit.
📜 PART 2 · YOUR FIRST SALLUST

Adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 1–2 and 5. This is a simplified retelling in Latin, not Sallust's own sentences — it keeps his ideas and his portrait of Catiline while staying inside the grammar you already know. Read it straight through once for the sense before you analyze anything.

Sallustius, historicus Rōmānus, dē bellō Catilīnae scrīpsit. In prīmō capitulō librī suī Sallustius dīxit animum hominis esse summum: "Hominēs," inquit, "mente, nōn corpore, clārī esse dēbent." Itaque Sallustius opus suum ad memoriam posteritātis scrīpsit.

Catilīna autem erat vir magnae audāciae sed malī animī. Multōs virōs dēspērātōs et perīculōsōs in ūnum locum convocāvit. Ille crēdidit sē Rōmam capere posse et rem pūblicam dēlēre.

Sed Cicerō, cōnsul Rōmānus quī tum rem pūblicam regēbat, omnia coniūrātiōnis cōnsilia cognōvit. Senātuī nūntiāvit Catilīnam esse hostem reī pūblicae. Tum senātōrēs, verbīs Cicerōnis perterritī, magnā cūrā audiēbant.

📖 VOCABULARY GLOSS · every word not on your running list
  • historicus, historicī m. — historian
  • capitulum, capitulī n. — chapter, section
  • summus, -a, -um — highest, greatest
  • mente — abl. sg. of mēns, mentis f., mind — here ablative of means, "by the mind"
  • corpore — abl. sg. of corpus, corporis n., body — ablative of means
  • clārus, -a, -um — famous, distinguished
  • inquit — "he says / he said" (a verb that appears only in a few forms; always inserted inside a quotation)
  • opus, operis n. — work, literary work
  • posteritās, posteritātis f. — posterity, future generations
  • audācia, audāciae f. — boldness, audacity
  • dēspērātus, -a, -um — desperate, without hope (PPP of dēspērō, used as an adjective)
  • convocō, convocāre, convocāvī, convocātum — to call together, summon
  • rēs pūblica, reī pūblicae f. — the republic, the state (two words, each declining: acc. rem pūblicam, gen. reī pūblicae)
  • dēleō, dēlēre, dēlēvī, dēlētum — to destroy, wipe out
  • coniūrātiō, coniūrātiōnis f. — conspiracy
  • perterritus, -a, -um — thoroughly frightened (PPP of perterreō; per- = thoroughly)
  • cūra, cūrae f. — care, attention, anxiety
🧠 PART 3 · NAME THE CONSTRUCTION
✅ CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING
Each phrase below is bold in the passage. What construction is it?
Sallustius dīxit animum hominis esse summum.
Catilīna erat vir magnae audāciae sed malī animī.
Ille crēdidit sē Rōmam capere posse.
Cicerō, cōnsul Rōmānus quī tum rem pūblicam regēbat, cōnsilia cognōvit.
Tum senātōrēs, verbīs Cicerōnis perterritī, magnā cūrā audiēbant.
💡 That last one is the single most common error in advanced reading. Whenever you see an ablative near a participle, check the participle's own case first. Ablative participle plus ablative noun, unconnected to the subject → ablative absolute. Nominative participle agreeing with the subject → ordinary participial phrase. Get in the habit now; the subjunctive clauses coming later will pile on top of these.

Write out your English for each sentence before you flip the card. Use the gloss freely — that is what it is for.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Hominēs mente, nōn corpore, clārī esse dēbent.
click to compare
"Human beings ought to be distinguished by the mind, not by the body." Both mente and corpore are ablatives of means; clārī is nominative plural agreeing with hominēs.
Multōs virōs dēspērātōs et perīculōsōs in ūnum locum convocāvit.
click to compare
"He called together many desperate and dangerous men into one place." Three accusatives agreeing: multōs virōs dēspērātōs. In + accusative = motion into.
Cicerō senātuī nūntiāvit Catilīnam esse hostem reī pūblicae.
click to compare
"Cicero announced to the Senate that Catiline was an enemy of the republic." Senātuī dative; Catilīnam esse hostem indirect statement, with hostem accusative as the predicate noun; reī pūblicae genitive.
🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · WHO WAS SALLUST?

Gāius Sallustius Crispus (about 86–35 BC) was a Roman senator before he was a historian, and not an especially admired one. He served under Julius Caesar, held the office of tribune of the plebs, and governed the province of Africa Nova — where he was accused of enriching himself. The charge never came to trial, but it stuck to his name.

He then left politics and wrote history. He chose two subjects, and both are studies in failure: the conspiracy of Catiline, and Rome's war against the Numidian king Jugurtha. His argument in both is the same — that Rome's real crisis was moral, that virtūs had given way to avāritia (greed) and ambitiō (self-seeking ambition).

His opening move, which you just read in adapted form, is a trap worth noticing. He says human greatness lives in the animus, the mind, not the body. That sounds noble. But if he is right, then the Roman ruling class of his own day — chasing money, villas, and offices — stands condemned by its own standard. Sallust is never only telling you about Catiline. Read him knowing that he had reasons of his own, and enemies of his own.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Before you translate a participle, find its case. Ablative with an ablative noun of its own means "under these circumstances." Nominative agreeing with the subject just describes the subject. One question, asked every time, and half of advanced Latin reading gets easier.
