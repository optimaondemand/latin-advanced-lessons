Lesson 8.03
Reported Orders — Indirect Command
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The same shape you learned Monday, doing a completely different job.
When Latin reports an order instead of quoting it, it reaches for ut or nē plus a subjunctive — the identical machinery. What changes is the main verb: a verb of ordering, asking, urging, begging, warning, or persuading. Learn the list, and above all learn which case the person takes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to turn a direct command into an indirect command with ut/nē + subjunctive, and translate one into natural English.
2
I will be able to put the person commanded in the right case — dative after imperō and persuādeō, accusative after rogō, ōrō, moneō, hortor.
3
I will be able to explain why iubeō is the exception, and write it correctly with an accusative and an infinitive.
📚 PART 1 · FROM QUOTED TO REPORTED

In November of 63 BC Cicero stood up in the Senate and told Catiline to get out of Rome. Quoted directly, that is an imperative — the mood of command you have had since 0706000:

"Discēde ex urbe!"
"Leave the city!" — a direct command, imperative mood

Now report it. Nobody is quoting anymore; a narrator is telling us what happened. Latin drops the imperative and builds a clause:

Cicerō Catilīnae imperāvit ut ex urbe discēderet.
"Cicero ordered Catiline to leave the city."

Three things to notice, and only the third is new:

PieceWhat it is doing
ut…discēderetNothing new. Ut + imperfect subjunctive, secondary sequence after the perfect imperāvit — exactly the shape from Monday.
nē instead of utNothing new. A negative order uses nē, same as negative purpose: Cicerō cīvēs monuit nē timērent — "Cicero warned the citizens not to fear."
CatilīnaeThis is new. Imperō takes the person commanded in the dative, not the accusative. Get this wrong and the sentence stops being Latin.
💡 English will mislead you here, so decide now to ignore it. English says "ordered Catiline to leave" — an infinitive, and "Catiline" looks like a direct object. Latin says Catilīnae imperāvit ut discēderet — a dative and a subjunctive clause. Translate into the English infinitive freely; never translate out of it by writing an infinitive in your Latin.

✍️ Two of them you can reason about. Imperō and persuādeō both take the dative because of what they literally do: you give an order to someone, and you make something sweet (suādeō, from the same root as English suave) to someone. Latin treats the person as the one to whom the action is directed, not the thing acted on. The accusative group treats the person as the one you work on directly — you ask them, you warn them.
⚠️ PART 3 · THE ONE VERB THAT REFUSES UT: IUBEŌ

Iubeō, iubēre, iussī, iussum means "order, command" — it looks like a perfect member of the imperō family. It is not. Iubeō takes an accusative and an infinitive, with no ut anywhere.

IUBEŌ — ACC. + INFINITIVE ✓
Cicerō Catilīnam ex urbe exīre iussit.
"Cicero ordered Catiline to go out of the city."
NOT THIS ✗
Cicerō Catilīnae iussit ut exīret.
A very common error. Iubeō does not take a dative, and it does not take ut.
📖 GLOSS FOR THE SENTENCE ABOVE
  • exīre — "to go out," present infinitive of exeō (a compound of eō, "to go"). Not yet studied — gloss provided: the irregular verbs ferō, eō, volō, nōlō, mālō, fiō are taught in Module 15. You only need to recognize this one form as an infinitive. Latin authors write ex urbe exīre often; if you would rather write discēdere ("to depart"), that is equally correct: Cicerō Catilīnam ex urbe discēdere iussit.

Put the two side by side and hold the contrast, because this is the single most-tested distinction in the module:

VerbPersonThe order itself
imperōdative — Catilīnaeut / nē + subjunctive — ut discēderet
iubeōaccusative — Catilīnaminfinitive, no ut — discēdere
💡 A memory hook. Iubeō gives you an English word you already know from music: a jussive command. It behaves like the verbs of saying you met in 0706010 — accusative plus infinitive, the indirect-statement shape. Imperō behaves like a verb of giving. Two different pictures of what an order is.

📜 Etymology moment. Imperō is the root of an enormous English family: emperor, empire, imperial, imperative — a sentence that commands is an imperative sentence, and a man whose whole title is "the one who commands" is an imperātor. Suspicor gives you suspect and suspicion (sub- "from below" + specere "to look" — to look at someone from below). And testis gives you testify, testimony, testament, contest. Cicero's whole case on December 3rd turned on testēs and signa — witnesses and seals.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

An indirect command uses the same ut/nē + subjunctive clause as a purpose clause; what marks it is the main verb. The work is in the case: imperō and persuādeō take the dative, rogō, ōrō, moneō, hortor take the accusative, and iubeō takes an accusative with an infinitive and no ut at all.
