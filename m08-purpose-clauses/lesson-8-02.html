Lesson 8.02
Nē: One Word for "So That…Not"
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
English needs four words. Latin needs two letters.
"So that the envoys would not escape" — in Latin that is nē lēgātī effugerent. One little word, nē, replaces both ut and the negative. And it is not ut nōn. Of everything in this module, that is the distinction most worth owning.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to build and translate a negative purpose clause with nē + subjunctive, in either sequence.
2
I will be able to state the rule that negative purpose is nē, never ut nōn, and say what ut nōn is reserved for.
3
I will be able to put a passive or deponent verb inside a purpose clause with the right subjunctive endings.
📚 PART 1 · SWAP ONE WORD

To make a purpose clause negative, you change exactly one thing: ut becomes nē. The verb does not move. The sequence rule does not change. Nothing else happens.

POSITIVE PURPOSE
ut Catilīna suspicārētur
"so that Catiline might suspect"
NEGATIVE PURPOSE
nē Catilīna suspicārētur
"so that Catiline might not suspect"

Three sentences, both sequences, with the English you would actually write:

LatinEnglish
Cicerō tacuit nē Catilīna suspicārētur.Cicero kept quiet so that Catiline would not suspect. (tacuit perfect → secondary → imperfect subj.)
Cōnsul custōdēs pōnet nē lēgātī effugiant.The consul will station guards so that the envoys may not escape. (pōnet future → primary → present subj.)
Cicerō cōnsilia cēpit nē rēs pūblica dēlērētur.Cicero made plans so that the republic would not be destroyed. (passive subjunctive — see Part 3.)
✍️ An older English word that fits nē perfectly: lest. "Cicero kept quiet lest Catiline suspect." You do not have to write that way, and "so that…not" is always acceptable — but if you meet lest in an older translation or a dictionary entry, now you know it is a nē clause underneath.
⚠️ PART 2 · THE MISTAKE WORTH NOT MAKING
Negative purpose is nē. It is never ut nōn.

The temptation is obvious. You know ut means "so that" and you know nōn means "not," so ut nōn looks like it should mean "so that…not." It is a reasonable guess and it is wrong. Latin reserves ut nōn for a different construction — one you have not met yet.

🔏 Forward glance — not yet studied. Ut nōn belongs to result clauses, which you learn in Module 9: clauses that report what actually happened as a consequence, rather than what someone was aiming at. You will not be asked to read or write one this module, and nothing on the Module 8 quiz or translation requires it. All you need now is the boundary: nē for negative purpose; ut nōn is Module 9's job.

Two more traps, both smaller, both common:

Do not writeWriteWhy
ut…nōn ✗nē ✓Negative purpose has its own word. Using ut nōn tells your reader you meant a result clause.
nē nōn ✗nē ✓Nē already contains the negative. Adding nōn double-negates it.
nē + indicative ✗nē + subjunctive ✓A purpose is still an intention whether it is positive or negative. The mood does not change.
📚 PART 3 · PASSIVE AND DEPONENT INSIDE THE CLAUSE

Cicero constantly wants to say "so that something might be done" — passive. And half his narrative verbs are deponents from Modules 2 and 3. Both go into a purpose clause with no special rules: you just need the passive-shaped subjunctive endings from Module 6.

VerbPresent subjunctive passiveImperfect subjunctive passive
1st — amōamer, amēris, amētur, amēmur, amēminī, amenturamārer, amārēris, amārētur, amārēmur, amārēminī, amārentur
2nd — moneōmonear, moneāris, moneātur…monērer, monērēris, monērētur…
3rd — regōregar, regāris, regātur…regerer, regerēris, regerētur…
3rd io — capiōcapiar, capiāris, capiātur…caperer, caperēris, caperētur…
4th — audiōaudiar, audiāris, audiātur…audīrer, audīrēris, audīrētur…
💡 The pattern is the one you already know. Take the active subjunctive, then swap the active personal endings for the passive set -r, -ris, -tur, -mur, -minī, -ntur. Amem → amer. Amārem → amārer. That is the same trade you have been making since 0706010.

And a deponent, being passive in form and active in meaning, simply uses those same passive-shaped endings with an active sense:

DeponentPresent subjunctiveImperfect subjunctiveMeans
loquorloquar, loquāris, loquāturloquerer, loquerēris, loquerēturspeak
sequorsequar, sequāris, sequātursequerer, sequerēris, sequerēturfollow
suspicorsuspicer, suspicēris, suspicētursuspicārer, suspicārēris, suspicārētursuspect
🔍 Read the two boxes above against each other. Regerētur means "he might be ruled" — a genuine passive. Loquerētur means "he might speak" — a deponent, active in sense. Identical endings, opposite direction, exactly as in Module 2. The subjunctive changes nothing about that.

✍️ PART 5 · TRANSLATE, THEN CHECK

Write your English out before you flip. Two of these four are negative; one has a passive inside the clause.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Cicerō omnia dīligenter custōdīvit nē quid ad hostēs mānāret.
click to compare
"Cicero guarded everything carefully so that nothing might leak out to the enemy." Nē quid = "so that nothing" (after nē, quid means "anything"). Mānāret is imperfect subjunctive of mānō, mānāre, "to flow, leak" — secondary sequence after custōdīvit.
Cōnsul ad Forum prōcēdit ut populus omnia audiat.
click to compare
"The consul goes forward to the Forum so that the people may hear everything." Prōcēdit is present → primary → present subjunctive audiat. Gloss: prōcēdō, prōcēdere, prōcessī — to go forward, advance.
Cicerō tacēbat nē coniūrātī cōnsilium suum cognōscerent.
click to compare
"Cicero was keeping quiet so that the conspirators would not learn his plan." Tacēbat is imperfect → secondary → imperfect subjunctive cognōscerent (cognōscere + nt). Gloss: taceō, tacēre, tacuī — to be silent.
Litterae ad senātum portātae sunt ut signa agnōscerentur.
click to compare
"The letters were carried to the Senate so that the seals might be identified." Agnōscerentur is imperfect subjunctive passive — the seals are not identifying, they are being identified. Secondary sequence after the perfect passive portātae sunt. Gloss: agnōscō, agnōscere, agnōvī — to recognize, identify; signum, signī n. — sign, seal.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Negative purpose is nē — one word covering both "so that" and "not." Never ut nōn: Latin is saving that phrase for a different construction in Module 9. Everything else about the clause — mood, sequence, passive and deponent endings — stays exactly as it was.
