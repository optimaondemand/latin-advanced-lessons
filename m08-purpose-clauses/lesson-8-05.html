📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The skill worth having is not "recognize ut." It is telling apart four constructions that all look alike.
Today: one review table, one identifier drill, one form check, and a plain, honest word about the Module 8 gate — what it asks for, why it exists, and why a low first attempt costs you nothing.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell a purpose clause apart from an indirect command, an indirect statement, and an ordinary relative clause.
2
I will be able to produce any present or imperfect subjunctive form the module asks for, active, passive, or deponent.
3
I will be able to state exactly what the Module 8 gate requires of me and what I will do if my first attempt falls short.
🧠 MODULE 8 IN FIVE LINES
LessonThe one thing to keep
8.01Purpose = ut + subjunctive, answering "Why?" The main verb picks the tense: present/future → present subj., past → imperfect subj.
8.02Negative purpose = nē, one word, never ut nōn. Passive and deponent verbs go inside the clause with the passive-shaped subjunctive endings.
8.03Indirect command = the same clause after a verb of ordering or asking. imperō, persuādeō + dative; rogō, ōrō, moneō, hortor + accusative; iubeō + accusative + infinitive, no ut.
8.04In real prose these clauses carry the argument. A relative pronoun plus a subjunctive verb (quī…custōdīrent) is also purpose — recognize it.
8.05The reader's real job is discrimination: ut + subjunctive is not always purpose, and a subjunctive is not always ut.
✍️ Five questions to ask of any purpose clause you write. (1) Is the purpose positive or negative — ut or nē? (2) What tense is my main verb? (3) Therefore, present or imperfect subjunctive? (4) Is the clause's verb active, passive, or deponent? (5) If there is a person being ordered, does this verb want the dative or the accusative? Run that list and you will not lose a mark on this module.
🔍 PART 2 · THE IDENTIFIER

Seven sentences. For each one, name the construction. This is the exact skill the first section of the quiz tests.

✅ PURPOSE, OR SOMETHING ELSE?
Read the main verb, then the subordinate clause, then decide.
Cicerō ad Forum prōcessit ut populus omnia audīret.
Cicerō lēgātīs persuāsit ut tabellās acciperent.
Cicerō dīxit coniūrātōs tabellās dedisse.
Cicerō custōdēs mīsit quī pontem custōdīrent.
Cicerō custōdēs mīsit quī pontem custōdiēbant.
Cicerō Catilīnam ex urbe discēdere iussit.
Cicerō ita fortiter ēgit ut omnēs eum laudārent. (ita — so, in such a way; fortiter — bravely; ēgit — he acted; laudārent — impf. subj. of laudō)
✍️ PART 3 · FORM CHECK

Write each form out on paper before you flip the card. Anything you miss here is exactly what to review before the quiz — and the quiz asks for forms like these in writing.

🔍 PRODUCE THE FORM · THEN CLICK TO CHECK
3rd person plural present subjunctive of audiō, audīre
click to check
audiant. The 4th-conjugation present subjunctive runs audiam, audiās, audiat, audiāmus, audiātis, audiant. Use it after a present or future main verb.
3rd person singular imperfect subjunctive of capiō, capere
click to check
caperet. Present infinitive capere + t. Do not reach for capiam-type endings here: the imperfect subjunctive ignores the io-stem entirely.
3rd person plural imperfect subjunctive of effugiō, effugere
click to check
effugerent. Effugere + nt. This is the form in nē lēgātī effugerent — the most-quoted clause in the module.
2nd person plural present subjunctive of teneō, tenēre
click to check
teneātis. From Cicero's own request in the reading: Ōrō vōs ut huius diēī memoriam teneātis. Present, because ōrō is present.
3rd person singular imperfect subjunctive passive of dēleō, dēlēre
click to check
dēlērētur. Build the active dēlēret, then trade the active ending for the passive one: -t becomes -tur with the long vowel — dēlērētur, "it might be destroyed."
3rd person singular imperfect subjunctive of the deponent suspicor, suspicārī
click to check
suspicārētur. Passive in form, active in meaning: "he might suspect." As in Cicerō tacuit nē Catilīna suspicārētur.
Fill the gap: Cicerō mīlitibus imperāvit ____ venīrent.
click to check
ut — "he ordered the soldiers to come." And notice mīlitibus: imperō always takes the dative of the person ordered. If the sense were negative, the gap would be nē, never ut nōn.
🔒 THE MODULE 8 GATE — PLAINLY

Module 8 is a mastery gate. The Module 8 Quiz is out of 40 points, and you need 32 out of 40 — 80% — to unlock the rest of the semester. Modules 9 through 16 stay closed until you get there.

You may retake the quiz as many times as you like, and we would like you to. Canvas keeps your highest score, so a first attempt that goes badly costs you nothing except the time to go back and fix what it showed you. There is no penalty, no limit, and no record of the attempts that did not work. Take the first one as a diagnosis.

Why the gate is here, and only here. There are exactly two in the semester — this one and Module 16 — and this is not an arbitrary place for one. Everything from Module 9 on is built directly on top of the subjunctive: result clauses (9), cum clauses (10), indirect questions (11), fear clauses (12), then two more tenses of the subjunctive (13), the jussive and hortatory uses (14), and conditions (15). Every one of them assumes you can build a subjunctive and pick its tense by sequence without stopping to think.

A student who slips past this point shaky does not get an easier course — they get a much harder one, for eight straight modules. The gate is not a hurdle we put in your way. It is the point at which the rest of the semester becomes readable. If your first attempt lands under 32, go back to 8.01 Part 4 (sequence), 8.02 Part 3 (passive and deponent forms), and 8.03 Part 2 (the case each verb takes) — in our experience that is where nearly all the lost points live — then come back and take it again.

➡️ WHAT'S COMING
M9
Module 9 — Result Clauses
The other ut clause — and finally, the place where ut nōn belongs. Result reports what actually followed rather than what was intended, and it usually announces itself with a word like ita, tam, or tantus in the main clause. The reading turns to Sallust and the debate in the Senate over what to do with the men Cicero had just arrested. Module 9 opens when the Module 8 gate is cleared.
💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A subjunctive after ut is a question, not an answer: purpose, or a reported order, or something else? Read the main verb first — it tells you the construction, and it tells you the tense. That habit is what the gate is checking, and it is what makes the next eight modules readable.
