📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In Module 4 we told you a third way to say purpose was coming. It is here.
You already own two: ad dīcendum and dīcendī causā, both "in order to speak." Today you get the one Cicero reaches for most often — a whole clause, with ut and its own verb in the subjunctive: Cicerō surrēxit ut dīceret, "Cicero rose so that he might speak." Everything you learned in Modules 6 and 7 was built for this moment.

1

2

3

📚 PART 1 · THE PROMISE FROM MODULE 4, PAID

Lesson 4.03 was called Two Ways to Say Purpose, and it ended by telling you there was a third — the most common one of all — waiting in Module 8. Here is the whole family in one table. Two of these three you can already write.

ConstructionLatinEnglish
Module 4 · ad + accusative gerundCicerō surrēxit ad dīcendum.Cicero rose to speak / for the purpose of speaking.
Module 4 · genitive gerund + causāCicerō surrēxit dīcendī causā.Cicero rose for the sake of speaking.
Module 8 · ut + subjunctiveCicerō surrēxit ut dīceret.Cicero rose so that he might speak.
💡 Why Latin bothers with three. The gerund constructions are compact but silent about who is doing the purposed action — ad dīcendum just means "for speaking." A full ut clause has its own verb, so it can carry its own subject, its own object, its own negative. When Cicero needs to say "so that the people might know what the consul had done," only the clause can do it.

📚 PART 2 · THE DEFINITION, AND THE SHAPE
A purpose clause answers the question "Why?" It is built from ut + a verb in the subjunctive, and it tells you what the action of the main clause was aimed at.

Look at what changes and what does not:

A FACT — INDICATIVE
Senātōrēs intellegunt.
"The senators understand." It happened. It is true.
AN AIM — SUBJUNCTIVE
…ut senātōrēs intellegant.
"…so that the senators may understand." Aimed at. Maybe achieved, maybe not.

English gives you four good ways to render the clause. All four are correct; pick whichever reads best:

English patternExample on Cicerō dīxit ut senātōrēs intellegerent
in order to + verbCicero spoke in order for the senators to understand.
so that…might / wouldCicero spoke so that the senators might understand.
to + verb (when the subject is the same)Cicero rose to speak. (from ut dīceret)
in order that…mayCicero spoke in order that the senators might understand.
⚠️ What you must not do: translate the subjunctive as if it were indicative. Ut senātōrēs intellegerent is not "so that the senators understood" — that claims it worked. It is "so that the senators might understand." The whole point of the mood is that Latin is telling you about an intention, and intentions can fail.
🔄 PART 3 · THE FORMS YOU ALREADY HAVE — MODULE 6 RECALL

Nothing new here. This is the Module 6 material, and you should be able to fill in most of it from memory before you look. Purpose clauses use only two of the four subjunctive tenses — present and imperfect. (The perfect and pluperfect subjunctive arrive in Module 13 and are not used in purpose clauses.)

✍️ The imperfect subjunctive is the easiest form in Latin. Take the present infinitive exactly as it is and add -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt. Regere → regerem, regerēs, regeret, regerēmus, regerētis, regerent. No stem changes, no exceptions to memorize. If you can read a dictionary entry, you can build this tense.
🔄 PART 4 · WHICH TENSE? THE MAIN VERB DECIDES — MODULE 7 RECALL

This is the sequence of tenses from Module 7, and in a purpose clause it comes down to one line: look at the main verb, then pick.

Main verb is…SequencePurpose clause verb
present or future
dīcit, dīcet		primarypresent subjunctive — ut…intellegant
past: imperfect, perfect, or pluperfect
dīcēbat, dīxit, dīxerat		secondaryimperfect subjunctive — ut…intellegerent

Four worked examples. The only thing changing on the left is the tense of dīcō; watch what that forces on the right.

LatinEnglish
Cicerō dīcit ut senātōrēs intellegant.Cicero speaks so that the senators may understand. (present → primary)
Cicerō dīcet ut senātōrēs intellegant.Cicero will speak so that the senators may understand. (future → primary)
Cicerō dīxit ut senātōrēs intellegerent.Cicero spoke so that the senators might understand. (perfect → secondary)
Cicerō dīcēbat ut senātōrēs intellegerent.Cicero was speaking so that the senators might understand. (imperfect → secondary)
💡 A shortcut that will not fail you here. English does the same thing without being asked: "so that they may" after a present verb, "so that they might" after a past one. If your English has may, your Latin wants the present subjunctive. If your English has might, your Latin wants the imperfect.

★ VIRTŪS · COURAGE

Virtūs comes from vir, "man," and for Romans it meant courage first and excellence generally. You met it in Module 2 as the nerve to hold two things at once. Here it means something more specific: acting on purpose.

Romans drew a hard line between virtūs and audācia — recklessness, nerve without a plan. Catiline had audācia. What Cicero claimed for himself was virtūs: he did each thing so that a particular good would follow, or so that a particular harm would not. That is not a coincidence of vocabulary. The purpose clause is the grammar of intentional courage — it is the shape a sentence takes when someone can say why they did what they did. Watch for it in Thursday's reading; nearly every action Cicero reports comes with one attached.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A purpose clause is ut + subjunctive, and it answers "Why?" The tense is not your choice: a present or future main verb takes the present subjunctive, a past main verb takes the imperfect. Read the main verb first, then build.
