Lesson 8.04
The Seals at the Senate
Reading · Cicero, In Catilīnam III (adapted)
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A dozen purpose clauses and indirect commands in four short paragraphs. That is not an accident.
December 3rd, 63 BC, is the day the Catilinarian conspiracy stopped being a rumour and became evidence — sealed letters, in the conspirators' own hands, opened in front of witnesses. Cicero told the story that afternoon in the Forum. Nearly every action in it comes attached to a clause explaining what it was for.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to parse every purpose clause and indirect command in an adapted passage from Cicero's third Catilīnarian.
2
I will be able to recognize a relative purpose clause (quī + subjunctive) when I meet one in real prose.
3
I will be able to explain why written, sealed evidence changed what Cicero could say to the Roman people.
📜 PART 1 · THE READING

Adapted from Cicero, In Catilīnam III. A simplified Latin retelling of the events of December 3, 63 BC — not Cicero's own sentences. The narrative frame is ours; the events are the ones Cicero reports. Every purpose clause and indirect command is marked in red. Read it once straight through for sense before you parse anything.

Catilīna ex urbe discesserat; Cicerō enim eum ex urbe discēdere iusserat. Lentulus autem et Cethēgus in urbe manēbant, et coniūrātī nova cōnsilia capiēbant. Illī ad Allobrogēs, gentem Gallicam, lēgātōs mīsērunt ut auxilium peterent.

Sed Allobrogēs rem Cicerōnī nūntiāvērunt. Tum cōnsul lēgātīs persuāsit ut tabellās ā coniūrātīs acciperent. Deinde duōbus praetōribus imperāvit ut ad Pontem Mulvium prōgrederentur. Illī noctū ad pontem vēnērunt nē lēgātī cum tabellīs effugerent. Ibi et lēgātōs et Volturcium comprehendērunt, et tabellās signātās ad cōnsulem portāvērunt.

Prīmā lūce Cicerō senātum convocāvit. Ibi singulīs coniūrātīs imperāvit ut signa sua agnōscerent. Lentulus signum suum agnōvit; deinde omnia cōnfessus est. Testēs aderant nē quis posteā dubitāret. Cōnsul tamen tabellās dīligenter custōdīvit, nē quid ad Catilīnae exercitum mānāret.

Post haec Cicerō ad Forum prōcessit ut populus omnia audīret. Cīvēs monuit nē timērent. "Ōrō vōs, Quirītēs," inquit, "ut huius diēī memoriam semper teneātis. Ego enim, nē quid vōbīs nocēret, haec omnia fēcī." Nam cōnsul, ut rem pūblicam servāret, magna perīcula suscēperat. Nōn armīs sed tabellīs signātīs et testibus rem pūblicam servāvit.

  • Lentulus, Lentulī m. — Lentulus, a praetor in 63 BC and the senior conspirator left in the city
  • Cethēgus, Cethēgī m. — Cethegus, another of the conspirators in Rome
  • coniūrātī, coniūrātōrum m. pl. — the conspirators
  • Allobrogēs, Allobrogum m. pl. — the Allobroges, a Gallic people whose envoys were in Rome with grievances
  • gentem Gallicam — "a Gallic people" (accusative of gēns Gallica)
  • lēgātus, lēgātī m. — envoy, ambassador
  • auxilium, auxiliī n. — help, military support
  • petō, petere, petīvī — to seek, request
  • nūntiāvērunt — "they reported" (perfect of nūntiō, nūntiāre)
  • tabella, tabellae f. — writing tablet; in the plural, the sealed letters
  • signātus, -a, -um — sealed (from signō, signāre)
  • signum, signī n. — sign; here the seal, the mark a man's ring pressed into the wax
  • praetor, praetōris m. — praetor, a Roman magistrate ranking just below consul
  • duōbus — dative plural of duo, "two"
  • prōgredior, prōgredī, prōgressus sum — to advance, go forward (deponent, Module 2)
  • Pontem Mulvium — the Mulvian Bridge, on the road running north out of Rome (accusative after ad)
  • noctū (adv.) — by night
  • Volturcius, Volturciī m. — Titus Volturcius, the courier travelling with the envoys
  • comprehendērunt — "they arrested, seized" (perfect of comprehendō)
  • portāvērunt — "they carried" (perfect of portō, portāre)
  • prīmā lūce — "at first light, at dawn" (ablative of time)
  • convocāvit — "he called together, summoned" (perfect of convocō)
  • singulīs — dative plural of singulī, "one at a time, each man individually"
  • agnōscō, agnōscere, agnōvī — to recognize, identify
  • cōnfessus est — "he confessed" (perfect of the deponent cōnfiteor)
  • testis, testis m./f. — witness; testēs aderant — "witnesses were present"
  • nē quis — "so that no one" (after nē, quis means "anyone")
  • posteā (adv.) — afterwards
  • dubitō, dubitāre — to doubt, hesitate
  • dīligenter (adv.) — carefully
  • custōdiō, custōdīre, custōdīvī — to guard
  • nē quid — "so that nothing" (after nē, quid means "anything")
  • exercitum — accusative of exercitus, exercitūs m., "army"
  • mānō, mānāre, mānāvī — to flow, trickle, leak; used of news getting out
  • prōcēdō, prōcēdere, prōcessī — to go forward, advance
  • Quirītēs — "Citizens!" the formal address to the Roman people in a public speech
  • inquit — "he says / he said," inserted inside a quotation
  • huius diēī — "of this day" (genitive of hic diēs)
  • memoriam tenēre — "to hold the memory of," to remember
  • noceō, nocēre, nocuī (+ dat.) — to harm, hurt
  • suscēperat — "he had taken on, undertaken" (pluperfect of suscipiō)
  • rem pūblicam — accusative of rēs pūblica, reī pūblicae f., the republic, the state — always two words
  • armīs — ablative plural of arma, armōrum n. pl., "arms, weapons"
💡 Watch the first sentence. Cicerō eum ex urbe discēdere iusserat reports an order — but there is no ut and no subjunctive, because the verb is iubeō. Two sentences later, lēgātīs persuāsit ut…acciperent reports another order with a dative and a full clause. Same kind of event, two different constructions, chosen by the verb. That contrast is on the quiz.

🔍 Eleven secondary, one primary — and the exception proves the rule. The narration is all in the past, so every clause in it takes the imperfect subjunctive. Then Cicero opens his mouth: Ōrō vōs…ut…teneātis. He is speaking now, in the present, so the sequence flips to primary and the subjunctive is present. Sequence is not about the clause — it is about the verb that governs it.
📚 PART 3 · ONE MORE SHAPE TO RECOGNIZE: RELATIVE PURPOSE

Cicero and Sallust both do this, so you should be able to spot it: instead of ut, a purpose clause can be introduced by a relative pronoun — quī, quae, quod — when the purpose attaches to a noun in the main clause. The relative pronoun stands in for "so that he / so that they."

ORDINARY RELATIVE CLAUSE — INDICATIVE
Cicerō custōdēs mīsit quī pontem custōdiēbant.
"Cicero sent guards who were guarding the bridge." A fact about the guards.
RELATIVE PURPOSE — SUBJUNCTIVE
Cicerō custōdēs mīsit quī pontem custōdīrent.
"Cicero sent guards to guard the bridge." Why he sent them.

One letter of difference in the verb; a completely different job in the sentence. The tell is always the mood: a relative clause in the subjunctive, after a verb of sending, choosing, or looking for, is expressing purpose.

🔏 Recognition only, and we mean it. You need to be able to identify a relative purpose clause and translate it. You are not asked to write one — on the quiz, on the translation, or on the ARTHUR project, an ut clause is always accepted where a relative purpose clause would also work. Cicerō custōdēs mīsit ut pontem custōdīrent says the same thing and is entirely correct Latin.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Illī ad Allobrogēs lēgātōs mīsērunt ut auxilium peterent.
click to compare
"They sent envoys to the Allobroges so that they might seek help" — or, more naturally, "…to ask for help." A plain positive purpose clause: mīsērunt is perfect, so secondary sequence, so peterent (imperfect subjunctive of petō).
Deinde duōbus praetōribus imperāvit ut ad Pontem Mulvium prōgrederentur.
click to compare
"Then he ordered the two praetors to advance to the Mulvian Bridge." Indirect command: imperāvit + dative duōbus praetōribus + ut + subjunctive. Prōgrederentur is the imperfect subjunctive of a deponent — passive-shaped endings, active meaning, exactly as in Module 2.
Ibi singulīs coniūrātīs imperāvit ut signa sua agnōscerent.
click to compare
"There he ordered each of the conspirators to identify his own seal." Again imperō + dative (singulīs coniūrātīs). Sua is reflexive — each man's own seal, which is the whole force of the moment: no one could claim the letter was somebody else's.
"Ōrō vōs, Quirītēs, ut huius diēī memoriam semper teneātis."
click to compare
"I beg you, citizens, always to hold the memory of this day." The one primary-sequence clause in the passage: ōrō is present, so teneātis is present subjunctive. Ōrō takes the accusative of the person begged — vōs. Note how English prefers the infinitive ("to hold") where Latin has a subjunctive clause.
🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · THE DAY THE EVIDENCE ARRIVED

Until December 3rd, Cicero had informants, rumours, and Catiline's own behaviour. What he did not have was proof a Roman jury — or a Roman crowd — could see. That changed with a piece of wax.

Envoys of the Allobroges, a Gallic people, were in Rome with complaints about Roman administration. The conspirators approached them for military help; the Allobroges reported it. Cicero arranged for the envoys to leave the city carrying tabellae — letters, folded and sealed — and sent two praetors ahead to the Pons Mulvius to intercept them by night, along with a courier named Titus Volturcius. At dawn Cicero convened the Senate and had the letters produced. A Roman signum was a personal thing: the ring you wore pressed your mark into the wax, and everyone who dealt with you knew it. Asked to look at his own seal, Lentulus — a sitting praetor — acknowledged it, in front of testēs.

Why the grammar of this lesson fits the day. Cicero's account of December 3rd is almost entirely a chain of purposes: he did this so that that would follow, and this so that something else would not. It is a consul arguing that everything he did was deliberate and for the rēs pūblica — that it was virtūs and not audācia. Whether you believe him is the reader's decision, and Romans themselves argued about it for the rest of his life. But notice how much of the argument is carried by clause structure rather than by adjectives. A man who can say why about every single action is making a case.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

In real prose, purpose clauses and indirect commands are not decoration — they are how a narrator explains a plan. Twelve of them in four paragraphs is Cicero insisting that nothing he did on December 3rd was accidental. Read the ut and nē clauses and you have read his argument.
