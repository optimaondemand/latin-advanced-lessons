📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You have been reading sī since your first year. This week you learn what Latin can do with it that English cannot do at all.
A condition is a sentence built out of two halves: a sī clause that supposes something, and a main clause that says what follows from the supposing. Latin has four of these patterns, and it tells you which one you are in by the mood and tense of the verbs — not by any extra word. One of the four you already own from 0706010. The other three are this module, and they end at the pattern Latin uses to say what would have happened and did not — the grammar of every argument anyone has ever had about the past.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to point at the protasis and the apodosis of any conditional sentence, whichever half comes first.
2
I will be able to read and translate a simple condition (sī + indicative) and explain why the indicative is the honest mood for it.
3
I will be able to name the four condition types and say what a subjunctive in a sī clause is telling me that an indicative would not.
📚 PART 1 · A SENTENCE MADE OF TWO SENTENCES

Take an ordinary English sentence apart:

If Catiline is in the city, the danger is great.
The red half supposes something. The blue half says what follows if the supposing holds. Neither half is a complete thought on its own: “if Catiline is in the city” leaves you waiting, and “the danger is great” on its own is a plain claim rather than a consequence of anything.

The two halves have names, and it is worth learning them now, because from Lesson 15.02 onward you will need to talk about each half separately:

NameWhich halfWhat it does
protasis
pro-TASS-iss		the sī clause
sī Catilīna in urbe est		supposes something
the “if” half; it makes no claim that the thing is so
apodosis
ap-ODD-oss-iss		the main clause
perīculum magnum est		states the consequence
the “then” half, even when no “then” is written
✍️ Where those two words come from. They are Greek. Roman schoolmasters took most of their grammatical vocabulary from Greek teachers, and these two came along with the rest: protasis is “the stretching-forward,” the part held out first, and apodosis is “the giving-back,” the answer to it. If you would rather think in Latin: the sī clause states the condiciō — the condition, the terms — and the main clause states what follows from those terms.
💡 The names are not about word order. Latin puts the sī clause first most of the time, but not always: Perīculum magnum est sī Catilīna in urbe est is the same sentence with the halves swapped, and the protasis is still the sī half. Find the word sī, and you have found the protasis, wherever it is sitting.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Before any Latin: think about the English sentence “If the Senate had not acted, Rome would have burned.” Is that sentence true? What kind of thing is it even claiming?
📚 PART 2 · THE ONE YOU ALREADY OWN: SĪ + INDICATIVE

This is the type you have been reading since 0706000, and it has a name: the simple or factual condition. Both halves are indicative. Nothing else is going on.

Sī Catilīna in urbe est, perīculum magnum est.
“If Catiline is in the city, the danger is great.” Two present indicatives. The speaker does not say whether Catiline is in the city; he says that if that is the case, this follows. That is all a factual condition ever claims.
Sī hoc crēdis, errās.
“If you believe this, you are wrong.” Gloss: crēdō, crēdere — to believe; errō, errāre — to be wrong, to wander.
Sī senātus hoc dēcrēvit, cōnsul rectē fēcit.
“If the Senate decreed this, the consul acted rightly.” Past tenses, still indicative, still simply factual. Gloss: dēcernō, dēcernere, dēcrēvī — to decree, decide; rectē (adv.) — rightly.

And the future belongs here too. When a Latin writer means “if this happens, that will happen” — a real possibility, expected to be settled one way or the other — both halves stay indicative, in the future or future perfect:

Sī tacēbis, tūtus eris.  ·  Sī Catilīna vēnerit, cīvēs eum capient.
“If you keep silent, you will be safe.” · “If Catiline comes, the citizens will capture him.” Latin is fussier than English here: it puts a future perfect (vēnerit) in the protasis when the coming has to be finished before the capturing can start. English says “if he comes” and lets you work it out.
💡 Why the indicative is the right mood for all of these. The indicative is Latin's mood for fact — for what is, was, or will be the case. In a factual condition the writer is not backing away from anything. He is not saying “this is not so” or “this might conceivably be so.” He is saying: whether or not the protasis happens to be true, the two things are tied together. Nothing about that requires the subjunctive, and Latin does not use it.
📚 PART 3 · WHAT THE SUBJUNCTIVE ADDS

Now look at three English sentences that are all “about” Catiline being in the city, and notice that they say three completely different things:

EnglishWhat the speaker is committing to
If Catiline is in the city, there is danger.No commitment either way. Maybe he is, maybe he is not.
If Catiline should be in the city, there would be danger.A possibility held at arm's length. It could happen; the speaker is not betting on it.
If Catiline were in the city, there would be danger.He is not. The speaker says so by the way he builds the sentence.

English marks those differences with a pile of helper words — should, would, were — and does it so badly that people argue about “if I was” versus “if I were.” Latin marks them with the mood and tense of the verb, and it is completely regular. Here is the whole module in one table, on one verb. You are not expected to produce these today; you are expected to see the shape.

TypeLatinEnglish
1. simple / factual
indicative in both halves		Sī Catilīna in urbe est, perīculum est.If Catiline is in the city, there is danger.
No claim about whether he is.
2. future less vivid
present subjunctive in both halves		Sī Catilīna in urbe sit, perīculum sit.If Catiline should be in the city, there would be danger.
A possibility, held at a distance.
3. contrary to fact, present
imperfect subjunctive in both halves		Sī Catilīna in urbe esset, perīculum esset.If Catiline were in the city, there would be danger.
And he is not.
4. contrary to fact, past
pluperfect subjunctive in both halves		Sī Catilīna in urbe fuisset, perīculum fuisset.If Catiline had been in the city, there would have been danger.
And he was not.
💡 Read the middle column downward and you have the module. est · sit · esset · fuisset. One verb, four tenses, four different things said about the same imaginary Catiline. Nothing is added to the sentence but the ending.
✍️ And now the thing that makes this module unlike everything since Module 7. Look at rows 2, 3 and 4 again: both halves carry the same tense. That has not been true of anything else you have learned. In a purpose clause, a cum clause, an indirect question or a fear clause, the tense of the subjunctive is chosen by sequence — you look at the indicative main verb, decide primary or secondary, and pick from the row. In a subjunctive condition there is no indicative main verb to look at: the apodosis is subjunctive too. So sequence has nothing to work on, and the rule is simply match. Lesson 15.02 says this again, because it is the single most useful sentence in the module.

★ GRAVITĀS · RESPONSIBILITY

Gravitās, gravitātis f. comes straight from gravis, grave, “heavy.” For a Roman it meant the weight a serious person carries: the habit of treating important things as important, and of not being flippant where flippancy costs other people something. The same root gives English gravity, grave, aggravate, and — borrowed whole and unchanged — gravitas.

It is the right virtue for a module about conditions, because a contrary-to-fact condition is what weighing actually sounds like. Sī hoc fēcissem, illud accidisset. “If I had done this, that would have happened.” You cannot make a decision without running sentences of that shape, and you cannot take responsibility for a decision afterwards without running them again, honestly, including the ones that do not flatter you. Cicero built his whole defence out of one such sentence — if the Senate had not acted, the republic would have fallen — and his enemies replied with another: if you had not been in such a hurry, five citizens would still be alive. Neither sentence can be checked against anything. That is precisely why the person who says one needs gravitās: the grammar will not stop you from claiming whatever you like about a past that never happened. Only you will.

Lesson 15.02 — Two Kinds of Not-Yet-True
Types 2 and 3: the future less vivid (“should…would”) with the present subjunctive in both halves, and the present contrary-to-fact (“were…would be”) with the imperfect subjunctive in both halves. One letter of difference in Latin, and a large difference in what you are claiming.
💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Two halves, two names: the protasis is the sī clause, the apodosis is the main clause, and word order does not decide which is which. Then one question: indicative or subjunctive? Indicative in both halves means the writer is making no claim about whether the supposing is true. Subjunctive in both halves means he is — and the tense tells you which claim. Est · sit · esset · fuisset.
