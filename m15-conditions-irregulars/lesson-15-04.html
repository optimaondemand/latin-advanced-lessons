Lesson 15.04
Six Verbs That Refuse to Behave
Lesson · ferō · eō · volō · nōlō · mālō · fīō
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A change of subject for one day. No conditions in this lesson at all — today is six verbs, and they are the six you will meet most often for the rest of your life in Latin.
Ferō (carry, bear), eō (go), volō (want), nōlō (not want), mālō (prefer), fīō (become, be made). None of them fits any of the four conjugations. All six have full paradigms below, in the tenses you need — and all six are vocabulary as much as morphology: they carry meanings, compounds, and English descendants that are worth as much to you as the endings. This lesson is longer than the others in the module, and that is on purpose. Take thirty minutes. Come back to it tomorrow before the reading; come back to it again before the quiz.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognise and produce the present, imperfect and future indicative, and the present and imperfect subjunctive, of all six verbs.
2
I will be able to say what fīō means and explain the odd thing about it — passive in meaning, active in form, except in the perfect system where it borrows from faciō.
3
I will be able to avoid the four traps: volō against volō, adeō against adeō, eō against eō, and the forms that are future indicative and present subjunctive at once.
📚 PART 1 · WHY THESE SIX ARE THE STRANGE ONES

Irregular verbs are not scattered at random through a language. They cluster at the top of the frequency list, and the reason is worth knowing: words that are used constantly get worn down. A speaker who says a word ten thousand times a year files the edges off it. A word used twice a year keeps its full shape, because every speaker has to build it from the rule.

English does exactly this. Go / went / gone is a mess; walk / walked / walked is tidy. Nobody planned either. The messy verb is the one everybody uses. Latin's list of irregulars is the same list of everyday actions: be, be able, carry, go, want, become. You already own sum and possum. Here are the rest.

Principal partsMeaningWhat is irregular about it
ferō, ferre, tulī, lātumto carry, bring, bear; to endure; to reportThree unrelated stems (fer-, tul-, lāt-) and some forms with no connecting vowel.
eō, īre, iī (or īvī), itumto goThe stem shrinks to a single letter in places, and swells to eu- in others.

💡 How to study this page. Do not try to memorise thirty rows of table. For each verb, learn three things: (1) the principal parts, said aloud as one phrase; (2) the 3rd singular of each tense, because that is the form you will actually meet on a page; (3) the meaning and one derivative. Everything else follows from patterns you already know, and the tables are here so you can check yourself, not so you can recite them.
📚 PART 2 · FERŌ — TO CARRY, TO BEAR, TO ENDURE

Ferō behaves like a third-conjugation verb (regō) almost everywhere. The exceptions are three present-tense forms and the imperfect subjunctive, where the connecting vowel simply is not there: fers, fert, fertis instead of the expected feris, ferit, feritis, and ferrem instead of fererem.

⚠️ The collision to know about now: feram is both the future indicative (“I shall carry”) and the present subjunctive (“let me carry / that I may carry”). This is not special to ferō — every third and fourth conjugation verb does it (regam, audiam), and you have lived with it since Module 6. The cure is the one from Lesson 13.02: read the clause, not the word. A sī clause, an ut clause, an indirect question → subjunctive. A main clause with nothing subordinating it → future indicative.

The compounds of ferō are everywhere in Latin prose, and they have the strangest principal parts in the language, because tulī and lātum get compressed:

✍️ Look at that last column. Refer and relate are the same Latin verb: one came from referre, the other from relātum. So are transfer and translate. A translator is someone who carries a text across. Nothing in English tells you that; the Latin does.
📚 PART 3 · EŌ — TO GO

⚠️ Two traps, and they are the sort that ruins a translation quietly.
1. eō the verb against eō the pronoun. Eō is also the ablative singular of is, ea, id — “by that, with that, in that place, therefore.” Eō diē is “on that day,” not “I go day.” Ask whether the sentence already has a verb.
2. adeō the verb against adeō the adverb. Adeō can be “I approach” — or the adverb “so much, to such a degree,” which you learned in Module 9 as a result-clause signpost. Adeō timēbant ut fugerent is “they were so afraid that they fled,” not “I approach they were afraid.” The clue is the same one as always: is there an ut further along, and does the sentence already have a main verb?
📚 PART 4 · VOLŌ · NŌLŌ · MĀLŌ — ONE FAMILY OF THREE

Treat these three as one verb with three moods of feeling. Volō is “I want.” Nōlō is nōn + volō: “I do not want, I refuse.” Mālō is magis + volō: “I want more, I prefer.” Learn one paradigm properly and the other two are variations on it.

Three forms of nōlō are two words. The 2nd singular, 3rd singular and 2nd plural never finished fusing: nōn vīs, nōn vult, nōn vultis. Everything else is one word beginning nōl-. If you meet nōn vult and translate it “he does not want,” you have handled nōlō correctly without needing to know that is what you did.

🎉 The most useful thing on this page: nōlī + infinitive is how Latin says “don't.” Nōlī timēre! — “Do not be afraid!” Literally: “be unwilling to fear.” Nōlīte discēdere! — “Do not leave!” This is the ordinary polite prohibition in classical Latin. (Module 14 gave you the other way, the negative jussive: nē timeās. Both are correct; nōlī + infinitive is the commoner one in prose addressed to a person in front of you.)
⚠️ The trap that catches everybody once: there are two verbs spelled volō. This one is volō, velle, voluī, “to want” — irregular. The other is volō, volāre, volāvī, “to fly” — a perfectly ordinary first-conjugation verb, and the source of English volatile. The 1st singular is identical; nothing else is. Vult is “he wants”; volat is “he flies.” If a form looks like a tidy first-conjugation ending, you are dealing with the bird.
📚 PART 5 · FĪŌ — TO BECOME, TO BE MADE, TO HAPPEN

Take this one slowly, because it is the strangest verb in Latin and the honest description of it is worth more than any memory trick.

Fīō is passive in meaning but active in form — except in the perfect system, where it borrows the perfect passive of faciō outright: factus sum. And its infinitive, fierī, is passive in form. So it is inconsistent in both directions, and you simply learn it.

Here is what that means in practice. Faciō means “I make, I do.” Its passive ought to be *facior, “I am made” — but in the present system that form does not exist. Latin uses fīō instead. So:

✍️ Two forms you have already met in English. Fīat is the present subjunctive, 3rd singular: “let it be made, let it happen.” It is the jussive from Module 14, and English borrowed it whole — a fiat is an order that something be done, and fiat lux is “let there be light.” The perfect gives us fact, from factum: a fact is a thing that has been done. The same verb, from both ends.
💡 A short list of things fīō says that nothing else says well. Quid fit? — “What is happening?” Cōnsul fit — “He becomes consul” (or “is made consul”; Latin does not distinguish, and neither did Roman politics). Fierī nōn potest — “It cannot happen; it is impossible.” That last phrase is worth memorising as a unit.
🧠 PART 6 · SIX VERBS, AND THE FOUR TRAPS

Six items. Every one of them is a form or a trap from this page, and every one of them turns up in the Ludus.

Finally, the derivative minute. These six verbs left more English behind them than any other six verbs in Latin.

🔍 SAY THE ENGLISH FIRST · THEN CLICK
ferō, ferre, tulī, lātum
how many English words? · click
offer, refer, confer, transfer, prefer, differ, suffer, infer, circumference, fertile — and from lātum: translate, relate, collate, superlative, dilatory. One verb, two stems, dozens of descendants.
eō, īre, iī, itum
going words · click
exit (exit = “he goes out”, a real Latin verb form), transit, initial, initiative, perish, obituary, sedition, ambition. Every one of them is somebody going somewhere.
volō, velle, voluī
wanting words · click
voluntary, volition, volunteer, benevolent (“well-wishing”), malevolent (“ill-wishing”). Note that volatile is not here — that belongs to the other volō, the one that flies.
nōlō, nōlle, nōluī
two Latin phrases · click
English kept the Latin whole rather than making new words: nolo contendere, a legal plea meaning “I do not wish to contest,” and nolens volens, “unwilling or willing” — whether you like it or not.
mālō, mālle, māluī
be honest · click
None. Mālō left no English descendant at all. And be careful: malice, malevolent and malcontent come from malus, “bad,” which is a different word. Not every Latin verb has children.
fīō, fierī, factus sum
from both ends · click
From the subjunctive: fiat — an order that something be done, and fiat lux, “let there be light.” From the borrowed participle factum: fact — a thing that has been done.
