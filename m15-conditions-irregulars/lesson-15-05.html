📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Cicero spent the last twenty years of his life defending one night, and he did it in the grammar you learned this week.
Today's passage is Cicero looking back: the argument he made about his consulship for the rest of his career, and the counter-argument his enemies made against him. Both sides are built out of contrary-to-fact conditions, because that is the only grammar available for arguing about a past that did not happen. Six of the ten lines contain a condition; four contain an irregular verb from Lesson 15.04. Then you review the module, sort the four types one last time, run a self-check reaching back to Module 12, and pick up your graded work.

📚 PART 1 · BEFORE YOU READ

Three things the passage assumes. You know all three from earlier modules, but they matter more here than they did then.

1. The Senate passed an emergency decree, and it was deliberately vague. In late October 63 BC the Senate instructed the consuls to see to it that the republic should suffer no dētrīmentum — no harm. It did not say what they were allowed to do. Later Romans called this kind of decree the senātūs cōnsultum ultimum, the last decree. Cicero's whole case rests on it, and so does the case against him: an instruction that names no limits protects a consul who acts, and gives him nothing to point at when asked why he went that far.
2. There was real evidence, and Cicero mentions it every time. The conspirators in Rome were caught with letters they had written to the Gallic envoys of the Allobroges, the letters were read out in the Senate, and the men confessed in front of it. You read that scene in Module 8. It is the strongest thing in Cicero's argument, because it is the one part that is not a supposition.
3. He was driven out of Italy for it. Five years later, in 58 BC, the tribune Clodius carried a law against anyone who had put a Roman citizen to death without trial. Cicero left Italy rather than face it and was recalled the following year. He never withdrew the claim that he had saved the city — and a man who keeps making an argument after it has cost him everything is either very honest or very stubborn. Probably both.
💡 Watch for the grammar doing the arguing. Count the conditions as you read: there are six, and they are not all the same type. One of them is the mixed condition Lesson 15.03 warned you about — a past protasis with a present apodosis — and it belongs to Cicero's opponents, which is not an accident. Their case is precisely that the consequences are still going on.
📜 PART 2 · THE READING
An adaptation, not a quotation. The Latin below was written for this course. It is a composite in Cicero's voice, looking back at his consulship, and every position in it is one Cicero genuinely argued in public: that the conspirators confessed and their letters were read out in the Senate; that the Senate's decree and his own speed saved the city; and that he never withdrew that claim, even after the law of 58 BC drove him out of Italy. No sentence below is a translation of anything Cicero wrote. Where a real quotation would have been better, we could not verify one that fits the grammar you have been taught, so the passage is flagged as an adaptation rather than attributed to him.

1Saepe amīcī mē rogāvērunt quid accidisset, nisi senātus tum dē salūte reī pūblicae dēcrēvisset.

2Respondeō semper eōdem modō: nisi cōnsilium cēpissēmus, rēs pūblica ēversa esset.

3Nam coniūrātī, sī impūnītī fuissent, et urbem incendissent et cīvēs occīdissent.

4Hoc nōn ex mē fingō: ipsī cōnfessī sunt, et litterae eōrum in senātū lēctae sunt.

5Sed inimīcī meī aliter loquuntur: “Nisi cōnsul tam celeriter ēgisset, quīnque cīvēs adhūc vīverent.”

6Fateor: sī quis mē rogat num umquam dubitāverim, respondeō mē dubitāvisse.

7Nam sī quis hodiē mē cōnsulem iterum faceret, idem facerem — nōn quia hoc mālō, sed quia aliter fierī nōn posset.

8Sī enim cōnsul dubitat, nihil fit; hostis autem nōn dubitat, et in urbem it.

9Haec omnia mihi magnum dētrīmentum tulērunt: nam post quīnque annōs ex Italiā abiī.

10Sī igitur mē rogās utrum rēctē ēgerim, respondeō: rem pūblicam cōnservāvī, sed hoc onus semper feram.

📖 GLOSS — EVERY WORD NOT ON YOUR RUNNING LIST, IN THE FORM IT APPEARS
  • 1 · saepe (adv.) — often · accidisset — pluperfect subjunctive of accidō, accidere, accidī: to happen · nisi — unless, if…not (15.03) · dē salūte — about the safety (ablative of salūs, salūtis f.) · dēcrēvisset — pluperfect subjunctive of dēcernō, dēcernere, dēcrēvī: to decree, vote (15.03 vocabulary)
  • 2 · eōdem modō — in the same way · cōnsilium capere — to form a plan, take a decision; here cēpissēmus, pluperfect subjunctive, 1st plural · ēversa esset — pluperfect subjunctive passive of ēvertō, ēvertere, ēvertī, ēversum: to overturn, destroy — PPP + essem, agreeing with rēs pūblica (15.03 vocabulary)
  • 3 · impūnītī — unpunished (nominative plural of impūnītus, -a, -um) · et…et — both…and · incendissent — pluperfect subjunctive of incendō, incendere, incendī: to set on fire (15.03 vocabulary) · occīdissent — pluperfect subjunctive of occīdō, occīdere, occīdī, occīsum: to kill
  • 4 · ex mē fingō — I invent out of my own head (fingō, fingere, fīnxī, fictum — to shape, invent) · ipsī — they themselves · cōnfessī sunt — they confessed (perfect of the deponent cōnfiteor, cōnfitērī, cōnfessus sum) · litterae, litterārum f. pl. — a letter, dispatch · lēctae sunt — were read out (perfect passive of legō, legere, lēgī, lēctum)
  • 5 · inimīcus, inimīcī m. — personal enemy (not hostis, the enemy of the state) · aliter (adv.) — otherwise, differently (15.03 vocabulary) · celeriter (adv.) — quickly · ēgisset — pluperfect subjunctive of agō, agere, ēgī, āctum: to act, do, drive · quīnque — five · adhūc (adv.) — still · vīverent — imperfect subjunctive of vīvō, vīvere, vīxī: to live
  • 6 · fateor — I admit (deponent fateor, fatērī, fassus sum) · sī quis — if anyone · num — whether (m11) · umquam (adv.) — ever · dubitāverim — perfect subjunctive of dubitō, dubitāre: to hesitate, doubt — primary sequence after the present rogat (m13) · mē dubitāvisse — indirect statement: “that I did hesitate”
  • 7 · hodiē (adv.) — today · iterum (adv.) — again, a second time · idem — the same thing · quia — because (m10) · mālō, mālle, māluī — to prefer, want more (15.04) · fierī — present infinitive of fīō: to happen, be done — passive in form (15.04)
  • 8 · enim — for, indeed (never first in its clause) · fit — 3rd singular of fīō: “it happens, it is done” — active in form, passive in meaning (15.04) · autem — however, moreover (never first in its clause) · it — 3rd singular present of eō, īre: “he goes” (15.04)
  • 9 · dētrīmentum, dētrīmentī n. — harm, loss (15.03 vocabulary) · tulērunt — perfect of ferō, ferre, tulī, lātum: “brought” (15.04) · post quīnque annōs — five years later · abiī — perfect of abeō, abīre, abiī: “I went away, I left” (15.04) · Italia, Italiae f. — Italy
  • 10 · igitur — therefore · utrum… — whether (m11) · rēctē (adv.) — rightly · ēgerim — perfect subjunctive of agō, primary sequence after rogās · cōnservāvī — perfect of cōnservō, cōnservāre: to preserve, keep safe (15.03 vocabulary) · onus, oneris n. — burden, load · feram — future indicative of ferō here: “I shall bear” — the main verb of its own clause, so not the subjunctive (15.04)
⚠️ Two honest notes on the Latin. First, line 5 is a mixed condition: ēgisset is pluperfect subjunctive (the past) but vīverent is imperfect subjunctive (now). That is correct Latin and it is what the speakers mean — the men are dead today. You are not asked to produce one. Second, line 10's feram is the form Lesson 15.04 warned you about: future indicative and present subjunctive are spelled alike. Here it is the main verb of its own clause with nothing subordinating it, so it is the future indicative. Read the clause, not the word.

🏛️ THE ROMAN WORLD · THE DECREE THAT NAMED NO LIMITS

Rome had no written constitution and no supreme court. When the Senate believed the state was in danger it passed a decree telling the consuls, in words close to these, to see to it that the republic suffer no harm — nē quid rēs pūblica dētrīmentī caperet. That is all it said. It named no powers, set no limits, and specified no procedure, and Romans argued for the rest of the republic's life about what it actually authorised. Cicero received such a decree in late October 63 BC. Six weeks later five citizens were dead in the Tulliānum without a trial, and the Senate that had passed the decree hailed him as the father of the fatherland, pater patriae.

Five years later the tribune Clodius carried a law against anyone who had executed a Roman citizen without trial. Nobody had to name Cicero; everybody knew. He left Italy, his house on the Palatine was pulled down, and he came back the next year to a career that never fully recovered. What is striking is what he did with the rest of it: he kept making the argument. He wrote to the historian Lucceius asking him to write the conspiracy up as a book, and admitted in the letter that he badly wanted to be praised for it. He was still defending that December in the last year of his life. Both halves of the quarrel had to be argued in contrary-to-fact conditions, because the only evidence either side could offer was a world that had not happened.

📚 PART 4 · THE WHOLE MODULE IN TWO TABLES

If you can rebuild both of these from memory, you are ready for the quiz. First, the conditions — four types, one verb, and the two negatives:

TypeMood and tenseLatinEnglish — and the hidden claim
1. simple / factualindicative in both halves (any tense, incl. future)Sī cōnsul dubitat, nihil fit.“If a consul hesitates, nothing gets done.”
No claim either way about whether he does.
2. future less vividpresent subjunctive in both halvesSī cōnsul dubitet, nihil fīat.“Should a consul hesitate, nothing would get done.”
A possibility held at a distance; not denied.
3. contrary to fact, presentimperfect subjunctive in both halvesSī cōnsul dubitāret, nihil fieret.“If a consul were hesitating, nothing would be getting done.”
He is not hesitating.
4. contrary to fact, pastpluperfect subjunctive in both halvesSī cōnsul dubitāvisset, nihil factum esset.“If the consul had hesitated, nothing would have been done.”
He did not hesitate.
the two halvesprotasis = the sī clause, which supposes · apodosis = the main clause, which states the consequence. Word order does not decide which is which.
the negativesnisi in the protasis (“unless, if…not”) · nōn in the apodosis · never nē in either. Sī nōn is for denying one particular word, usually with sed supplying the replacement.
the rule that is not sequenceIn types 2, 3 and 4 both halves take the same tense, because there is no indicative main verb for sequence to measure from. Real authors mix the types when the two halves are about different times (line 5 of the reading).

✍️ And the four traps, in one line each. Volō, velle (want) is not volō, volāre (fly) — vult against volat. Adeō the verb (“I approach”) is not adeō the adverb (“so much,” the m09 result signpost). Eō the verb (“I go”) is not eō the ablative of is, ea, id. And feram, fīam, nōlam, mālam, volam are all future indicative and present subjunctive at once — read the clause, never the word.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Line 10 ends hoc onus semper feram — “I shall bear this burden always.” It is the last thing the passage says, and it is the most honest thing in it: Cicero never claims the night was costless, only that the alternative was worse. That is what a contrary-to-fact condition is for. It is not a way of proving you were right; it is a way of saying out loud what you think you prevented, and then living with the fact that nobody can check you. Gravitās is the willingness to say it anyway, in a form clear enough to be argued with.
