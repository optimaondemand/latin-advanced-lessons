📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Module 13 gave you a tense with no obvious use. Here is what it was for.
The pluperfect subjunctive — fuisset, fēcisset, dēcrēvisset — is the form Latin uses in both halves of a condition about the past that did not happen. “If the Senate had not acted, the republic would have fallen.” This one pattern is the grammar of nearly every argument about history, every regret, and every self-defence. Today you also get nisi, “unless,” which is how Latin makes a protasis negative, and this module's vocabulary — most of which is the vocabulary of that argument.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to build a past contrary-to-fact condition with the pluperfect subjunctive in both halves, and translate it “had…would have.”
2
I will be able to make a protasis negative with nisi and an apodosis negative with nōn, and say why they are different words.
3
I will be able to use this module's twelve new words, and name the English descendants of five of them.
📚 PART 1 · TYPE 4: THE PLUPERFECT, IN BOTH HALVES
Sī + pluperfect subjunctive … ,  pluperfect subjunctive.
English: “If X had happened, Y would have happened.” — and X did not happen.

No new endings. From Lesson 13.02: perfect stem + -issem, -issēs, -isset, -issēmus, -issētis, -issent — or, if you prefer, the perfect active infinitive plus -m, -s, -t… And the good news from that lesson still holds: nothing else in the Latin language ends in -isset, so this is the one completely unambiguous form in the module.

Now put two of them in one sentence, matched:

Sī Catilīna in urbe mānsisset, Cicerō eum accūsāvisset.
“If Catiline had stayed in the city, Cicero would have accused him.” Understood: he did not stay, and so Cicero did not accuse him — he left, and was declared an enemy of the state instead.
Sī coniūrātī tacuissent, nēmō cōnsilium invēnisset.
“If the conspirators had kept quiet, no one would have discovered the plan.” Understood: they did not keep quiet. Somebody talked — which is, in fact, how the conspiracy came apart.
Sī Lentulus fūgisset, in carcere nōn mortuus esset.
“If Lentulus had fled, he would not have died in prison.” Two things to notice: mortuus esset is the deponent morior (Module 2), PPP + essem as always; and the apodosis is made negative with plain nōn. Hold on to that — Part 3 is about the other half.
🎉 The promise from Module 7 is now fully paid. Back then you were given a four-cell table with two cells greyed out and told to wait. Module 13 opened the grey cells. This lesson is the first construction in the course where the pluperfect subjunctive is not merely allowed but required — nothing else can express a past contrary to fact. Four tenses, four types of condition, one system.
📚 PART 2 · WHY ROMANS ARGUED IN THIS TENSE

A past contrary-to-fact condition is a claim about a world that does not exist. You cannot check it. There is no evidence anywhere for what would have happened, because it did not happen. And yet these sentences are not empty: some of them are well argued and some are nonsense, and telling the difference is most of what a historian does.

This is why the type sits here in the course, three lessons before you read Cicero looking back at his own consulship. Every man in that story had a sentence of this shape to offer:

The claim, in LatinWho is making it, and what it buys him
Sī senātus tardius dēcrēvisset, urbs incēnsa esset.
“If the Senate had decreed too late, the city would have been burned.”		Cicero. It turns an emergency execution into a rescue, and nobody can prove otherwise.
Nisi cōnsul tam celeriter ēgisset, quīnque cīvēs adhūc vīverent.
“If the consul had not acted so quickly, five citizens would still be alive.”		His enemies. It turns the same rescue into a killing, and nobody can prove otherwise either.
⚠️ Look carefully at the second sentence, because it is not a plain type 4. The protasis is pluperfect subjunctive (ēgisset, about the past) but the apodosis is imperfect subjunctive (vīverent, about now) — “they would still be alive,” not “they would have been.” Real Latin authors mix the types when the thought requires it, and this is the commonest mix: a past cause with a present consequence. You are not asked to produce a mixed condition on the quiz or the translation. You are asked not to be surprised by one, because Lesson 15.05's reading contains one and the honest thing is to tell you now.
💡 A reader's habit worth building. Whenever you meet a contrary-to-fact condition in an argument, ask the question the grammar cannot answer for you: how would anybody know? Sometimes there is a real answer — a letter, a troop movement, a confession. Sometimes the sentence is doing nothing but flattering the person who said it. Both kinds of sentence look exactly the same in Latin.
📚 PART 3 · NISI: HOW A PROTASIS GOES NEGATIVE
nisi = “unless” = “if…not”
It is one word doing the work of two: nī (an old negative) + sī.

The negatives in a conditional sentence divide up neatly, and this is the whole rule:

Which halfNegative wordExample
protasis — the sī halfnisi
replaces sī altogether		Nisi Cicerō vigilāvisset, coniūrātī urbem incendissent.
“If Cicero had not been watchful, the conspirators would have set the city on fire.”
apodosis — the main halfnōn
the ordinary negative		Sī Lentulus fūgisset, in carcere nōn mortuus esset.
“If Lentulus had fled, he would not have died in prison.”
✍️ Not nē. By now nē has done four jobs for you — negative purpose (m08), indirect command (m08), fear clause (m12), and negative jussive or wish (m14). It does not do this one. A conditional protasis never uses nē. If you see nē, you are not in a condition; if you are in a condition and want a negative, you want nisi.

Nisi works with every condition type, indicative included:

Nisi hoc crēdis, errās.  ·  Nisi fallor, Catilīna iam abest.
“Unless you believe this, you are wrong.” · “Unless I am mistaken, Catiline is already gone.” Both indicative, both plain factual conditions. The second is an everyday Latin phrase and a good one to own. Gloss: fallō, fallere, fefellī, falsum — to deceive; fallor — I am mistaken; absum, abesse, āfuī — to be away, be absent.

Nisi or sī nōn?

Latin has both, and they are not quite the same. The useful rule of thumb: nisi sets aside the whole supposition — “except in the case that…” — while sī nōn denies one particular word in it, usually because something else is being put in that word's place.

Nisi Cicerō cōnsul fuisset, rēs pūblica cecidisset.
“Unless Cicero had been consul, the republic would have fallen.” The whole supposition is set aside: take away the Cicero-being-consul case and disaster follows.
Sī Catilīna nōn Rōmae, sed in Etrūriā fuisset, aliter ēgissēmus.
“If Catiline had been not at Rome but in Etruria, we would have acted differently.” Here nōn is attached to one word, Rōmae, and sed supplies the replacement. Nisi would be wrong: nothing is being excepted.
💡 For your own writing, keep it simple: if the English says unless, or says if…not about the whole idea, write nisi. That will be right nearly every time, and the second pattern above is something to recognise rather than something you need to produce.

📚 PART 5 · THIS MODULE'S VOCABULARY

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

-isset…-isset is a past that did not happen. That is the whole of type 4, and the pluperfect subjunctive is unmistakable, so this is the easiest of the four types to recognise on the page and the hardest to argue about honestly. Negatives: nisi in the protasis, nōn in the apodosis, and never nē in either.
