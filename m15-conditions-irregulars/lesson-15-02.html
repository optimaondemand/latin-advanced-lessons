Lesson 15.02
Two Kinds of Not-Yet-True
Lesson · future less vivid and present contrary to fact
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two of the four types today, and one rule that replaces everything you have been doing since Module 7.
Type 2, the future less vivid, holds a possibility at arm's length: “if this should happen, that would happen.” Type 3, the present contrary to fact, denies the possibility outright: “if this were so — and it is not — that would be so.” Latin builds the first with the present subjunctive in both halves and the second with the imperfect subjunctive in both halves. No new endings. You have owned both tenses since Module 6. What is new is where they go, and the fact that sequence of tenses does not apply here at all.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state the matching rule — both halves take the same tense — and explain why sequence of tenses has nothing to do here.
2
I will be able to build a future less vivid condition with the present subjunctive and translate it with “should…would.”
3
I will be able to build a present contrary-to-fact condition with the imperfect subjunctive and say what fact it is denying.
📚 PART 1 · THE RULE THAT REPLACES SEQUENCE

Every subjunctive you have met since Module 7 has had its tense chosen for it by sequence. The procedure was always the same: find the indicative main verb, decide whether it is primary or secondary, and read the tense off the two-row table.

ConstructionMain verbHow the subjunctive's tense was chosen
purpose · result · cum · indirect question · fear (m08–m13)indicative — rogat, rogāvit, timēbatby sequence: primary → present or perfect subj.; secondary → imperfect or pluperfect subj.
conditions with the subjunctive (m15)subjunctive too — there is no indicative main verbby matching: the apodosis takes the same tense as the protasis
In a subjunctive condition, both halves carry the same tense of the subjunctive. Present with present. Imperfect with imperfect. Pluperfect with pluperfect.
💡 And here is why, which is worth thirty seconds of thought. Sequence of tenses is a rule about how a subordinate clause positions itself in time relative to a main clause that is stating a fact. A conditional sentence of types 2, 3 and 4 has no such main clause: the apodosis is in the subjunctive as well, because it too is unreal. The two halves are unreal together, in the same imaginary world, at the same imaginary time. So there is nothing for sequence to measure from, and Latin does the obvious thing instead — it uses one tense for the whole sentence.
⚠️ Do not throw the sequence rule away. It is still in force everywhere else, and Module 16 will test it. What is more, a condition can sit inside a construction that does use sequence — and then both rules apply at once, each to its own clause. You are not replacing a rule; you are learning where its territory stops.
📚 PART 2 · TYPE 2: THE FUTURE LESS VIVID (“SHOULD…WOULD”)
Sī + present subjunctive … ,  present subjunctive.
English: “If X should happen, Y would happen.”

Compare the two future conditions side by side. The Latin difference is one mood; the difference in what the speaker is doing is large.

TYPE 1 · FUTURE, INDICATIVE
Sī Catilīna in urbem veniet, cīvēs arma capient.
“If Catiline comes into the city, the citizens will take up arms.”
A live question. The speaker expects it to be settled one way or the other, quite soon, and is telling you what happens then.
TYPE 2 · FUTURE LESS VIVID, PRESENT SUBJUNCTIVE
Sī Catilīna in urbem veniat, cīvēs arma capiant.
“If Catiline should come into the city, the citizens would take up arms.”
A supposing. The speaker is imagining the case rather than expecting it — and is not saying it will not happen either.

The name is worth unpacking, because it explains itself. Both are future conditions. Type 1 is vivid — it puts the scene in front of you as a thing that may well happen. Type 2 is less vivid — the same scene, held further off, sketched rather than painted. Some books call it the “ideal” condition for the same reason.

No new forms are needed. This is the present subjunctive from Module 6, in both halves:

Sī hoc dīcās, omnēs mīrentur.
“If you should say this, everyone would be astonished.” Both present subjunctive; mīrentur is the deponent mīror from Module 2, passive in form and active in meaning as always. Gloss: mīror, mīrārī, mīrātus sum — to wonder at, be astonished.
Sī senātus taceat, rēs pūblica cadat.
“If the Senate should keep silent, the republic would fall.” Watch the shape: taceat and cadat, matched. Gloss: cadō, cadere, cecidī — to fall; rēs pūblica, reī pūblicae f. — the republic, the state, public affairs.
📚 PART 3 · TYPE 3: PRESENT CONTRARY TO FACT (“WERE…WOULD BE”)
Sī + imperfect subjunctive … ,  imperfect subjunctive.
English: “If X were so, Y would be so.” — and X is not so.

The phrase contrary to fact means exactly what it says: the sentence runs against the facts, and the writer knows it and expects you to know it. This is the type that carries a hidden second claim. When a Roman writes sī Catilīna in urbe esset, he has told you two things at once — a supposing, and a denial.

Sī Catilīna in urbe esset, cīvēs timērent.
“If Catiline were in the city, the citizens would be afraid.” Understood: he is not in the city, and they are not afraid. Both verbs imperfect subjunctive, matched.
Sī cōnsul tacēret, coniūrātī vincerent.
“If the consul were keeping silent, the conspirators would be winning.” Understood: the consul is not keeping silent, so they are not winning. Notice how much argument is packed into two endings: the sentence is a compliment to Cicero disguised as a supposition. Gloss: vincō, vincere, vīcī — to conquer, win.

The forms are the imperfect subjunctive from Lesson 6.03 — present infinitive + -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt. Set the two subjunctive tenses of this lesson side by side and learn them as a pair:

⚠️ An honest warning about the English word “would.” English uses would for at least three unrelated jobs: the apodosis of a condition (“he would be afraid”), habitual past action (“every morning he would walk to the Forum”), and future-in-the-past (“he said he would come”). Only the first is a conditional apodosis. So do not translate into Latin from the English word would alone — read the whole sentence and find out whether there is an “if” anywhere. Two of the three “woulds” above need no subjunctive in Latin at all.
✍️ Negatives are tomorrow. Every example on this page is positive on purpose. Latin has a special word for a negative protasis — nisi, “unless, if…not” — and it arrives in Lesson 15.03 alongside the last and most important condition type.

📚 PART 5 · SAY THE PAIR

Type 2 and type 3 differ by one tense, so drill them as a pair rather than one at a time. Say both aloud before you click.

🔍 SAY IT FIRST · THEN CLICK TO CHECK
sum — 3 sg. pair
present subj. · imperfect subj.
sit · esset. The most common pair in the whole module, because most contrary-to-fact conditions are about how things are.
possum — 3 sg. pair
present subj. · imperfect subj.
possit · posset. One letter apart. In a condition the difference is “would be able” (possible) versus “would be able” (but is not).
veniō — 3 sg. pair
present subj. · imperfect subj.
veniat · venīret. Sī veniat = “should he come”; sī venīret = “if he were coming — and he is not.”
faciō — 3 sg. pair
present subj. · imperfect subj.
faciat · faceret. Watch the vowel: facia- against face-. This is the pair students most often blur.
loquor — 3 sg. pair
deponent · m02
loquātur · loquerētur. Passive in form, active in meaning, and it behaves in a condition exactly like any other verb.
the matching rule
say it out loud · click
Both halves take the same tense. No sequence, no primary or secondary, nothing to look up. Sit…sit. Esset…esset.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Match the halves. Sit…sit is “should…would” — a possibility held at arm's length. Esset…esset is “were…would be” — and it is not so. If you catch yourself reaching for the two-row sequence table in a conditional sentence, stop: there is no indicative main verb to measure from, and the answer is simply the tense already sitting in the other half.
