📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The commonest cum in Latin narrative — and no new endings to learn.
Temporal cum is the one you will meet most often, because it is how historians hold a scene still. It uses the subjunctive you built in Module 6, in the tense Module 7's sequence rule already tells you to use. Nothing new is being manufactured today. What is new is a use, not a form.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to build a temporal cum clause in any conjugation, active, passive, or deponent, using the sequence rule to choose the tense.
2
I will be able to say what cum + indicative does that cum + subjunctive does not.
3
I will be able to state honestly which two tenses of this construction are not mine yet, and what they are for.

Imperfect subjunctive — how?
Module 6 · click
Present infinitive + -m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt. pugnāre → pugnārem · tacēre → tacērem · dīcere → dīcerem · capere → caperem · audīre → audīrem. And esse → essem.
Present subjunctive — how?
Module 6 · click
1st conjugation takes -e-: amem, amēs, amet. Everything else takes -a-: moneam, regam, capiam, audiam. And esse → sim, sīs, sit.
The sequence rule
Module 7 · click
Main verb present or future → present subjunctive. Main verb imperfect, perfect, or pluperfect → imperfect subjunctive. Two rows, and they cover everything you have been given so far.
Passive and deponent
Modules 2 and 6 · click
Passive imperfect subj.: dūcerētur (“he was being led”), audīrētur. Deponent imperfect subj.: loquerētur (“he was speaking”), sequerētur. Same endings, and a deponent still means something active.
📚 PART 2 · TEMPORAL CUM: HOLDING A SCENE STILL

Temporal cum + subjunctive translates as “when” — and sometimes, where the sense fits, “while” or “after.” But it does more than name a time. It describes the circumstances the main action happened inside. Older grammar books call it the cum circumstantial, and that name is worth keeping in your head because it explains what the subjunctive is doing there.

The subjunctive here is not doubt and not a wish. It marks the clause as background — scenery rather than news. The indicative would report a fact; the subjunctive sets a stage.

Here it is across the conjugations. Every main verb below is past, so every subjunctive is imperfect — that is Module 7's second row doing its job.

ConjugationLatinEnglish
1stCum cīvēs in viīs stārent, Cicerō tacēbat.When the citizens were standing in the streets, Cicero was silent.
2ndCum omnēs tacērent, cōnsul surrēxit.When everyone was silent, the consul stood up.
3rdCum Cicerō haec dīceret, senātōrēs eum audiēbant.When Cicero was saying these things, the senators were listening to him.
3rd io-stemCum praetōrēs Lentulum caperent, populus clāmāvit.When the praetors were seizing Lentulus, the people shouted.
4thCum vesper venīret, turba in forō convēnit.When evening was coming on, the crowd gathered in the Forum.
esseCum Cicerō cōnsul esset, coniūrātiōnem superāvit.When Cicero was consul, he defeated the conspiracy.
passiveCum Lentulus in carcerem dūcerētur, nēmō clāmāvit.When Lentulus was being led into the prison, nobody shouted.
deponentCum cōnsul ad populum loquerētur, omnēs tacēbant.When the consul was speaking to the people, everyone was silent.
📖 GLOSS FOR THE TABLE
  • stō, stāre, stetī — to stand (imperf. subj. stārem, stārēs, stāret…)
  • taceō, tacēre, tacuī — to be silent, say nothing
  • praetor, praetōris m. — praetor (the magistrate ranked just below consul; the praetors carried out the Senate's order)
  • vesper, vesperī m. — evening
  • turba, turbae f. — crowd
  • conveniō, convenīre, convēnī, conventum — to come together, assemble
  • superō, superāre, superāvī, superātum — to overcome, defeat
💡 Where does the cum clause sit? Overwhelmingly first, before the main clause. That is not a rule you have to obey, but it is a habit worth copying: a Roman reader expected the circumstances to be laid out before the news arrived. If you put your cum clause first when you write Latin this week, your sentences will read like Latin instead of like translated English.
📚 PART 3 · AND WHEN CUM TAKES THE INDICATIVE

Sometimes an author does not want to paint a circumstance. He just wants to say when. Then cum takes the indicative, and the meaning shrinks to pure time.

UseLatinEnglish
Dating a single event
perfect indicative		Cum vesper vēnit, praetōrēs coniūrātōs dūxērunt.When evening came, the praetors led the conspirators away.
“Whenever” — a repeated or general truth
present indicative		Cum Cicerō loquitur, omnēs audiunt.Whenever Cicero speaks, everyone listens.
Present or future time
present or future indicative		Cum in Tullīānum dēscendis, tenebrae undique sunt.When you go down into the Tullianum, there is darkness on every side.
✍️ The working rule, stated as honestly as it can be. Cum + indicative is almost always plain “when” — you are safe translating it that way and moving on. Cum + subjunctive is one of the three, and you have to decide. That single split does most of the work of this module, and it costs you nothing but a glance at the verb's mood.
⚠️ Do not flip this round. Students who half-remember this rule end up thinking “subjunctive means when, indicative means although,” which is backwards and produces nonsense. The subjunctive is the rich one — three possible jobs. The indicative is the thin one — one job, done plainly.
📚 PART 4 · WHAT THIS CONSTRUCTION HAS THAT YOU DO NOT YET

You met the four-cell sequence table in Lesson 7.01, and you were told that half of it belongs to Module 13. That half matters here more than anywhere else so far, so here is the straight version.

⚠️ A cum clause has four subjunctive tenses available. You own two of them. The two you have — present and imperfect — describe something going on at the same time as the main verb. The two you do not have yet — perfect and pluperfect subjunctive — describe something already finished before the main verb happened. In real Sallust, “when Cicero had spoken” would use a pluperfect subjunctive, and that form is built in Module 13. This module works entirely with same-time cum clauses, and where a reading genuinely needs a completed prior action, it uses cum + indicative instead, which is fully in your grammar. Nothing in this module's quiz or translation will ask you for a perfect or pluperfect subjunctive, and you should not offer one.

📖 MODULE 10 VOCABULARY

The signal words this module lives on, plus the words the reading on Friday will need. Add all of these to your running list.

  • cum (conj.) — + subjunctive: when, since, although  ·  + indicative: when, whenever (and still the preposition “with” + ablative)
  • tamen (adv.) — nevertheless, all the same, still (the concessive signal — learn this one cold)
  • quamquam (conj.) — although (+ indicative — the plain-statement alternative to concessive cum)
  • quia (conj.) — because (+ indicative — the plain-statement alternative to causal cum)
  • iam (adv.) — already, by now, now
  • vix (adv.) — scarcely, barely, hardly
  • nōndum (adv.) — not yet
  • apertē (adv.) — openly, plainly
  • cēnseō, cēnsēre, cēnsuī, cēnsum — to judge, be of the opinion; in the Senate, to move or propose
  • plaudō, plaudere, plausī, plausum — to applaud, clap
  • taceō, tacēre, tacuī — to be silent, say nothing
  • stō, stāre, stetī, statum — to stand (review)
  • ēgredior, ēgredī, ēgressus sum — to go out, step out (deponent, like prōgredior)
  • incipiō, incipere, incēpī, inceptum — to begin (in- + capiō)
  • turba, turbae f. — crowd, throng
  • vesper, vesperī m. — evening
  • tenebrae, tenebrārum f. pl. — darkness (plural in form, singular in sense)
  • mōs, mōris m. — custom, practice; in the plural, mōrēs — character, way of life
  • carcer, carceris m. — prison (review from Module 9)
📜 DERIVATIVE MINUTE · CLICK EACH CARD
cēnseō
what English words?
census, censor, censure. The Roman cēnsor ran the cēnsus and could give his opinion — cēnsēre — on who stayed on the citizen rolls. To censure someone is still to pass judgment on them.
plaudō
what English words?
applaud, applause, plaudit — and explode. Ex-plaudere was to clap a bad actor off the stage; the sense of bursting outward came later.
carcer
what English word?
incarcerate. Literally “to put into the carcer.” The building itself still stands beside the Forum.
mōs, mōris
what English words?
moral, morale, mores. Roman mōrēs were not rules written down but the customs a people actually lived by — which is why mōs maiōrum, “the custom of the ancestors,” could be used as an argument in the Senate.
tenebrae
what English word?
tenebrous — dark, shadowy, gloomy. A rare word in English, but you now know exactly what it means, and where Sallust used it.
incipiō
what English words?
inception, incipient. An incipient problem is one that has just begun. The inception of something is its taking-hold.

Lesson 10.03 — Since and Although
The other two jobs of the same construction: causal and concessive. The tamen tell, how to negate a cum clause, and the two indicative cousins — quia and quamquam — that do the same work without a subjunctive.
💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Temporal cum + subjunctive does not just say when — it paints the circumstances the main action happened inside, which is why it takes the subjunctive at all. No new endings: past main verb → imperfect subjunctive, every time, in every conjugation. And cum + indicative is the thin version: plain “when,” nothing more.
