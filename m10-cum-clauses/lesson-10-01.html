📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You learned cum in your first year of Latin. You did not learn most of it.
Since 0706000 cum has meant “with,” a preposition with the ablative: cum senātōribus, “with the senators.” That word is still there and still means that. But Latin has a second cum — a conjunction — and it does three different jobs: “when,” “since,” and “although.” Four jobs, one spelling, and this week you learn to tell them apart.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell cum the preposition from cum the conjunction in one look.
2
I will be able to name the three meanings of cum + subjunctive and the one meaning of cum + indicative.
3
I will be able to explain why the form of the verb cannot, by itself, tell me which of the three meanings is in front of me.
📚 PART 1 · THE ONE YOU ALREADY OWN

Start with what is safe. Cum the preposition means “with” and takes the ablative. You have used it for two years:

Cicerō cum senātōribus in Cūriam vēnit.
“Cicero came into the Senate House with the senators.” Gloss: Cūria, Cūriae f. — the Senate House.

And you know its odd habit of climbing onto the back of a pronoun: mēcum (“with me”), tēcum, nōbīscum, sēcum. Same word, glued on.

The test that never fails: the preposition cum governs a noun or pronoun in the ablative and has no verb of its own. The conjunction cum opens a whole clause — and a clause has a verb.
💡 So the first question is not “what does cum mean here?” It is “is there a verb in this group of words?” That question you can answer without understanding the sentence at all — which, as Module 9 taught you with ut nōn, is exactly the kind of question worth asking first.

📚 PART 2 · THE CONJUNCTION, AND ITS FOUR OUTCOMES

Once you have found a verb inside the cum group, you are dealing with the conjunction. Now look at the mood of that verb. It splits the possibilities in two:

What follows cumHow many meaningsWhat it means
INDICATIVE
vēnit, loquitur, dīxit		almost always one“when” — plain time, nothing more
and with a present tense, often “whenever”
SUBJUNCTIVE
venīret, loquerētur, esset		three“when / after”  ·  “since / because”  ·  “although”
and you decide which from the sense

Here are all four, one line each. Read the English before you read the label.

Cum vesper vēnit, praetōrēs coniūrātōs ex aedibus dūxērunt.
“When evening came, the praetors led the conspirators out of the houses.”
Indicative (vēnit). Pure time: it tells you the hour and nothing else.
Cum cīvēs in viīs stārent, Cicerō tacēbat.
“When the citizens were standing in the streets, Cicero was silent.”
Subjunctive — temporal. The clause paints the circumstances the main action sat inside.
Cum nēmō respondēret, Cicerō iterum rogāvit.
“Since no one was answering, Cicero asked again.”
Subjunctive — causal. The silence is not just background; it is the reason he asked a second time.
Cum perīculum magnum esset, tamen Catō surrēxit.
“Although the danger was great, Cato nevertheless stood up.”
Subjunctive — concessive. The clause states something that should have stopped the main action, and did not.
✍️ Notice the shape of each relationship. Temporal: the two events sit side by side. Causal: the second flows out of the first. Concessive: the second pushes against the first. Three relationships, and Latin marks all three with the same four letters. What it gives you instead is the little word tamen.
📚 PART 3 · THE ONE MECHANICAL HANDLE: TAMEN

Module 9 gave you a mechanical test: read the negative word off the page and you are done. This module gives you exactly one test of that kind, and it is worth memorising today:

Tamen in the main clause → the cum clause is concessive: “although.”
Tamen means “nevertheless, all the same, still.” A writer who puts it in the main clause is telling you out loud that what came before should have led somewhere else.
⚠️ Be honest about how much this test does. Tamen is a strong signal toward concessive, but it is not a law: not every concessive clause bothers to include it, and once in a while tamen is doing other work in the sentence. Treat it as your best single clue, not as a guarantee. Where it is absent, you decide from the sense — and Lesson 10.04 will give you a procedure for doing that.

And the part nobody should pretend about

There is no ending that says “causal” and no ending that says “temporal.” Cum senātōrēs tacērent is exactly the same Latin whether it means “when the senators were silent” or “since the senators were silent.” The form tells you the mood and the tense. The sense tells you the job.

💡 Which means — sometimes two readings are both good. Cum Cicerō cōnsul esset, coniūrātiōnem superāvit can be read “When Cicero was consul, he defeated the conspiracy” or “Since Cicero was consul, he defeated the conspiracy.” Both are defensible; good translators disagree about sentences like this one. Your job is not to find a certainty the language does not offer — it is to name what is decidable and translate the rest sensibly.

★ CŌMITĀS · COURTESY

Cōmitās, cōmitātis f. is courtesy — graciousness, human warmth, the quality of being good to be around without giving up your dignity. It comes from comes, comitis, “companion,” literally “one who goes with.” The same root gives English comity and, by a long road through medieval Latin, the title count.

And it is the right virtue for this week, because cum clauses are Latin's way of saying that no action stands alone. A cum clause never carries the main news of a sentence. Its whole job is to hold up the circumstances that the main clause happens inside — the going-with of one event beside another. Courage is only courage when there is danger. Courtesy is only courtesy although you had the power to be rude. Circumstance is not decoration around a virtue; it is what makes the virtue mean anything. Latin built a whole construction to keep that in view.

Ask two questions in order. Is there a verb in the cum group? No → preposition, “with” + ablative. Yes → conjunction; now, what mood is that verb? Indicative → plain “when.” Subjunctive → one of three, and tamen is your best clue.
