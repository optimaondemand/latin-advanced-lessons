📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Module 9 ended with the Senate voting. Today you read what happened before that night was over.
Cato's motion carried. Within hours, five Roman citizens were dead in a cell beside the Forum, and Sallust wrote the coldest paragraph in his book about it. You will read it adapted — ten lines carrying almost every use of cum you have learned this week, including one line with the conjunction and the preposition side by side. Then you review the module and pick up your graded work.

📚 PART 1 · BEFORE YOU READ

Three things to hold in your head, because the passage assumes all three and explains none of them.

1. The vote had just happened. That is the debate you read in Module 9: Caesar arguing for imprisonment, Cato for death. Cato won. Sallust's next sentence is the one you are about to read, and it moves without a pause from a vote to an execution.
2. Lentulus was a serving magistrate. Publius Cornēlius Lentulus Sūra was praetor in 63 BC — and had been consul years earlier, in 71. Rome did not execute a magistrate in office. So the first thing that had to happen was a resignation. You met that sentence in 10.04.
3. The prison was two rooms. The carcer beside the Forum had an upper chamber and, below it, a cell called the Tulliānum, sunk into the rock. Rome did not use prisons for long sentences; the Tulliānum was where sentences were carried out. Sallust stops the narrative to describe it, which is the one moment in the chapter where his voice shows.
💡 Watch for the grammar doing the work. Count the cums as you read — there are five, and they are not all doing the same job. One of them is the preposition. One line has both kinds in it. Sallust did not arrange this for your benefit; this is simply what Latin prose looks like, which is why the four-step procedure was worth learning.
📜 PART 2 · THE READING
Adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 55. The sentences have been shortened and simplified to the grammar you have been taught; the order of events, the names, and the description of the Tulliānum are Sallust's. This is an adaptation, not Sallust's own Latin.

1Cum senātōrēs Catōnī assentīrentur, Cicerō diūtius nōn dubitāvit.

2Nox iam appropinquābat; itaque cōnsul trēsvirōs supplicium parāre iussit, nē quid eā nocte novārētur.

3Cum Lentulus praetor esset, prīmum magistrātū sē abdicāre dēbuit.

4Tum Cicerō ipse eum cum praesidiō per Viam Sacram in carcerem dēdūxit; idem per praetōrēs cēterīs factum est.

5Est in carcere locus quī Tulliānum appellātur, circiter duodecim pedēs humī dēpressus.

6Tam foedus est ille locus ut nēmō eum sine horrōre aspiciat.

7Cum Lentulus in eum locum dēmitterētur, vindicēs laqueō gulam frēgērunt.

8Ita ille patricius, cum ex clārissimā gente Cornēliōrum orīrētur et cōnsul quondam esset, tamen exitium mōribus factīsque suīs dignum invēnit.

9Dē Cethēgō, Statiliō, Gabiniō, Caepāriō eōdem modō supplicium sūmptum est.

10Sed cum Catilīna ipse cum exercitū in Etrūriā adhūc esset, bellum nōndum cōnfectum erat.

📖 GLOSS — EVERY WORD NOT ON YOUR RUNNING LIST, IN THE FORM IT APPEARS
  • 1 · assentīrentur — imperfect subjunctive of assentior, assentīrī, assēnsus sum (+ dative), a deponent: to agree with · diūtius — any longer · dubitō, dubitāre — to hesitate
  • 2 · appropinquābat — was drawing near (appropinquō, appropinquāre) · itaque — and so, therefore · trēsvirōs — the board of three officials who carried out sentences (accusative plural) · supplicium, suppliciī n. — punishment; the death penalty · iussit + accusative + infinitive — ordered (someone) to (do something) · nē quid novārētur — so that nothing should be changed — that is, so that no rescue attempt could alter the situation (quid after nē = anything)
  • 3 · praetor, praetōris m. — praetor, a senior elected magistrate · prīmum (adv.) — first · magistrātū — from his public office (ablative of magistrātus, magistrātūs m.) · sē abdicāre (+ ablative) — to resign from
  • 4 · praesidiō — with an armed escort (ablative of praesidium, praesidiī n.) · Via Sacra — the Sacred Way, the road through the Forum · dēdūxit — led down, escorted (dēdūcō, dēdūcere, dēdūxī) · idem — the same thing · cēterīs — for the rest, to the others (dative plural of cēterī)
  • 5 · Tulliānum, Tulliānī n. — the Tullianum, the lower cell of the Roman prison · appellātur — is called (present passive of appellō, appellāre) · circiter (adv.) — about, roughly · duodecim pedēs — twelve feet (accusative measuring how far) · humī — into the ground · dēpressus — sunk, pressed down (dēprimō, dēprimere, dēpressī, dēpressum)
  • 6 · foedus, -a, -um — foul, loathsome · sine (+ ablative) — without · horrōre — shuddering, horror (ablative of horror, horrōris m.) · aspiciat — present subjunctive of aspiciō, aspicere: to look at
  • 7 · dēmitterētur — was being let down (imperfect subjunctive passive of dēmittō, dēmittere) · vindicēs — the officers who carried out capital sentences (nominative plural of vindex, vindicis m.) · laqueō — with a noose (ablative of laqueus, laqueī m.) · gulam — throat (accusative of gula, gulae f.) · frēgērunt — broke (frangō, frangere, frēgī, frāctum)
  • 8 · patricius, patriciī m. — patrician, a man of the old nobility · gente — family, clan (ablative of gēns, gentis f.) · Cornēliōrum — of the Cornelii, Lentulus's family · orīrētur — imperfect subjunctive of orior, orīrī, ortus sum, a deponent: to rise, be descended from · quondam (adv.) — once, formerly · exitium, exitiī n. — end, destruction · mōribus factīsque suīs dignum — worthy of his own character and deeds (dignus, -a, -um takes the ablative)
  • 9 · eōdem modō — in the same way · supplicium sūmptum est dē (+ ablative) — the death penalty was carried out on
  • 10 · exercitū — with an army (ablative of exercitus, exercitūs m.) · Etrūria, Etrūriae f. — Etruria, the region north of Rome · adhūc (adv.) — still · cōnfectum erat — had been finished (cōnficiō, cōnficere, cōnfēcī, cōnfectum)
⚠️ One honest note about line 8. Sallust's own sentence here uses a pluperfect tense you will not meet until Module 13, and it is one of the most quoted sentences in the Bellum Catilīnae. The adaptation keeps his judgment — that Lentulus got the end he deserved — and puts it into a concessive cum clause you can read today. When you reach Module 13 you will be able to read the line as he wrote it.

🏛️ THE ROMAN WORLD · FIVE MEN AND NO TRIAL

The Tulliānum is still there. You can look down into it beside the Forum: a stone room perhaps twelve feet below street level, and Sallust says its darkness and its smell made it terrible to look at. On the evening of 5 December 63 BC, five Roman citizens were taken down into it and strangled — Lentulus, Cethēgus, Statilius, Gabinius, Caepārius. Plutarch, writing much later, says that when Cicero came back up he told the waiting crowd one word: vīxērunt. “They have lived.” Meaning: they are finished.

That evening was legal, and it was also a problem. The men had confessed before the Senate, and the Senate had voted — but the Senate was not a court, and a Roman citizen had the right to appeal to the people before a capital sentence. Cicero had used an emergency decree to skip that. For a few weeks Rome called him pater patriae, father of the fatherland. Five years later a tribune named Clōdius passed a law against anyone who had executed citizens without trial, and Cicero left Italy in exile. He never entirely stopped defending that night, which is usually a sign that a man knows the case against him is not empty.

clause with a subjunctive verb, giving the reason

✍️ And the negatives, from 10.03: a cum clause is made negative with nōn inside the clause — cum nōn respondēret, “since he was not answering.” It does not use nē, and it does not use ut nōn. Those belong to purpose and result, which is the m08–m09 pair, not this one.

Module 11 — Indirect Questions
“I ask who did this” — and the verb goes subjunctive. A fourth construction joins purpose, result and cum, and this time the signal is a question word: quis, quid, cūr, ubi, quandō, quōmodo. The reading turns back to Cicero, for the fourth and last speech against Catiline.
💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Line 10 of the reading has both cums in it, four words apart, and you can now tell them apart in about a second. That is what a week on one small word buys you: not a fact you have memorised, but a question you ask automatically. Verb? Mood? Tamen? Reason?
