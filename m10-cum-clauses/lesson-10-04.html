📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
For three days the sentences have arrived with labels on them. Today they arrive with nothing.
Lesson 10.01 sorted preposition from conjunction. 10.02 taught temporal cum. 10.03 taught causal and concessive, plus the indicative cousins quia and quamquam. Every drill so far told you in advance which one you were looking at. Real Latin does not. So today you get a four-step procedure you can run on any cum in any sentence you will ever meet, an unlabelled pile to run it on — and an honest account of the sentences where two answers are both right and the procedure has to stop.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to run the four-step cum procedure in order, out loud, without looking it up.
2
I will be able to sort unlabelled cum sentences into preposition, plain “when,” causal, and concessive.
3
I will be able to recognise a genuinely ambiguous cum clause and say what my translation commits me to.
📚 PART 1 · THE FOUR-STEP PROCEDURE

Run these in order. Each step either finishes the job or hands you to the next one. Notice that the first two steps need no understanding of the sentence at all — they are pure form. Only steps 3 and 4 ask you to think about meaning, and by then you have narrowed four possibilities to two.

1
Is there a verb inside the cum group?
No → it is the preposition: “with” + ablative. Stop.
Yes → it is the conjunction. Go to step 2.
2
What mood is that verb?
Indicative → plain “when” (or “whenever,” if the tense is present). Pure time, no extra claim. Stop.
Subjunctive → one of three. Go to step 3.
3
Is tamen in the main clause?
Yes → concessive: “although.” Stop.
No → go to step 4. (Remember from 10.03: a concessive clause does not have to carry tamen. Its absence rules nothing out; its presence nearly settles it.)
4
Does the cum clause give the reason the main clause happened? Test it by putting “since” in front and asking whether the sentence still makes sense as an argument.
Yes → causal: “since, because.”
No → temporal: “when, after.” The clause is setting the scene, not explaining it.
✍️ Why the order matters. Students who go straight to “what does it mean?” guess. Students who ask “is there a verb?” and then “what mood?” have eliminated half the possibilities before meaning enters the picture — and they have done it with two questions they cannot get wrong. Keep the cheap questions first.
📚 PART 2 · THREE WORKED WALK-THROUGHS

Read each one and watch the four steps run. Say the steps to yourself; the point is the habit, not the answers.

Cum praetōrēs cum mīlitibus vēnissent…
⚠️ Trap sentence — and not one you can finish yet. Vēnissent is a pluperfect subjunctive, which is Module 13. Set it aside; what matters here is the other half.
Step 1, twice. There are two cums in this line. The first has a verb in its group (vēnissent) → conjunction. The second, cum mīlitibus, has no verb of its own and a plain ablative plural → preposition, “with the soldiers.” One sentence, both cums, and step 1 separated them without your knowing what the sentence says.
Cum cōnsul haec cognōvit, statim ad senātum vēnit.
1. Verb in the group? Yes — cognōvit. Conjunction.  2. Mood? Cognōvit is perfect indicative (the subjunctive would be cognōsceret). → Plain “when.” Stop — steps 3 and 4 never run.
“When the consul learned these things, he came at once to the Senate.” Gloss: cognōscō, cognōscere, cognōvī — to learn, find out; statim — at once.
Cum tenebrae iam venīrent, tamen cōnsul nōn dubitābat.
1. Verb? Venīrent. Conjunction.  2. Mood? Imperfect subjunctive (venīre + -nt). Three possibilities live.  3. Tamen in the main clause? Yes. → Concessive. Stop.
“Although darkness was already coming on, the consul nevertheless was not hesitating.” Gloss: dubitō, dubitāre — to hesitate, doubt.
Cum Lentulus praetor esset, prīmum magistrātū sē abdicāre dēbuit.
1. Verb? Esset. Conjunction.  2. Mood? Imperfect subjunctive.  3. Tamen? No. Go on.  4. Does the clause give the reason? Put “since” in front: since Lentulus was a praetor, he first had to resign his office. Yes — being praetor is exactly why the resignation was required. → Causal.
Gloss: magistrātus, magistrātūs m. — public office; sē abdicāre (+ ablative) — to resign from; prīmum (adv.) — first.
💡 That last one is real history, and you will read it on Friday. Lentulus was a serving praetor in December 63 BC. Rome could not execute a magistrate, so before anything else could happen he was made to resign the office. The Latin sentence and the constitutional problem have the same shape: the cum clause is not scene-setting, it is the reason.
🧠 PART 3 · THE UNLABELLED PILE

Six sentences, no labels, in no helpful order. Run the four steps on each one before you look at the options.

✅ CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING
What job is cum doing in this sentence?
Cum Cicerō cōnsul esset, coniūrātiōnem apertē accūsāvit.
Cum vesper vēnit, coniūrātī in aedibus Brūtī convēnērunt.
Cicerō cum praetōribus per Viam Sacram ambulābat.
Cum multī cīvēs Catilīnam timērent, tamen quīdam eum adhūc laudābant.
Cum cōnsul cum custōdibus ad carcerem venīret, turba tacēbat.
Cum Catilīna nihil respondēret, Cicerō sententiam suam iterum dīxit.
📚 PART 4 · WHEN TWO ANSWERS ARE BOTH RIGHT

Twice in Part 3 the feedback admitted that a second reading was defensible. That was not hedging. Step 4 asks a question about meaning, and meaning is sometimes genuinely open. Here is the honest picture:

BoundaryHow often it is decidableWhat to do
preposition vs. conjunctionalwaysStep 1 settles it every time. There is no judgment call here.
indicative vs. subjunctivealwaysStep 2 settles it every time. Read the ending.
concessive vs. the other twousuallyTamen settles it when present. Absent, the sense normally still makes it clear, because a concessive clause has to push against something.
temporal vs. causaloften notChoose the reading that makes the sentence say something worth saying, translate it, and be able to name your choice.
Cum Cicerō cōnsul esset, coniūrātiōnem superāvit.
“When Cicero was consul, he defeated the conspiracy.” — a date. It tells you which year.
“Since Cicero was consul, he defeated the conspiracy.” — an argument. It says the office is why he could.
The Latin is identical. Both translations are grammatically sound. They are not the same claim about history, and that is the interesting part: your translation takes a position the Latin left open.
⚠️ What this does not license. “Two readings are possible” is a real fact about some cum clauses; it is not a defence for guessing. It never applies to steps 1 and 2, and a translation that reads a subjunctive as an indicative, or a conjunction as a preposition, is simply wrong. Ambiguity lives in one place only — the temporal/causal boundary — and you have to have run the first three steps to earn it.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
You are translating a cum clause and both “when” and “since” work. What do you do?
📜 PART 5 · SPEED SORT · CLICK EACH CARD

Say your answer out loud, then flip. These are short on purpose — the goal is to get the four steps down to about a second each.

🃏 FLIP CARDS · NAME THE JOB BEFORE YOU FLIP
cum senātōribus
which job?
Preposition. No verb in the group. “with the senators.” Step 1, done.
cum senātōrēs tacērent
which job?
Conjunction + imperfect subjunctive. Temporal or causal — the main clause decides. Not concessive without tamen or a clear push-back.
cum senātōrēs tacēbant
which job?
Conjunction + indicative. Plain “when.” Step 2 stops you: pure time.
mēcum
which job?
Preposition, glued to the back of the pronoun. “with me.” Same with tēcum, nōbīscum, sēcum.
cum…, tamen…
which job?
Concessive. “Although.” Tamen in the main clause is your one mechanical handle in this whole module.
quamquam perīculum magnum erat
which job?
Not cum at all. Quamquam + indicative — “although,” stated as plain fact. The indicative cousin from 10.03.
quia nēmō respondēbat
which job?
Not cum either. Quia + indicative — “because,” the reason given flatly. Causal cum is the same idea in the subjunctive.
cum vesper venīret
which job?
Conjunction + imperfect subjunctive, temporal. Evening's arrival is scenery, not a reason. Compare cum vesper vēnit — indicative, same meaning, no subjunctive.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Verb? Mood? Tamen? Reason? Four questions, always in that order. The first two are free — you read them off the page. The third is nearly free. Only the fourth costs you any thought, and by then there are only two answers left.
