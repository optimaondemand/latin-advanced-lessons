📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The same construction, pointed two other directions.
Yesterday's cum clause sat beside the main clause. Today you meet the two that lean on it. Causal cum pushes the main clause forward: “since this was so, that followed.” Concessive cum pushes against it: “although this was so, that happened anyway.” Not one new ending in the whole lesson — and one new word, tamen, that does more work for you than any ending could.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognise and translate causal cum (“since, because”) and concessive cum (“although”).
2
I will be able to use tamen as a signal when I read, and supply it as a signal when I write.
3
I will be able to negate a cum clause correctly, and name the two indicative conjunctions that do the same jobs plainly.
📚 PART 1 · CAUSAL CUM: “SINCE, BECAUSE”

A causal cum clause gives the reason the main clause happened. English usually wants “since” — sometimes “because,” sometimes “seeing that.”

Cum nēmō respondēret, Cicerō iterum rogāvit.
“Since no one was answering, Cicero asked again.” The silence is not scenery. It is why there was a second question. Gloss: respondeō, respondēre, respondī — to answer; iterum (adv.) — again.
Cum Catō ita cēnsēret, senātōrēs sententiam mūtāvērunt.
“Since Cato was proposing this, the senators changed their vote.” Gloss: mūtō, mūtāre, mūtāvī, mūtātum — to change.
Cum haec vēra sint, senātus statim agere dēbet.
“Since these things are true, the Senate ought to act at once.” Note the tense: the main verb dēbet is present, so primary sequence gives the present subjunctive sint. Causal and concessive cum turn up in both sequences; temporal cum lives almost entirely in past narrative.
💡 The sense test for causal. Put the word therefore between the two clauses and read it aloud. “No one was answering — therefore Cicero asked again.” If that works, you have a causal clause. If it sounds like a non-sequitur, you probably have a temporal one.
📚 PART 2 · CONCESSIVE CUM: “ALTHOUGH” — AND THE ONE WORD THAT GIVES IT AWAY

A concessive cum clause concedes something — it admits a fact that ought to have stopped the main clause from happening. And Latin very often flags it with tamen, “nevertheless,” sitting in the main clause.

Cum perīculum magnum esset, tamen Catō surrēxit.
“Although the danger was great, Cato nevertheless stood up.”
Cum Cicerō cōnsul esset, tamen ipse Lentulum in carcerem dūxit.
“Although Cicero was consul, he nevertheless led Lentulus to the prison himself.” A consul had officers to do such things. The point of the sentence is that he did not use them.
Cum perīculum magnum sit, tamen cōnsul nōn timet.
“Although the danger is great, the consul is nevertheless not afraid.” Present main verb, so primary sequence, so sit.
✍️ Two uses for tamen, depending on which direction you are working. Reading: tamen in the main clause is your strongest single clue that the cum clause is concessive — not a certainty, but the best clue the construction offers. Writing: when you want “although,” put tamen in. Without it, your reader has no way to tell your concessive clause from a temporal one, and will probably read it as temporal.
🎵 MEMORY WORK · SAY IT OUT LOUD THREE TIMES
Cum + indicative — that is when.
Cum + subjunctive — that is when, since, or although.
And if I see tamen — it is although.

Three lines. Say them now, before the drill. On Friday's translation you will be choosing between “when” and “although” under time pressure, and a chant is faster than reasoning the rule out from scratch.

📚 PART 3 · HOW TO MAKE A CUM CLAUSE NEGATIVE

You have spent two modules choosing between nē and ut nōn. Put that choice down. A cum clause negates the ordinary way: plain nōn, inside the clause.

ConstructionNegativeExample
Purpose Module 8nēCicerō custōdēs posuit nē quis effugeret.
Result Module 9ut nōnTam callidus erat ut nōn comprehenderētur.
Cum clause this modulecum … nōnCum Cicerō nōn timēret, tamen cautus erat.
Cum Cicerō nōn timēret, tamen cautus erat.
“Although Cicero was not afraid, he was nevertheless cautious.” Gloss: cautus, -a, -um — careful, on guard.
⚠️ The error to expect from yourself. After Modules 8 and 9 your hand wants to write nē or ut nōn whenever a subordinate clause turns negative. There is no such thing as cum nē, and cum ut nōn is not Latin at all. Cum already told the reader what kind of clause this is; the negation is just nōn, doing its ordinary job.
📚 PART 4 · THE INDICATIVE COUSINS: QUIA AND QUAMQUAM

Latin can say “because” and “although” without any subjunctive at all. It has separate conjunctions for it, and they take the indicative. Knowing them is useful twice over: you will meet them in reading, and they tell you something about what the subjunctive is for.

IdeaWith the subjunctiveWith the indicative
whencum + subj. — the circumstance
Cum omnēs tacērent, cōnsul surrēxit.		cum + indic. — plain time
Cum vesper vēnit, praetōrēs vēnērunt.
becausecum + subj.
Cum nēmō respondēret, iterum rogāvit.		quia (or quod) + indic.
Quia nēmō respondēbat, iterum rogāvit.
althoughcum + subj., usually with tamen
Cum perīculum magnum esset, tamen surrēxit.		quamquam + indic.
Quamquam perīculum magnum erat, surrēxit.
💡 What the difference amounts to. Broadly: quia and quamquam report a fact and stand back from it. Cum + subjunctive folds the clause into the main clause's world — it presents the circumstance as part of the same thought rather than as a separate assertion. Do not lean hard on that distinction in a translation; lean on it when you are deciding which one to write.
✍️ One useful consequence for reading. If you see quamquam, you already know the clause is concessive and you already know the verb will be indicative — no decision required. That is why authors reach for it: it is unambiguous. Cum is the flexible one, and flexibility always costs the reader something.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Causal cum makes the main clause follow from it; concessive cum makes the main clause push against it. Tamen in the main clause is your signal for concessive — and when you write “although” in Latin yourself, put tamen in so your reader gets the signal too. Negation inside a cum clause is plain nōn: never nē, never ut nōn.
