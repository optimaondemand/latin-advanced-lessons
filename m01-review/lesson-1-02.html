Lesson 1.02
Catiline's Portrait
Reading · Participles in Context
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Sallust gives Catiline every gift a Roman could want. Then he shows you the bill.
Eight lines of adapted Sallust, and seven participles inside them. Noble birth, a body that could take anything, a mind quick enough for anything — all of it real, all of it turned the wrong way. Read for the man first. Then go back and find every participle.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to read an adapted passage of Sallust's portrait of Catiline and say what it claims about him.
2
I will be able to find every participle in that passage and give its type, case, number, and gender.
3
I will be able to explain how Sallust builds a character sketch, and why the structure is itself an argument.
📜 PART 1 · THE READING

Adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 5 and 14. A simplified Latin retelling of Sallust's portrait of Catiline — not Sallust's own sentences. Forms worth stopping on are marked in red. Read it once straight through for the man before you analyze anything.

Catilīna nōbilī genere nātus erat, sed vir malī animī et ingeniī prāvī fuit. Corpus eius patiēns labōrum et frīgoris erat; animus audāx et callidus semper nova et perīculōsa cupiēbat. Ab adulēscentiā bella et caedēs amāverat.

Catilīna, pecūniam aliēnam semper petēns, suam prōfundēbat. Itaque multī hominēs, quī pecūniam suam iam āmīserant, ad eum veniēbant. Verbīs blandīs multōs virōs dēceptōs in coniūrātiōnem suam trāxit.

Sallustius scrīpsit Catilīnam multōs adulēscentēs corrūpisse. Tandem, multīs virīs convocātīs, Catilīna dux coniūrātiōnis factus est; iam bellum gestūrus, arma parābat.

📖 VOCABULARY GLOSS · every word not on your running list
  • nōbilis, -e — noble, of distinguished birth (abl. sg. nōbilī)
  • genus, generis n. — birth, family, stock; nōbilī genere — ablative of origin, "of noble family"
  • nātus erat — "had been born." Not yet studied — gloss provided. It comes from nāscor, one of a class of verbs that look passive but mean something active. You meet the whole class next week, in Module 2. For now treat nātus erat as a single vocabulary item meaning "had been born."
  • ingenium, ingeniī n. — natural character, disposition, talent
  • prāvus, -a, -um — crooked, warped, depraved
  • patiēns (gen. patientis) — able to endure, tough against (takes the genitive of what is endured) — a 3rd-declension adjective
  • labor, labōris m. — hardship, toil
  • frīgus, frīgoris n. — cold, the cold
  • audāx (gen. audācis) — bold, daring, reckless
  • callidus, -a, -um — shrewd, cunning, crafty
  • nova et perīculōsa — "new and dangerous things" (neuter plural adjectives used as nouns)
  • adulēscentia, adulēscentiae f. — youth, young manhood
  • caedēs, caedis f. — slaughter, bloodshed (acc. pl. caedēs)
  • aliēnus, -a, -um — another's, belonging to somebody else
  • prōfundēbat — "was pouring away, was squandering" (imperfect of prōfundō, prōfundere, prōfūdī, prōfūsum)
  • āmīserant — "had lost" (pluperfect of āmittō, āmittere, āmīsī, āmissum)
  • blandus, -a, -um — flattering, coaxing, smooth
  • dēceptōs — PPP of dēcipiō, dēcipere, dēcēpī, dēceptum (to deceive) — "having been deceived"
  • coniūrātiō, coniūrātiōnis f. — conspiracy
  • trāxit — "he dragged, he drew" (perfect of trahō, trahere, trāxī, tractum)
  • adulēscēns, adulēscentis m. — young man
  • corrūpisse — perfect active infinitive of corrumpō, corrumpere, corrūpī, corruptum (to corrupt, spoil)
  • tandem (adv.) — at last, finally
  • convocātīs — PPP of convocō, convocāre, convocāvī, convocātum (to call together) — "having been summoned"
  • factus est — "was made" (perfect passive of faciō); with a predicate noun: dux factus est — "was made leader"
  • gestūrus — FAP of gerō, gerere, gessī, gestum; bellum gerere — "to wage war," so gestūrus — "about to wage"
  • arma, armōrum n. pl. — weapons, arms
✍️ One note about the adaptation. Sallust's own version of the first sentence is famously compressed — he stacks descriptive genitives and ablatives without a main verb for whole clauses at a time, and English has to unpack every one of them. The adaptation here keeps his ideas and his order of attack, but supplies the verbs he leaves out. What you are reading is easier than Sallust. What it says is his.

🔍 Three participles, three times. Dēceptōs (PPP) is earlier than the main verb. Petēns (PAP) is at the same time as it. Gestūrus (FAP) is later than it. One short passage just walked you through the entire time system of the Latin participle — and it did it without a chart.
✍️ PART 3 · TRANSLATE, THEN CHECK

Write your English out before you flip. Use the gloss freely — that is what it is for. These four are the sentences most worth having under your belt before Friday.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Corpus eius patiēns labōrum et frīgoris erat.
click to compare
"His body was able to endure hardship and cold." Patiēns is a predicate adjective agreeing with corpus (neuter), and the two genitives labōrum and frīgoris tell you what it endured. Sallust's point is that the toughness was genuine.
Catilīna, pecūniam aliēnam semper petēns, suam prōfundēbat.
click to compare
"Catiline, always going after other people's money, was pouring away his own." Petēns is nominative and agrees with Catilīna — not an ablative absolute. Suam is accusative feminine and agrees with an understood pecūniam.
Multī hominēs, quī pecūniam suam iam āmīserant, ad eum veniēbant.
click to compare
"Many men, who had already lost their own money, kept coming to him." Quī is masculine plural because hominēs is, and nominative because it is the subject of āmīserant. The pluperfect puts their ruin before their arrival — they came because they were already ruined.
Sallustius scrīpsit Catilīnam multōs adulēscentēs corrūpisse.
click to compare
"Sallust wrote that Catiline had corrupted many young men." Indirect statement: accusative subject Catilīnam, perfect infinitive corrūpisse. The perfect infinitive puts the corrupting before the writing — which is why English needs "had."
🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · HOW TO BUILD A PORTRAIT

Sallust's sketch of Catiline is one of the most imitated passages in Latin prose, and its power is structural. Look at the order he moves in: birth, then body, then mind, then appetite. Each item is a genuine advantage. Noble family. Physical endurance. Quickness of thought. A Roman reader would have recognized that list — it is the description of a man built for the consulship.

Then Sallust turns each gift over. The tough body is spent on nights of crime. The quick mind is callidus, shrewd rather than wise. The appetite is for nova et perīculōsa — new things, dangerous things — never for anything finished or kept. He never has to say "Catiline was a bad man." He arranges the good qualities so that the reader draws the conclusion, and then the reader owns it.

The Roman word for what is missing: moderātiō — measure, restraint, the sense of a proper limit. Roman moral writing treats virtūs without moderātiō as not really virtūs at all, just force pointed somewhere. That is the standard Sallust is holding Catiline to. It is worth noticing that it is also the standard he holds the Roman ruling class of his own generation to — and by that standard, Catiline is a symptom, not a cause.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

A participle carries two pieces of information at once: time relative to the main verb (PPP before, PAP alongside, FAP after) and who it belongs to, which you read off its case. Sallust puts all three in eight lines because his whole argument is about a man doing several things at the same time.
