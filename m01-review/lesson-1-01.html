📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Last week you had the tables. This week the tables are gone.
No new grammar arrives in Module 1 — not one form. What changes is where the forms live. In Module 0 they sat in charts you could look at. This week they are buried inside continuous Latin about a real Roman conspiracy, and you have to pull them out. That is a different skill, and it is the one the rest of the year runs on.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to work through a Latin sentence with three questions, asked in the same order every time.
2
I will be able to name the case, tense, or construction of a form inside a phrase, with no paradigm table in front of me.
3
I will be able to say who Catiline was and why Sallust turned him into a character study.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGES THIS WEEK
Module 0 asked
"Here is the imperfect passive table. Which row is timēbātur?"
Module 1 asks
"Here are nine lines of Sallust. What is premēbantur doing, and to whom?"

Both questions use the same grammar. Only the second one builds a reader. Looking a form up in a chart feels like learning, because it feels smooth. Digging it out of a sentence feels slower and rougher — and that is the feeling of the knowledge actually moving into a place you can reach it from later.

💡 So no tables this week? There are a few, but they come after you have worked, as a way to check yourself — never as the first thing you see. If you want the full charts again, Module 0 is still there and you should use it. Just try the sentence first.

🧠 PART 2 · THREE QUESTIONS, ASKED IN ORDER

This is the whole method. It is not clever — it is just fixed, which is what makes it fast.

 AskBecause
1Where is the main verb, and what is it? Person, number, tense, voice.The verb tells you how many people are involved, when it happened, and whether the subject is acting or being acted on. Nothing else can be settled first.
2Who is the subject? Find a nominative that agrees with the verb — or accept that the ending alone is the subject.Latin often leaves the subject inside the verb: trāxit already means "he dragged." Do not go hunting for a noun that is not there.
3For every remaining word: what case, and what job? If it is a participle, find its case before anything else.A participle's case is what tells you whether you have an ablative absolute, a phrase describing the subject, or part of a verb form. Get the case first and the rest follows.

Worked example

Here is one sentence from the passage you will read tomorrow. Nine words, no new grammar, and it will not open if you read it out of order.

Verbīs blandīs multōs virōs dēceptōs in coniūrātiōnem suam trāxit.
adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 14
StepWhat you find
1 · the verbtrāxit — perfect stem trāx- plus -it. Perfect active, 3rd singular: "he dragged, he drew."
2 · the subjectThere is no nominative noun in the sentence at all. The subject is inside the ending: he — Catiline, from the sentence before. Stop looking.
3 · the restverbīs blandīs — ablative plural, and nothing else in the sentence needs them, so this is ablative of means: "by flattering words."
multōs virōs — accusative plural, the direct object of trāxit.
dēceptōs — a participle. Case first: accusative plural masculine, so it agrees with virōs, not with the subject. PPP, so it happened before the dragging: "men who had been deceived."
in coniūrātiōnem suam — in + accusative, so motion into: "into his own conspiracy."
"By flattering words he drew many men, once they had been deceived, into his conspiracy."
Or more naturally: "He deceived many men with flattery and drew them into his conspiracy."
✍️ Notice where the danger was. Two ablatives and a participle sat side by side, and the temptation was to read verbīs blandīs…dēceptōs as one ablative absolute. It is not one, because dēceptōs is accusative. Question 3 caught it. That single check — what case is the participle? — is the most valuable habit in this module.

🏛️ PART 4 · THE MAN YOU ARE READING ABOUT

Lūcius Sergius Catilīna was born around 108 BC into one of Rome's oldest patrician families, the Sergii. By birth he should have been one of the great men of the Republic. He served in Sulla's campaigns, governed a province, and ran for the consulship — and lost. Twice. By 63 BC he was deep in debt, out of options, and surrounded by other men in the same position.

Everything we know about him comes from two men who wanted him destroyed or condemned: Cicero, who beat him, and Sallust, who wrote about him twenty years later. Keep that in view all week. It does not mean the story is false. It means you are reading a case against someone, made by skilled people, and part of your job is to notice how it is built.

What is not in dispute
A conspiracy existed. Men gathered. Cicero exposed it as consul in 63 BC, and Catiline died fighting a Roman army the next year.
What is argument
What kind of man he was, and whether Rome's crisis was his fault or its own. That is where Sallust does his work — and where you should read hardest.
🌐 YOUR ARTHUR SESSION THIS MODULE

Where: the domus of a late-Republican Roman senator — the ātrium you enter through, the tablīnum where he keeps his records, the triclīnium where he entertains the men whose votes he needs. This is the kind of room where the men in this week's reading actually met. [ARTHUR: link and entry steps]

Do this: label six things, using Latin you already own — ātrium (entrance hall), iānua (door), mēnsa (table), lectus (couch), lucerna (lamp), liber (book/scroll), aqua (water), servus (household slave).

Then write one Latin sentence as an indirect statement about what the owner of this house thought or said. For example: Senātor putāvit domum suam esse tūtam. — "The senator thought that his house was safe." Accusative subject, then an infinitive. Formative and ungraded; the graded ARTHUR project comes at Module 8. [ARTHUR: capture / share step]

★ CŌNSTANTIA · PERSEVERANCE

Cōnstantia is built from cōnstāre, "to stand together, to hold firm." Romans used it for the person who keeps standing in the same place when standing there has stopped being comfortable. It is not the same as being stubborn, and it is not the same as being fast. This week asks exactly that of you: no new grammar to be excited about, no fresh paradigm to conquer — just the same forms you already know, now demanding to be found rather than looked up. That is the least glamorous work in the course and the work that decides how the rest of it goes. Cōnstantia is what carries a reader through a sentence that does not open on the first pass.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

Three questions, in this order: what is the main verb, who is the subject, what case is everything else. And when you reach a participle, ask its case before you ask its meaning. That one habit is worth more this year than any chart.
