📚 PART 1 · MODULE 1 IN FIVE LINES
LessonThe one thing to keep
1.01Three questions, in order: main verb → subject → case of everything else. For a participle, case comes before meaning.
1.02A participle tells you time (PPP earlier, PAP alongside, FAP later) and ownership (read it off the case).
1.03Ablative absolute = ablative participle + ablative noun with no other job. Then choose when, because, or although.
1.04In indirect statement the infinitive's tense is relative: future = later, present = same time, perfect = earlier. Sē points back to the main subject.
1.05You can now do all of it out of running prose. That, not the paradigm chart, is what "knowing the grammar" means.
💡 Nothing new arrived this week. Every form in both readings was something you brought with you from M/J Latin Intermediate. What changed is that you found them yourself, in continuous Latin, with a Roman historian's argument running underneath. Keep that in mind when the subjunctive lands in Module 6 — you will meet it the same way: explained, then found.

📄 THE FIRST WRITTEN TRANSLATION OF THE COURSE

Module 0 had no translation assignment — that week was for getting your footing. This is the first one, and there will be one every module from here to the end of the semester. So it is worth setting up properly once, today, and then never thinking about the mechanics again.

A few things worth knowing before you start. It is read by a person, not scored by a machine, so a sentence that is nearly right earns nearly full marks and gets a comment telling you exactly what to fix. The vocabulary is given to you on the assignment page — every word, with principal parts. And it is short: eight sentences. It is not a test of stamina. It is a test of whether you can put an ending in the right place on purpose.

✔ SET IT UP ONCE — SIX STEPS IN MICROSOFT WORD
 Do thisHow
1Open a new document in WordStart from the Word tile in Microsoft 365, or open the assignment in Canvas and use the Microsoft OneDrive option there. Working in the browser version is completely fine.
2Make a folder for this courseIn OneDrive, create a folder called Latin Advanced and keep every translation in it. By Module 17 you will have sixteen of these, and you will be glad they are in one place.
3Name the file exactlyLastname_Firstname_LatinAdv_M01_Translation.docx — underscores, no spaces. Same pattern every module, with only the number changing.
4Head the pageYour name, the date, and Module 1 Translation on three short lines at the top. Then number your answers A1–A5 and B1–B3 to match the assignment.
5Italicize the LatinSelect your Latin and press Ctrl+I (Cmd+I on a Mac). Leave English in plain text. This is standard scholarly practice — it is how every edition you will ever read does it, and it makes your work much faster to mark.
6Save, then submitWord saves to OneDrive as you type. Then go to the Canvas assignment and submit the file. Check that the file name in Canvas is the one from step 3.
✍️ About macrons. Type them if you can — on Windows, Insert → Symbol has ā ē ī ō ū, and it is worth saving them as AutoCorrect entries once. But you are not marked down for writing without macrons in a typed translation. Nobody is going to lose a point over a keyboard. What is marked is the ending itself: pressī and pressīs are different words, and no macron will save a wrong case.
💬 One more thing, and then go. If a sentence defeats you, write down what you did work out — "I know this needs an ablative absolute and I think the participle is convocātīs, but I could not place the noun." That is worth real credit and it tells your teacher precisely where to help. A blank tells nobody anything.

Module 2 — Deponent Verbs
The first genuinely new grammar of the year, and you have already met it twice in disguise: nātus erat in Monday's portrait, and abūtēre in Cicero's opening line back in Module 0. A whole class of ordinary Latin verbs wears passive endings and means something active. Next week you take the disguise off — and Sallust's narration suddenly gets much easier to read.
💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

This week added no grammar and changed everything anyway. Knowing a form is being able to find it — in a sentence you have not seen, with no heading telling you what to look for. That is cōnstantia: the same forms, one more time, until they come when called.
