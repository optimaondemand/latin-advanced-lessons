Lesson 1.04
The Cast of the Conspiracy
Reading · Relative Clauses & Indirect Statement
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Who actually shows up to a conspiracy?
Not monsters. Bankrupt noblemen, old soldiers with nothing to go home to, and men buried in debt — and Sallust names all three groups precisely, because the list is his argument about Rome. This passage carries three indirect statements with three different infinitive tenses and a participial trap. Everything you need is already yours.

📜 PART 1 · THE READING

Adapted from Sallust, Bellum Catilīnae 17, 20 and 21. A simplified Latin retelling of the first gathering of Catiline's inner circle — not Sallust's own sentences. Participial phrases and indirect statements are marked in red. Read it once straight through before you analyze a single word.

Catilīna, omnibus rēbus parātīs, coniūrātiōnem suam aperīre statuit. Itaque virōs dēlēctōs in ūnum locum convocāvit.

Eō convēnērunt hominēs variī. Aderant nōbilēs perditī, quī omnem pecūniam cōnsūmpserant; aderant mīlitēs veterānī, quī sub Sullā pugnāverant; aderant aliī magnīs dēbitīs pressī. Omnēs ūnam spem habēbant: novās rēs cupiēbant.

Catilīna eīs prōmīsit sē omnia dēbita dēlētūrum esse. Verbīs Catilīnae audītīs, coniūrātī laetī erant, et sē esse parātōs ad bellum dīxērunt.

Sallustius autem scrīpsit hōs hominēs nōn virtūtem sed pecūniam petīvisse. Ita nōn sōlum Catilīnam sed etiam tōtam cīvitātem accūsāvit.

📖 VOCABULARY GLOSS · every word not on your running list
  • statuit — "decided, determined" (perfect of statuō, statuere, statuī, statūtum; takes an infinitive)
  • aperiō, aperīre, aperuī, apertum — to open; to reveal, disclose
  • dēlēctōs — PPP of dēligō, dēligere, dēlēgī, dēlēctum (to choose, pick out) — "chosen, hand-picked"
  • eō (adv.) — to that place, there
  • convēnērunt — "came together, gathered" (perfect of conveniō, convenīre, convēnī, conventum)
  • varius, -a, -um — various, of several different kinds
  • aderant — "were present, were there" (imperfect of adsum, adesse, adfuī)
  • perditus, -a, -um — ruined, bankrupt, desperate (PPP of perdō, used as an adjective)
  • cōnsūmpserant — "had used up, had squandered" (pluperfect of cōnsūmō, cōnsūmere, cōnsūmpsī, cōnsūmptum)
  • veterānus, -a, -um — veteran, long-serving
  • Sulla, Sullae m. — Lucius Cornelius Sulla, Roman general and dictator, died 78 BC; sub Sullā — "under Sulla"
  • dēbitum, dēbitī n. — a debt (literally "what is owed," from dēbeō)
  • pressī — PPP of premō, premere, pressī, pressum (to press, weigh down) — "weighed down"
  • spēs, speī f. — hope (acc. sg. spem)
  • novae rēs, novārum rērum f. pl. — literally "new things," but as a Roman political term: revolution, the overturning of the established order
  • eīs — dat. pl. of is, ea, id — "to them"
  • prōmīsit — "promised" (perfect of prōmittō, prōmittere, prōmīsī, prōmissum)
  • dēlētūrum esse — future active infinitive of dēleō, dēlēre, dēlēvī, dēlētum (to wipe out, cancel) — "to be going to wipe out"
  • laetus, -a, -um — glad, delighted
  • parātus, -a, -um — ready, prepared (PPP of parō, used as an adjective)
  • hōs — acc. pl. m. of hic, haec, hoc — "these"
  • petīvisse — perfect active infinitive of petō, petere, petīvī, petītum — "to have sought, to have gone after"
  • nōn sōlum…sed etiam — not only…but also
  • cīvitās, cīvitātis f. — the citizen body; the state, the community
  • accūsāvit — "he accused, he indicted" (perfect of accūsō, accūsāre, accūsāvī, accūsātum)
✍️ One note about the adaptation. Sallust reports Catiline's promises in a long stretch of indirect speech, and full classical Latin puts most of the verbs in that stretch into the subjunctive — which you meet in Modules 6 and 7. The adaptation here uses the accusative-and-infinitive construction you already own instead. The promise itself is historical: cancellation of debts (tabulae novae, "new account books") was one of the things Catiline was said to have offered, and it is why men in debt came.
📑 PART 2 · FOUR PARTICIPIAL PHRASES — ONE OF THEM IS NOT WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Find all four yourself first, and decide for each one whether it is an ablative absolute. Then check. Yesterday's test is the only tool you need: what case is the participle?

PhraseParticiple's caseVerdictEnglish
omnibus rēbus parātīsabl. pl. f.Ablative absolute"once everything had been prepared" — PPP, so earlier than statuit
virōs dēlēctōsacc. pl. m.PPP with the object"hand-picked men" — agrees with virōs, the object of convocāvit
aliī magnīs dēbitīs pressīnom. pl. m.Not an absolute"others, weighed down by great debts" — pressī agrees with aliī, the subject; magnīs dēbitīs is ablative of means
verbīs Catilīnae audītīsabl. pl. n.Ablative absolute"when Catiline's words had been heard" — Catilīnae is genitive, sitting inside the absolute
🔍 Row three is the whole point of yesterday. Magnīs dēbitīs pressī has an ablative phrase and a participle sitting right beside each other, and it is not an ablative absolute, because pressī is nominative. If you read it as an absolute you get "with great debts having been pressed," which means nothing — and, worse, you lose the men the sentence is actually about.
📚 PART 3 · THREE REPORTS, THREE TIMES

This passage does something you should notice: it uses all three infinitive tenses in three consecutive indirect statements. An infinitive's tense in Latin does not tell you when something happened in absolute time. It tells you when it happened relative to the main verb.

Indirect statementInfinitiveTime vs. main verbEnglish
sē omnia dēbita dēlētūrum esse
after prōmīsit		future activelater"that he would wipe out all debts"
sē esse parātōs ad bellum
after dīxērunt		presentsame time"that they were ready for war"
hōs hominēs…pecūniam petīvisse
after scrīpsit		perfect activeearlier"that these men had gone after money"
💡 And notice sē. Twice the accusative subject is the reflexive sē, and each time it points back to the subject of the main verb: after Catilīna…prōmīsit, sē is Catiline; after coniūrātī…dīxērunt, sē is the conspirators. Latin uses sē precisely so you can tell "he promised that he himself would" from "he promised that somebody else would." English has to lean on context; Latin marks it.

✍️ PART 4 · TRANSLATE, THEN CHECK

Write out your English before flipping. These three are the closest thing on this page to tomorrow's translation assignment.

🔍 TRANSLATE FIRST · THEN CLICK TO COMPARE
Catilīna, omnibus rēbus parātīs, coniūrātiōnem suam aperīre statuit.
click to compare
"Catiline, once everything had been prepared, decided to reveal his conspiracy." The ablative absolute is a PPP, so it is earlier than statuit — "once / after," not "while." Statuit takes the infinitive aperīre directly.
Aderant nōbilēs perditī, quī omnem pecūniam cōnsūmpserant.
click to compare
"There were ruined noblemen present, who had used up all their money." Aderant is imperfect — the scene is being described, not narrated as a single event. The pluperfect cōnsūmpserant puts the squandering before the meeting: they were already bankrupt when they walked in.
Sallustius autem scrīpsit hōs hominēs nōn virtūtem sed pecūniam petīvisse.
click to compare
"Sallust, however, wrote that these men had gone after money, not virtue." Perfect infinitive petīvisse, so earlier than scrīpsit. Notice how much work autem ("however") is doing: it turns the whole preceding scene into something Sallust is about to judge.
🏛️ CULTURAL CALLOUT · NOVAE RĒS, THE ROMAN WORD FOR REVOLUTION

"New things." Two of the most ordinary words in Latin, and together they are one of the heaviest charges a Roman could bring. Novae rēs meant the overturning of the established order: the constitution, the authority of the Senate, the way things were done. To say that a man wanted novae rēs was to put him outside the community, and everyone in the room knew it.

Notice what that lets Sallust do. He could have written that Catiline's followers were poor, or in debt, or shut out of office — all of which was true and checkable. Instead he tells you they wanted novae rēs, and their real grievances vanish behind the label. This is worth watching because you will see the same move made about people in your own century, in whatever language you read.

Cicero, prosecuting the same men in the same year, chooses differently. His emphasis is legal and constitutional: these men are hostēs, enemies of the state, and there are laws for that. Sallust's emphasis is moral: Rome had lost its virtūs and got the conspirators it deserved. Same conspiracy, two accusations, two very different reasons to be angry. You will spend the year with both.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

An infinitive's tense in indirect statement is relative: future = after the reporting verb, present = alongside it, perfect = before it. Get the infinitive's tense and the whole reported sentence lands in the right place in time — and English needs "would," "was," or "had" to say what Latin says with one ending.
